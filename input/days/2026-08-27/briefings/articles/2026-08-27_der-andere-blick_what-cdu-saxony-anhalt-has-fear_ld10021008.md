# What the CDU in Saxony-Anhalt has to fear

**** · Der andere Blick · 2026-08-27 · 1636 words · [ld.10021008](https://www.nzz.ch/der-andere-blick/ld.10021008)

by Florian Eder

Editor-in-Chief of NZZ Germany

Having power has a catch: you have to know what to do with it. The AfD has so far avoided this test. In contrast, it confronts Volkswagen CEO Oliver Blume in a completely different way. Gerald Knaus, on the other hand, has concrete ideas about what Europe could actually do to end irregular migration.

****There is a thought that hardly any Christian Democrat dares to voice publicly at the moment. However, the closer the state election in Saxony-Anhalt gets, the more plausible it becomes: A landslide victory for the AfD to an absolute majority would be the best result for the CDU.

Of course, the party is fighting to prevent such a victory. Its lead candidate Sven Schulze wants to remain state premier, and the federal party fears an AfD-led state government, the first in Germany. Naturally, it would already be a severe defeat for the Chancellor's party if it ended up far behind the AfD in a federal state it has governed since 2002.

However, things could be much worse for the CDU if it achieves one of its election goals and manages to keep the AfD out of government. If the AfD wins by a landslide, the CDU would remain in opposition. With almost any other outcome, it would be the CDU that would have to organize a majority against the election winner.

There's a certain German tendency towards apocalyptic thinking. An AfD government in Saxony-Anhalt would be a profound turning point, but not the end of the Federal Republic. The state has a population of just over two million, fewer than Berlin, which has a rich history of political frivolity. The state government in Magdeburg governs Saxony-Anhalt. One doesn't need to downplay the AfD to grasp this scale.

This is one of the reasons why Germany has a federal system. It also limits political risks. State governments are allowed to pursue different paths, voters can observe the consequences and cast their judgment in the next election. To declare even an AfD government in Magdeburg a threat to the Federal Republic is to show little confidence in German democracy and, by extension, in the voters of Saxony-Anhalt.

Polls currently show the AfD at 41 to 43 percent, the CDU at 22 to 24 percent. The Left Party is at 13 percent, the SPD at around 7 percent. The Greens and Wagenknecht's party BSW are hovering around the five percent threshold, the FDP below it.

Depending on which of the smaller parties makes it into parliament, a state parliament could emerge in which there is only a majority against the AfD if almost all others cooperate. The CDU would thus be dependent on parties against which it is campaigning itself.

Because the West German party system first reaches its mathematical limits in the East, eastern Germany is, in a sense, becoming the republic's avant-garde. The old rules of government formation simply do not work with these power dynamics.

When mainstream parties shrink and the political extremes grow larger, ultimately three possibilities remain: ever broader coalitions of the center, minority governments with shifting majorities – or the participation of the one party that has so far been excluded from government formation.

The first possibility is visibly reaching its limits. The second is being tested next door. In Saxony, the CDU and SPD have governed without their own majority since 2024. A consultation mechanism involves all parliamentary groups early in legislative projects; they can propose changes, and the government must review them and justify rejections. This offer also applies to the AfD. The AfD, precisely, has so far stayed out of the process.

This is more than a Saxon peculiarity. While federal politics steadfastly adheres to the firewall, a CDU-led state government is already organizing parliamentary cooperation beyond fixed blocs.

Where traditional majorities disappear, minority governments and shifting majorities could become more normal. This would by no means be democratically disreputable. On the contrary: Parliaments are there to find majorities for specific decisions. The German coalition logic is not the only permissible form of parliamentarism.

A minority government could spare the CDU from being merely seen and used as an anchor for an anti-AfD alliance. However, it only works if at least the SPD and Greens no longer rigidly adhere to the firewall: A government without its own majority must be able to accept that its projects are supported by shifting majorities – and that it cannot dictate who agrees to them. Otherwise, the inflexibility of a coalition merely shifts to parliament.

In any case, a minority government is not a magic trick by which the CDU could simultaneously maintain its demarcation and regain its political freedom of movement through its own efforts.

Saxony-Anhalt could now force the third option: governmental responsibility for the AfD.

The fact that the CDU's election campaign hardly reflects the self-confidence of a governing party makes the situation in Saxony-Anhalt even more paradoxical. Reiner Haseloff only vacated the State Chancellery early in January so that his successor could capitalize on the incumbency advantage of the Minister-President before the election. Ultimately, this was late enough that the change of power itself appeared to be an election campaign tactic, but early enough that Schulze now has to be judged by the office he holds.

Distancing oneself from Berlin is the privilege of every state politician. To that end, Schulze toured the state with Dieter Hallervorden. The comedian, whom older West Germans still remember from a time when his political views didn't contribute to his entertainment value, sang against «armament» at CDU events and lashed out at the party leadership. Four events were sold out. It didn't help the polls.

The CDU is in a trap it built for itself over years. Since 2018, the CDU federal party congress resolution to reject coalitions and «similar forms of cooperation» with both the AfD and the Left Party has been in effect. Since then, the demarcation against the AfD has become sharper. The simultaneous demarcation from the Left Party is now becoming a serious problem.

Because eventually, a government emerges whose primary commonality is keeping another party out of power. This is democratically legitimate in a system that has always recognized the freedom to form coalitions or not. It's not an everyday occurrence, however: in Germany, parties often argue after an election about who is ahead by a few tenths of a percentage point and thus entitled to form a government.

In Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD could win by a huge margin. And yet, even before the polling stations close, it would be clear that the strongest party would be prevented from joining the government by the others.

Politically, this is devastating: It confirms the image of a party cartel that the AfD has been painting for years.

In doing so, the AfD is changing the party system without ever having governed itself. This demarcation forces together parties that actually want to be alternatives to each other. The CDU wants to be a bourgeois counterforce to the left and at the same time needs its votes to prevent a right-wing government. This firewall welds together those who stand on one side – the good side!

The CDU, in particular, pays a high price for this. An all-party government against the AfD could tear the party apart. It would have to make concessions to forces against which it is campaigning itself, and at the same time keep in line that part of its electorate and membership asking why what is possible to the left should remain excluded to the right, voters and members say. It would save a government and risk losing itself.

Eventually, even elections lose some of their impact when their outcome can change the balance of power but no longer the direction of the government.

For the AfD, such an outcome would be almost ideal. In opposition, they could watch their opponents squabble amongst themselves and explain to their supporters that this proves once again that the other side is a cartel. The defeat in forming a government would fuel their next electoral success.

Therefore, the other option holds an appeal for the CDU that hardly anyone in the party wants to admit. What if the AfD won so decisively that all these morally sound constructs no longer functioned? Then political responsibility would shift. The CDU would no longer have to explain with whom it was preventing the AfD from gaining power. It could simply be the opposition.

That would be political liberation in bitter defeat. The party might even try to win back voters instead of orchestrating defensive battles against the AfD.

Nevertheless, the CDU will fight until the last day to make Sven Schulze state premier. Anything else would be an admission of failure. Should it not succeed, a clear defeat would be easier for them to bear than a partial victory.

The AfD would then have to present its personnel, organize majorities, pass a budget, and conduct itself in the Bundesrat (Federal Council). It would finally have to resolve its internal power struggles and make the results visible in government action, not just in petty squabbles . It would have to confront accusations of cronyism with renewed vigor. The protest party would become a governing party, with all the constraints, contradictions, and disappointments that entails.

No one should succumb to the convenient hope that the AfD will simply disenchant itself in government. Perhaps that will happen. Perhaps it will prove the opposite and become even stronger in office. Voters must judge that.

The crucial point, however, is this: So far, the AfD can claim the advantages of a governing party without bearing its costs. It promises change without having to account for its consequences. Now, for the first time, it would have to prove that it can not only win elections but also govern.

This is the test from which its opponents have shielded it for years.
