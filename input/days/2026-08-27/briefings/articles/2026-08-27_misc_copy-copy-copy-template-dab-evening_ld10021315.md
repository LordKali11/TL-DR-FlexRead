by Beatrice Achterberg

Editor at NZZ Germany

Today's topic is the forgetting of the GDR dictatorship and the pivotal election in Saxony-Anhalt. But first, the commentary.

Life on the Oblomovka estate was good. Drinking tea, eating hazel grouse, and sleeping as much as one wanted. That's how Ilya Ilyich Oblomov spent his childhood as a nobleman in Tsarist Russia. He disliked change. He left letters unopened. After all, he lived comfortably enough on the income from his inherited estate. It's hard to imagine Friedrich Merz ignoring his mail.

But the inertia plaguing his party under his leadership is no less pronounced than that of the Russian protagonist. Under Merz, the CDU is wearily sliding towards its downfall. The Union of Helmut Kohl, Konrad Adenauer, and Ludwig Erhard is heading towards the 20 percent mark at the federal level, while its right-wing rival, the AfD, is threatening to break the 30 percent target.

The situation in the East is particularly dire. Saxony-Anhalt will elect a new state parliament in September. The CDU still holds the Minister President position there. But the AfD is far ahead of it. If nothing changes, the CDU will only have bleak prospects after the election: either the AfD makes the leap into governmental responsibility – or incumbent Sven Schulze would have to struggle with a minority government that relies on support from The Left, which emerged from the SED successor party, the PDS.

The painful truth is: there are no good options left for the CDU. This is not solely due to multiple crises, nor solely to the fragmented party system, but also to the party leadership itself. For years, it has committed to a strategy towards the AfD without developing a plan B should the goal of minimizing the AfD through exclusion fail. But that is precisely what has happened.

When former CDU family minister Kristina Schröder now calls for rapprochement by «formulating conditions» in order to «even begin a conversation,» and wants to appeal to the «moderates» in the AfD, then one must conclude: It is too late for that.

Because no one in the AfD is waiting for offers from the Christian Democrats anymore. The moderates don't hold sway anyway. The motto instead is: us or them. The goal is to become so strong that the CDU no longer matters. The Thuringian state leader, Björn Höcke, calls this «organic growth.» In eastern Germany, this strategy is already bearing fruit, as is evident in Saxony-Anhalt.

Another impediment is added: After years of maximum demarcation, the CDU would have to explain why cooperation should suddenly be acceptable. It would be a tightrope walk at a thousand kilometers high.

Merkel's asymmetric demobilization worked as long as the conflicts could be resolved on their own. In 2013, the CDU/CSU alliance garnered 41.5 percent of the vote, while the AfD, with 4.7 percent, remained outside the Bundestag. But four years later, the model had collapsed: The AfD entered parliament with 12.6 percent, and the CDU/CSU alliance fell to 32.9 percent. The strategy of keeping the AfD in check had clearly failed. At that point, at the very latest, the CDU should have recognized that its hitherto successful tactic of playing dead had become a method that strengthened the opposition.

But she did the opposite. With the incompatibility resolution, the CDU ruled out any cooperation with the AfD (and the Left Party). And from then on, she punished even mere statements in which someone treated the AfD as a normal political competitor.

Three episodes show how often the CDU had the opportunity to change course – and how consistently it let these opportunities slip away.

Thuringia was the first. After FDP politician Thomas Kemmerich was elected Minister President, also with votes from the AfD, the then Federal Government Commissioner for Eastern Germany, Christian Hirte, congratulated the «candidate of the center.» Shortly thereafter, he had to vacate his office. That went too far for Merkel. More than that: She called on the state to reverse the democratic election.

Merz continued in their vein. In 2023, it was someone who was targeted who hadn't even taken action, but had initially only considered a different strategy: The conservative historian Andreas Rödder advocated defining «red lines» for the AfD, instead of categorically excluding any parliamentary majority with AfD votes. The reactions to this fundamentally sensible proposal were fierce. Rödder ultimately resigned as chairman of the CDU's Fundamental Values ​​Commission. Another opportunity had been missed.

The noose tightened around the necks of the dissenters. Jens Spahn felt this firsthand. When the CDU member of parliament declared in an interview in May 2026 that there would be no cooperation «with this AfD,» the demonstrative pronoun alone was enough to cause outrage. If it couldn't work with _this one_ , then with another? Spahn subsequently refrained from using that phrase. Thus, the CDU increasingly censored itself—and learned the same lesson each time: Alternatives to the current predicament must not even be openly considered.

In the super election year of 2026, the CDU now faces a dilemma, both outcomes of which it must fear: If it moves towards the AfD, internal power struggles and public condemnation are equally certain. If it adheres to complete distancing, it will likely be permanently forced in parts of Germany to form ever-broader alliances to its left or to accept left-wing toleration in minority governments.

According to current polls, even a grand coalition government would no longer achieve a majority. At the federal level, the CDU/CSU thus faces the prospect of having to compromise not just with the SPD or the Greens in the next federal election – but with both. The center-right's power to shape policy would then be even diminished. The compromises already made with the SPD are fueling the Chancellor's party's slump in the polls. A future alliance with the climate-obsessed Greens would likely demand even more from the CDU/CSU and its voters than the coalition with the SPD leaders already does.

The left-wing alliances, in turn, make it difficult to present oneself as a credible conservative alternative. Here lies the second disadvantage of the firewall: It not only binds the CDU to left-wing partners, it also forces it to compromise on its former core issues: migration, internal security, and a sensible economic policy. Voters who want a change in policy cannot be offered a credible proposition in this way.

The former Union power machine has come to terms with this: It has chosen a path that is melting away its power.

The CDU's incompatibility resolution, which prohibits cooperation with the Left Party and the AfD, must also be judged by its political outcome. And the outcome is devastating. It doesn't tear the party apart overnight. Instead, it faces a slow decline.

The CDU itself proved that such self-imposed restrictions are not set in stone. When Merz failed in the first round of the chancellorship election in May 2025, the Union and the SPD, along with the Greens and The Left, agreed on a motion for a resolution so that a new election could be held on the same day.

The very next day, the then Parliamentary Secretary, Thorsten Frei, put the incompatibility resolution regarding the Left Party up for discussion. So, when its own power is at stake, the CDU is certainly capable of renegotiating its demarcation. The fact that it only does so when the Left Party is involved makes its total rejection of the AfD even less credible.

All of this does not mean that those defending the ban on contact with the AfD do not have serious arguments. Cooperation would further legitimize the AfD. It would drive away moderate CDU voters, tear apart West German state associations, and break up existing coalitions with the SPD and the Greens.

The defenders are right: Cooperation with the AfD can shatter the Union. What they conceal: Refusal does too. Just slower and without a big bang.

That things can be done differently was demonstrated by none other than Merz himself: In January of last year, he put a resolution on migration policy to a vote, which also received a majority with votes from the AfD. For a brief moment, the CDU/CSU had demonstrated that it did not want to make parliamentary majorities permanently dependent on how the AfD voted. Then Oblomovism prevailed: The impulse was there, but habit was stronger.

Because Merz didn't make this a lasting strategy. Instead of seizing the momentum and promoting as a new guiding principle the idea of ​​submitting proposals based on one's own convictions, without consulting the AfD, the CDU/CSU immediately found itself on the defensive again in the face of the left's outrage. In doing so, it may have squandered its last opportunity to define the rules of such a liberalization itself, instead of having them dictated to it at some point.

Every time an opportunity arose, whether in Thuringia, Rödder, or with the 2025 resolution, it was the CDU itself that slammed it shut with full force, overcome by fear of the possibilities. With each rejection, the AfD grew stronger.

The Russian novel «Oblomov» ends with the protagonist's early death. He succumbs to his lethargy. It led to his downfall. The Union could still awaken from its twilight sleep. If Merz cannot do that, someone else will have to.
