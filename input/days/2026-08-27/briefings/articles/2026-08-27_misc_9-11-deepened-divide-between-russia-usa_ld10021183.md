# 9/11 deepened the divide between Russia and the USA

Another miscalculation in the relationship between the US and Russia is discussed further below in this newsletter. Markus Ackeret examines a historical moment that could have brought the US and Russia closer together. However, the two superpowers fundamentally misunderstood each other. And so, as our Russia expert writes in his analysis, this brief moment only widened the gap between Russia and the US.

Ackeret looks back at September 11, 2001. The terrorist attacks shook America, and Russian President Vladimir Putin immediately sent a telegram to Washington. He was the first head of state to try to reach George W. Bush by phone. The next day, Putin offered the American president his support in the fight against international terrorism.

The proposal was surprising. What would the world look like today if Russia and America had found a common language back then? Counterfactual history doesn't change the course of events, but it can sharpen our vision for the future. For those moments when we have to make decisions for the future amidst the thicket of the present.

The telegram from Moscow arrived at noon on September 11, 2001, US time, and seemed like a ray of light on that dark day. Russian President Vladimir Putin was the first foreign head of state to attempt to reach then-US President George W. Bush by telephone. When he finally got through the following day, he expressed his solidarity with Bush in the fight against international terrorism.

The world was surprised. Would the largest attack on the U.S. since Pearl Harbor achieve what had not been possible since 1989, namely, bringing Russia and America together? Was this to be the beginning of a new world order, focused on a common goal: combating international terrorism?

The main actors themselves recalled the joint Soviet-American fight against Hitler in World War II. This time, al-Qaeda, the Taliban, and the scattered Islamist offshoots were the common enemy. Putin already saw himself fighting this enemy in the North Caucasus. The second Chechen War, starting in 1999, drew much criticism in the West. Now, the then-young Kremlin leader hoped for more understanding.

That Europeans and NATO initially and unequivocally sided with America was only natural. Putin's move, however, came unexpectedly.

Hopes in Russia and the West for a completely new relationship after the fall of the Iron Curtain had not been fulfilled. Much of what some on both sides had dreamed of remained illusory. The West saw itself as the victor in the Cold War. The Central and Eastern European states freed from Soviet embrace and the former Soviet republics that had gained independence wanted to go their own way and looked westward, not eastward.

Russia wanted to remain a great power and be able to exert influence on the reordered continent. The clearer it became that this would not succeed, the more Russia's politicians and society mourned their past greatness.

At the same time, the U.S., under its newly empowered neoconservative leadership, tried to challenge established norms. Among other things, they questioned the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty, thereby unsettling the Kremlin.

The first meeting between Bush and Putin in the summer of 2001 in Slovenia had been positive. But it had not looked like an embrace between the two powers. After the September 11 attacks, Putin suddenly made the unthinkable possible: among other things, he offered overflight rights for military supply flights and the exchange of intelligence, and gave his consent for the U.S. to use military bases in Central Asia for the fight against the Taliban in Afghanistan.

This was a geopolitically remarkable concession: Central Asia, described by the British geographer Halford Mackinder at the beginning of the 20th century as the «heartland» and «pivot point» for the control of Eurasia, is claimed by Russia as part of its sphere of influence.

Putin had good reasons for his apparent change of heart. He seized the opportunity and likely had two main hopes in mind: He saw it as a chance to portray the Chechen war as part of the fight against international terrorism, thereby seeking recognition and acceptance of his actions in the West. At the same time, he speculated that this joint struggle would finally put him on equal footing with the US and allow him to be treated accordingly – from one great power to another.

His generous offer, however, met with resistance in the Russian general staff, in politics, and in society. The September 11 attacks had also shocked Russians. But there could still be no talk of sympathy for the Americans. Mistrust remained high. In the eyes of many, Putin had made concessions without firm quid pro quos. Their suspicions were reportedly confirmed.

Essentially, Putin's hopes and the brief euphoria of world political observers were based on a mutual misunderstanding. America readily accepted any support, but also considered it a given in light of the gigantic scale of the catastrophe. Assessing Russian sensitivities and expectations seemed inappropriate. The US viewed it as equally beneficial for Russia and itself to eliminate the terrorist strongholds in Afghanistan and thus rid Russia's neighborhood of the Islamist threat.

At the same time, Bush and Putin did not understand the same thing by terrorism and the fight against it. Both perceived al-Qaeda and the Taliban as their adversaries. But the Palestinian Hamas, the Lebanese Hezbollah, and the Middle Eastern rivals Iraq and Iran were not terrorists or troublemakers for Russia. By broadly classifying Chechen resistance as international terrorism, Putin also diverted attention from his own failures in the North Caucasus.

Under these circumstances, expectations were not met. Russia felt a lack of appreciation for what it considered to be extensive concessions. America's view of the successor state to the Soviet Union did not change significantly. The following months and years demonstrated this, and it confirmed the suspicions of Russian skeptics. As early as December 2001, Washington withdrew from the ABM Treaty. NATO's eastward expansion continued as planned. As America steered toward the Iraq War, the lines of division seemed to be drawn as firmly as they had been before September 11, 2001.

The fact that the West and Russia pursued different interests became increasingly clear. Russia accused America in particular of wanting to overthrow leaders it favored – in Serbia, Georgia, and Ukraine. Conversely, it was an illusion to expect Putin to place greater emphasis on the rule of law, as well as freedom and human rights in Russia. In retrospect, the disappointment over these unfulfilled expectations even seems to have acted as a catalyst for deepening the divide.

Vladimir Putin's infamous speech at the Munich Security Conference in February 2007 referred, not least, to developments that had triggered the misunderstandings and fallacies of 2001. 9/11 had repercussions even further into the future: the 2014 rift over the Maidan in Kyiv, the annexation of Crimea, the incitement in the Donbas, and the 2022 break with Putin's major offensive against Ukraine were all already foreshadowed in 2001. Putin envisioned European security differently than the US, and the US's unwavering commitment to NATO expansion and the development of missile defense reinforced his rearmament projects.

Putin's alliance with Bush in September 2001 was a spark that quickly fizzled out. The mutual dreams of shared goals and overcoming differences soon proved to be an illusion. The perspectives and interests in the West and the East were simply too divergent. Western reflections on the relationship between America, Russia, and Europe tend to view Russia's policies since then as a reaction to Western decisions: as if Presidents Yeltsin and Putin had merely responded to the West and never pursued their own political course. This is an oversimplification.

Mistakes were made on both sides. A confrontation always requires at least two parties. But the belief that their dreams were aligned masked the more complex reality. After everything that has happened since, this is all the more true for the new attempts, this time initiated by US President Donald Trump, to find common ground and cooperate with Russia.

The path to the Ukraine war and the Ukraine war itself highlight the very different ideas of European security. For Europe, peaceful coexistence with Russia must be the goal. However, there will be no common Russian-European security policy with a Russia that is prepared to assert its great power claims, if necessary, by force. As 25 years ago, Trump and Putin threaten to fail because they cannot meet each other's expectations.
