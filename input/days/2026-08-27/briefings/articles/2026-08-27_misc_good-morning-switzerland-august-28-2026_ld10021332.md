# Good morning Switzerland, August 28, 2026

Is studying in Switzerland still the best path, as is always promoted in primary school?

One may have doubts about this when looking at the latest figures from the Federal Statistical Office.

Between 2000 and 2020, the number of university students doubled. And the university entrance qualification rates have remained high ever since – especially in urban cantons. In Geneva, for example, only 15 percent of young people started vocational training this year. Yet the figures clearly show that those who study no longer face – as was undoubtedly the case in the past – a life of ease. Job security, good pay, high social standing.

Much is changing in the labor market. The unemployment rate for academics has risen to over 2% – and has already aligned with that of non-academics. And for those who have only recently completed their studies, the outlook is even less rosy. New figures from the Federal Statistical Office show this.

There are significant differences within various fields of study. Those who study music, theater, or other arts at a university of applied sciences—or who have pursued further education in design or applied linguistics—face particular difficulties. One in ten of these graduates subsequently fails to find employment. At universities, it is primarily graduates from the «Interdisciplinary and Other» department group who are unable to find work (8.6 percent). This includes niche subjects such as Urban Studies. In the humanities and social sciences, the unemployment rate generally rose from 5.1 to 8.5 percent.

In comparison, traditional subjects continue to have the lowest unemployment rates: Those who complete a Master's degree in Medicine or Pharmacy at a university are almost guaranteed a job (1.6 percent). Bachelor's graduates from universities of applied sciences in the fields of health and social work are also in high demand. Only 1.7 and 1.5 percent, respectively, are unemployed one year after graduation.

Furthermore, almost half of university graduates are only employed on a temporary basis one year after graduation. Naturally, this includes medical graduates who work in various hospitals to obtain their specialist qualifications. However, this alone does not explain the high number. Nearly half of humanities and social science graduates also have temporary contracts. A third of university graduates even work as interns. By comparison, this is practically the case for graduates of universities of applied sciences. However, more of them—compared to university graduates—have risen to management positions.

Unemployment – ​​or temporary jobs – is not the only reason why university graduates are no longer the «masters of the universe» and their living conditions are becoming increasingly precarious. Part-time work also plays a significant role: in the humanities and social sciences, it accounts for almost 60 percent, and in interdisciplinary programs, it is over half. These two groups also report being «underemployed» most frequently one year after graduation. By comparison, less than a quarter of graduates from other faculties work part-time.

Overall, almost half of university graduates report having no opportunity or offer of full-time employment. At universities of applied sciences, this figure is around one-third. However, in these fields, it is a personal decision and only applies to certain areas. Over 70 percent of those who studied music, theater, or social work do not work full-time. In contrast, in construction and planning, IT, or business, the figure is significantly less than 20 percent. Across all higher education institutions, women are significantly overrepresented among part-time employees.

This also affects wages: The median wage of university of applied sciences graduates with a bachelor's degree is higher than that of university graduates with a master's degree. And only at universities are there graduates who earn less than 50,000 Swiss francs. Besides lawyers in traineeships, this also applies to humanities and social science graduates.

The reason for the shortage of university graduates is clear. Companies are currently hiring fewer entry-level candidates. On the one hand, this is because they are reacting to economic and geopolitical crises. On the other hand, they want to wait and see how AI will affect the job market. In short: which jobs will even be needed in the near future. The economist Mathias Binswanger has long said that it will primarily affect university graduates who hold so-called «bullshit jobs,» which are purely administrative, self-serving tasks.

In addition, the federal government and cantons must save money. The public sector, a paradise for many university graduates, offers fewer entry-level jobs than it used to. This is now becoming noticeable.
