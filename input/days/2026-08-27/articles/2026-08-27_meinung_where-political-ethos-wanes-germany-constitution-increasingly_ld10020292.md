# Where political ethos wanes: In Germany, the constitution increasingly misused to maintain power

*Actions in the name of «common good» or «our democracy» prove self-interested reinterpretation of the constitution*

More than two thousand years ago, Aristotle formulated an insight that appears more relevant today than ever before. A commonwealth does not live by Nomos, the written law, alone. It also lives by Ethos, the moral character of all citizens who must adhere to law, and especially those who apply this law and exercise political power.

Nomos specifies rules for the external life of the state and its citizens. Ethos determines the inner attitude. Only both together establish a stable society with a functioning constitutional order. If ethos is lost, Nomos eventually loses its ordering power.

In his «Politics,» Aristotle describes the decay of state orders not as a consequence of bad laws. Good constitutions can decay and degenerate, even though their provisions remain unchanged. It becomes dangerous when citizens and those who hold political power lose their moral compass. Rule then no longer serves the common good, but the preservation of one’s own power, the interests of one’s own group, or personal advantages. This is precisely what Aristotle views as the degeneration of every state order.

## Disturbing understanding of the state

Anyone observing political developments in Germany must ask whether we have not reached precisely this point. The Basic Law remains unchanged. Our courts continue to exist. Parliaments convene, governments are formed, elections take place. Outwardly, Nomos is intact. But the impression increasingly arises that the Ethos that should uphold Nomos is waning. The political elite no longer seems to understand law as a standard for its own actions, but as an instrument to be interpreted, stretched, or relativized as needed.

A particularly revealing example was the government reshuffle under Federal Chancellor Friedrich Merz. Federal Ministers were appointed to their offices before taking the oath of office prescribed by the Basic Law. One can debate the constitutional details. However, the underlying attitude is remarkable. The oath is far more than a solemn ritual. It expresses the voluntary subordination of political power to the constitution. Anyone who treats the immediate administration of the oath as a mere formality reveals an understanding of state power in which the office appears more important than a conscious commitment to law.

Equally significant was the handling of the newly elected German Bundestag after the last federal election. The convening of the new parliament was delayed to allow the already outgoing Bundestag to still pass amendments to the Basic Law. Constitutional arguments can also be made for this. Politically, however, this approach revealed a disturbing understanding of the state. The spirit of the constitution did not determine the actions, but rather the search for the last legal leeway to enforce one's own political goals.

The loss of ethos becomes even clearer in the example of former Federal Minister of Health Jens Spahn. Politically, he advocated for and explicitly defended the ban on surrogacy in Germany. At the same time, he personally chose to pursue surrogacy abroad with his husband. Legally, this may have been permissible. However, the real question is not a legal one, but a moral one. Anyone who publicly declares a certain legal order to be correct, but consciously circumvents it in their personal life, conveys the impression that Nomos should apply to the general public, but not to them. This moral transgression precisely indicates a personal lack of ethos.

## Dealing with the AfD

In a liberal democracy, opposition parties possess their rights under the constitution. A democracy can only function if a change of power is possible through free and secret elections. This can only happen if the opposition, while opposed in content by the majority, is respected in all its formal rights. If this right is violated, one invalidates the principle of a change of power through elections.

For years, it has been observed that this is increasingly happening to the AfD. Parliamentary rights are restricted, political cooperation is excluded, regardless of the specific issue, and even parliamentary customs that have been taken for granted for decades are overridden.

Particularly concerning, meanwhile, is the development at the municipal level. Election committees exclude candidates for district administrator or mayoral offices on the grounds that, according to them, findings or assessments from the interior ministries or the domestic intelligence agency are available. Whether these decisions will withstand judicial review in individual cases is one thing. The other is the related development: assessments by state authorities effectively gain influence over democratic competition.

The totalitarian party SED and its communist successor parties were never restricted in their rights to the extent the AfD is. This is precisely where the real connection between Nomos and Ethos lies. Where ethos wanes, Nomos becomes a tool. Laws are no longer understood as an expression of a common political order, but are judged by whether they serve one's own interests. Different standards apply to political opponents than to one's own side.

## Moralization

What would once have been considered an exception is becoming the new normal. The loss of that ethos, which originally underpinned the Basic Law, is concealed in a particular way: by its constant invocation and a moralized reinterpretation. Actions are increasingly taken in the name of the «common good,» «our democracy,» or similar formulas. What appears to be defense of the constitution, on closer inspection, proves to be its instrumental reinterpretation. The rhetoric of constitutional protection replaces adherence to the constitution itself.

Following Aristotle, it can be stated that we are experiencing a constitutional crisis more than a political crisis. Even if forms of government and their constitutions suddenly disappear one day, this is only the end of a long process in which they are hollowed out from within. Not by their texts disappearing, but by those who are supposed to act within their framework and apply them losing their inner commitment to the meaning of law.

Germany's real crisis is therefore not a crisis of the Basic Law. It is a crisis of political ethos. As long as the political elite no longer understands Nomos as an expression of moral obligation, but as a means of maintaining power or as a set of rules that applies more strictly to political opponents than to them, law loses its moral authority. The loss of Nomos is not at the beginning of the decline of a political order. The loss of Ethos stands at the beginning. If ethos decays, Nomos will also lose its binding force.
