# For the CDU, it is too late

*For a long time, the People's Party had the opportunity to integrate the AfD – but it let chance after chance slip away. Now it only has bad options left.*

Life is good on the Oblomovka estate. Drinking tea, eating hazel grouse, and sleeping as much as one wants. This is how Ilya Ilyich Oblomov, Ivan Goncharov's novel character, spends his childhood as a noble scion in Tsarist Russia. Changes bother him. He leaves letters unopened. After all, he lives well enough from the proceeds of his inherited country estate.

It is difficult to imagine Friedrich Merz ignoring his mail. But the inertia from which his party suffers under him is in no way inferior to that of the Russian title hero. Tired, the CDU under Merz is sliding towards its downfall. The Union of Helmut Kohl, Konrad Adenauer, and Ludwig Erhard is heading towards the 20% mark at the federal level, while its right-wing competitor, the AfD, threatens to break the 30% target.

## Dire prospects in the East

The situation in the East is particularly dire. Saxony-Anhalt will elect a new state parliament in September. The CDU still holds the Minister President position there. But the AfD is far ahead of it. If nothing changes, the CDU will only have bleak prospects after the election: either the AfD makes the leap into governmental responsibility – or incumbent Sven Schulze would have to struggle with a minority government that relies on support from The Left, which emerged from the SED successor party, the PDS.

The painful truth is: There are no good options left for the CDU. This is not solely due to multiple crises, nor just the fragmented party system, but also the party leadership itself. For years, it committed to a strategy against the AfD without developing a Plan B, should the goal of minimizing them through exclusion be missed. But that is precisely what happened.

When former CDU Family Minister Kristina Schröder now demands rapprochement by «formulating conditions» to «even get into conversation» and wants to approach the «moderates» in the AfD, it must be stated: it is too late for that.

Because in the AfD, no one has been waiting for offers from the Christian Democrats for a long time. The moderates do not call the shots anyway. The motto is instead: us or them. To become so strong that the CDU no longer matters. Björn Höcke, the Thuringian state leader, calls this «organic growth.» In East Germany, this is already bearing fruit, as shown in Saxony-Anhalt.

Another impediment is added: After years of maximum demarcation, the CDU would have to explain why cooperation should suddenly be acceptable. It would be a tightrope walk at a thousand kilometers high.

## Even thought experiments punished

Merkel's asymmetrical demobilization worked as long as conflicts could be pacified internally. In 2013, the Union garnered 41.5%, while the AfD, with 4.7%, remained outside the Bundestag. But four years later, the model was shattered: The AfD entered parliament with 12.6%, and the Union fell to 32.9%. The strategy of keeping them small had failed in plain sight. At the latest then, the CDU should have recognized that the hitherto successful tactic of playing dead had become a method that strengthened the opponent.

But it did the opposite. With the incompatibility resolution, the CDU excluded any cooperation with the AfD (and The Left). And from then on, it punished even mere statements in which someone treated the AfD as a normal political competitor.

Three episodes show how often the CDU had the opportunity to change course – and how consistently it let these opportunities slip away.

Thuringia was the first. After FDP politician Thomas Kemmerich was elected Minister President, also with votes from the AfD, the then Federal Government Commissioner for Eastern Germany, Christian Hirte, congratulated the «candidate of the center.» Shortly thereafter, he had to vacate his office. That went too far for Merkel. More than that: She called on the state to reverse the democratic election.

Merz continued in her spirit. In 2023, it affected someone who had not even acted, but had initially only thought about a different strategy: The conservative historian Andreas Rödder advocated defining «red lines» for the AfD instead of fundamentally ruling out any parliamentary majority that included AfD votes. The reactions to this fundamentally sensible proposal were fierce. Rödder eventually resigned as chairman of the CDU's Basic Values Commission. Another opportunity was missed.

The noose around the necks of dissidents tightened. Jens Spahn also felt this. When the CDU Bundestag member explained in May 2026 in an interview that there would be no cooperation «with this AfD,» even the demonstrative pronoun was enough to cause dismay. If it did not work with _this_ one, then with another? Spahn subsequently refrained from using the phrasing. Thus, the CDU increasingly censored itself – and each time learned the same lesson: Alternatives to the misery must not even be considered aloud.

## Dilemma in the super election year

In the super election year 2026, the CDU now faces a dilemma whose two outcomes it must fear: If it moves towards the AfD, internal factional struggles and public execution are equally certain. If it adheres to complete demarcation, it will probably be permanently forced in parts of Germany to form ever broader alliances to its left or to be tolerated in minority governments by the left.

According to current polls, even the black-red federal government would no longer achieve a majority. At the federal level, the CDU/CSU thus faces the prospect of having to arrange itself not with the SPD or the Greens in the next federal election – but with both. The creative power of the conservatives would then be even smaller. Already, compromises with the SPD are fueling the chancellor's party's poll slump. A future alliance with the climate-obsessed Greens is likely to demand even more from the Union and its voters than the coalition with the SPD leaders already does.

The left-wing alliances, in turn, make it difficult to present oneself as a credible conservative alternative. Here lies the second disadvantage of the firewall: It not only binds the CDU to left-wing partners, it also forces it to compromise on its former core issues: migration, internal security, and a sensible economic policy. Voters who want a change in policy cannot be offered a credible proposition in this way.

The former Union power machine has come to terms with this: It has chosen a path that is melting away its power.

## Flexible to the left, rigid to the right

The CDU's incompatibility resolution, which prohibits cooperation with The Left and the AfD, must also be measured by its political outcome. And the result is devastating. It does not tear the party apart in one fell swoop. Instead, a slow decline awaits it.

The CDU itself proved that such self-imposed restrictions are not set in stone. When Merz failed in the first round of the chancellorship election in May 2025, the Union and the SPD, along with the Greens and The Left, agreed on a motion for a resolution so that a new election could be held on the same day.

The next day, the then Parliamentary Secretary Thorsten Frei called the incompatibility resolution with regard to The Left into question. So, if its own power is at stake, the CDU is quite capable of renegotiating its demarcation. The fact that it only does so when it concerns The Left makes the total no to the AfD even more incredible.

This does not mean that the defenders of the AfD contact ban do not have serious arguments: cooperation would further legitimize the AfD, alienate moderate CDU voters, tear apart state associations in West Germany, and break up existing coalitions with the SPD and the Greens.

The defenders are right: Cooperation with the AfD can shatter the Union. What they conceal: Refusal does too. Just slower and without a big bang.

## Merz lets the momentum slip away

Merz himself demonstrated that there is another way: In January of last year, he put a resolution on migration policy to a vote, which received a majority with votes from the AfD. For a brief moment, the Union had demonstrated that it did not want to make parliamentary majorities permanently dependent on how the AfD voted. Then Oblomovism prevailed: The impulse was there, but habit was stronger.

For Merz did not turn this into a permanent strategy. Instead of seizing the momentum and propagating it as a new guideline – to introduce motions based on its own convictions, without agreements with the AfD – the Union immediately went back on the defensive in the face of left-wing oversensitivity. In doing so, it possibly squandered its last opportunity to determine the rules of such an opening itself, instead of having them dictated to it at some point.

Every time a window opened, whether Thuringia, Rödder, or the 2025 resolution, it was the CDU itself that slammed the window shut with full force out of fear of the possibilities. With every rejection, the AfD grew.

The Russian novel «Oblomov» ends with the protagonist's early death. He succumbs to his lethargy. It led to his downfall. The Union could still awaken from its twilight sleep. If Merz cannot do that, someone else will have to.
