# A cowshed as a classroom: India’s Cockroach Movement denounces the state of schools

*Leaky roofs, broken toilets, dilapidated classrooms, a lack of teachers: India's schools are in a deplorable state. For Prime Minister Modi, the issue is extremely uncomfortable.*

India’s Prime Minister Narendra Modi is learning what Indian housewives have long known: once cockroaches spread, they are very difficult to get rid of. What concerns Modi, however, is less the common kitchen cockroach than the youth protest movement that took its name from the vermin. The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) emerged in May as a satirical party online but has since developed into a serious challenge for the government.

After CJP protests forced Indian Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan to resign at the end of July, the movement has set a new goal: «School thik karo!» (Fix the schools!) is its new battle cry. In the campaign, which the CJP launched on Independence Day on Aug. 15, it denounces the neglect of village schools and demands that children be provided with an appropriate learning environment.

The condition of many schools is indeed disastrous: Buildings are dilapidated, roofs are leaky, toilets are broken, and fans are missing. There are also hardly any computers or internet access. Often, there is a lack of teachers. Many positions are vacant, and teachers are regularly seconded to assist with elections. Thousands of teachers are currently working to support the Election Commission in verifying voter registers.

## Children taught in a cowshed in one village

CJP activists visited numerous schools last week to draw attention to the grievances. In one village, according to media reports, they found students in a cowshed because the actual school was so dilapidated that it had to be closed. Clashes occurred in several places. Supporters of the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) tried to prevent the activists from inspecting the school buildings, the CJP said. In West Bengal, the father of an activist died after a scuffle.

The campaign is putting the government under considerable pressure. It is receiving widespread attention in the media, with newspapers and television channels reporting extensively on the issue and publishing their own investigations into the state of schools. In Rajasthan, the government banned non-school personnel from entering schools. A study in the northern Indian state concluded last year that more than 5,600 schools there were so dilapidated that they were no longer usable.

In India, the individual states are primarily responsible for schools. CJP founder Abhijeet Dipke criticized after visiting a school in Maharashtra, saying that even dogs were treated better. The 30-year-old called on all politicians to send their children to state schools instead of private schools. He criticized that politicians were arguing about caste and religion instead of advocating for education. A party that had been in power for more than 10 years had to be held accountable for the state of the schools, he added.

## Cockroach Movement striking a nerve in India

Within a few months, the CJP has become the most important protest movement in years. The movement began after India's Supreme Court Justice Surya Kant made a derogatory remark, referring to unemployed youth as cockroaches. In a country where one in five university graduates cannot find work, outrage was widespread. In protest, student Dipke launched the CJP as the «Party of the Lazy and Unemployed.» Under the hashtag «I am also a cockroach,» millions of Indians quickly joined.

From the internet, the CJP soon spread to the streets. The CJP demanded that Education Minister Pradhan take responsibility for the annulment of the medical entrance exam in May due to a fraud case. On July 20, ugly scenes occurred during a march on parliament in Delhi: The police used batons, tear gas, and even buckshot against the schoolchildren and students. Dozens were injured.

The outrage over the harsh police action was so great that Modi had no choice but to accept Pradhan's resignation to de-escalate the situation. Never before had a minister been forced to resign due to public pressure since Modi took office in 2014. The success has energized the CJP and encouraged other civil society groups to take their concerns to the streets as well.

## Education minister's resignation emboldening CJP

In the state of Jharkhand, there were weeks of protests by young people denouncing fraud and corruption in civil service entrance exams. Many had applied for the coveted positions but had not been selected, even though they met the requirements. The protests culminated on Aug. 10 in the storming of the regional parliament in Ranchi. Here too, the police used tear gas and batons against the demonstrators.

To defuse the protests, the government made some concessions. Modi, for example, announced new programs for youth in his Independence Day speech. At the same time, however, the government is increasing pressure on the Cockroach Movement. Although it had promised not to take legal action against participants in the protests in Delhi and other cities, investigations against many demonstrators continue.

## Modi's difficulty discrediting protests

Protesters were angered when Modi said he «forgave the misguided children» for insulting him. Supreme Court Justice Surya Kant also fueled resentment when he said during a hearing on the protests that he did not have time to watch videos of police violence. Graduates of several law faculties then announced that they did not want to see him as an honorary guest at their graduation ceremony.

It is difficult for Modi to discredit the protests as a foreign conspiracy or to portray the CJP as enemies of the nation, as he usually does with critics. The desolate state of schools is causing discontent even within his own base, and the annulment of exams is mobilizing many BJP supporters. Repeated fraud in selection processes calls into question the middle class's hope for upward mobility through education. In any case, doubts are growing as to whether it is still worthwhile to invest a lot of money in a degree if there are no jobs in either the civil service or the economy for all these graduates.
