# Meat-fed, but not without needs

*The «An Chay» restaurant near Bullingerplatz boasts being Zurich's first vegetarian Vietnamese restaurant. But is that enough to impress?*

The area around Zurich’s Lochergut has transformed from a bleak and joyless patch into the city’s most vibrant culinary district. It seems almost everything launched there turns into a hype, from kebab shops to gelaterias and bars.

In the orbit of this success story, Bullingerplatz has also risen to become a popular meeting point. Against the backdrop of the peculiar church tower, which resembles a bookshelf, young people gather on mild evenings in the square’s center around the fountain, sitting on the asphalt among potted plants. Those who prefer to sit at a table seek their luck in front of the «Café du Bonheur» or, across the way, have been spoiled for eight years by the excellent «Barranco» with its Peruvian creations.

![Bullingerplatz becomes a popular meeting point on mild evenings.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/24/8e33873e-6402-4f18-95fc-e0960cf11fb6.jpg?crop=5712,4284,x0,y0)
*Bullingerplatz becomes a popular meeting point on mild evenings.* (Urs Bühler)

Half as old is «An Chay,» located just around the corner, facing away from the square. Its offerings, particularly the filled baguettes, had become popular with pop-ups during the Corona era before it settled in here and triggered collective ecstasy across various channels. Its concept reads like a checklist for trend followers: Vietnamese cuisine, natural wines including sparkling Pet Nat, and vegetarianism. Its name means «meatless eating,» which perfectly suits the diet of my goddaughter and her partner. So, off we go together!

However, the slogan «The first meatless Vietnamese cuisine in Zurich» seems a bit exaggerated, as the selection of vegetarian dishes in East Asian restaurants is traditionally large. Nevertheless, the option to replace the standard fish sauce with a soy-garlic sauce upon request can be considered innovative.

We take a seat on the small terrace, which, like the interior, is simply furnished with beer garden tables and a few plastic stools. To welcome us, a sip of the house’s cold tea is brought, a nice gesture, but the service unfortunately remains impersonal throughout the rest of the evening. They merely take the order instead of advising the guests. Unaware of the portion sizes, we promptly order too much from the menu, which is not handed out but must be accessed via a QR code on a mobile phone. It is consistently in English, which in this «very cosmopolitan city» will probably soon be considered normal.

The «Won Ton Garden» (16 Swiss francs) are an impeccable appetizer: dumplings with a hearty chili-peanut-sesame paste on a bed of still slightly crisp pak choi. «Com Bi Cha» (31 francs), on the other hand, a kind of best-of plate with the label «the no brainer,» proves to be a wild potpourri, from tofu sausage on skewers to panko-breaded mushrooms and a few cucumber slices. In the stew-like «Banh Canh Xao» (25 francs) with lotus roots, the overcooked udon noodles lack the captivating smoothness that ideally characterizes them. And the enjoyment of the mushroom dish «Nam Kho Tieu» (25 francs) is diminished by a significantly too salty soy sauce.

Generally, we don’t miss meat here, but we do miss the refined interplay of sweetness, acidity, and textures that has impressed us in other Vietnamese restaurants in the city. We have also experienced more attention to detail in plating there. It may be that the kitchen and service were having an off day during our visit – it wasn’t excessively hot – but despite moderate prices, what was offered didn’t impress either of the two twentysomethings or the near-boomer. We cannot join in the hymns of praise that began after the opening four years ago.

*A bright spot: the «Coconut Island» dessert.* (Urs Bühler)

The homemade iced teas and lemonades (from 7 francs) are refreshing, and among the desserts, the «Coconut Island» (14 francs) becomes a bright spot: finely diced mango, soy-caramel sauce, and delicate coconut ice cream, flanked by finger-long, crispy cassava cake sticks reminiscent of Kaiserschmarren or Fotzelschnitten.

![No, this affogato is no good.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/24/b3813260-4f05-4c68-bb3e-a24f9f4f2d7c.jpg?crop=3024,4032,x0,y0)
*No, this affogato is no good.* (urs.)

Finally, the coffee specialties bridge Asia and Europe: in addition to the Vietnamese-style filter coffee (6 francs), now also known in Zurich, which drips through a sieve into a glass with sweetened condensed milk at the bottom, there is the Saigonese Affogato variation (8.50 francs).

That this is prepared with Fior di Latte instead of vanilla ice cream, like almost all affogatos in the city, delights connoisseurs. However, this anticipation soon gives way to disillusionment: by the time the coffee has dripped through the sieve, the ice cream has completely melted – and into a gritty rather than creamy consistency. The vegan ice cream used seems unsuitable for this specialty. We prefer the less hip Italian place around the corner.
