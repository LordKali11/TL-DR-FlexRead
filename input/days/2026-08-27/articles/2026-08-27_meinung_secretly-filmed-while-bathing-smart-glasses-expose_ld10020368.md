Shortly thereafter, the entire encounter landed on TikTok – filmed with his glasses. The recording shocked the woman because she knew nothing about it. SWR made the case public this spring. Countless videos can be found online where people were filmed unnoticed: in the park, in the gym, or on the school playground.

Secret filming is nothing new. Until now, however, it required a drawn smartphone or a hidden camera. Now smart glasses are becoming the hidden camera.

Smart glasses are on their way to becoming a mass product. They are becoming cheaper, more inconspicuous, and more versatile. If tech companies have their way, they are to become as commonplace as smartphones, these companies say.

## Law without enforcement: weakening of the rule of law

The more commonplace these glasses become, the greater the contradiction between law and reality. Because Swiss law is clear. Anyone who intentionally films someone generally needs their consent. Even those filmed by chance must be able to recognize that recording is taking place. Only then can they object or demand deletion.

Smart glasses undermine this. With a held-up smartphone, everyone immediately knows that recording is taking place. With glasses, it is difficult to tell whether filming or just looking is happening. The LED light is barely visible in the sun and can even be removed from some models from the outset.

The problem is exacerbated by artificial intelligence (AI). For the glasses to analyze their surroundings with AI, they must capture images – and in some cases send them to the cloud. With newer Ray-Ban Meta glasses, the recording LED does not even light up for such AI functions.

The extent to which this development could go is shown by a patent application published two weeks ago in the U.S. by market leader Meta. This provides for a link between the glasses and AI facial recognition, according to the Meta patent application. At an aperitif, for example, it could display the name of the person opposite, the application states.

The patent goes even further: The glasses are intended to analyze the facial expressions of the person opposite and track interesting people – and create a personal memory archive from this, the patent application explains.

We are heading toward times when the right to one's own image exists on paper, but can no longer be enforced in everyday life.

But a law without enforcement weakens the rule of law.

## General ban no solution

Smart glasses are not bad per se. They can describe the surroundings to visually impaired people, display spoken words as text for the hearing impaired, guide through a recipe while cooking, and show instructions to craftsmen.

But a technology that is supposed to benefit society must fit into its rules – not the other way around.

First, users are responsible. They must not simply film strangers. This is illegal and in many cases simply inappropriate. But self-responsibility is not enough if those affected do not notice the recording.

Manufacturers also have a duty: Their glasses must protect privacy before damage occurs (Privacy by Design). This could include technical barriers for certain locations.

Where this is not enough, binding rules are needed. Public swimming pools, schools, gyms, and hospitals should consider a ban on camera glasses. Some German public swimming pools have already banned such glasses.

And in sensitive research facilities or military installations, the state must prohibit camera glasses or at least their recording function. It can also issue technical specifications for the devices.

As a society, we must now find a sensible way to deal with this technology – only then will these glasses become a benefit for all of us.
