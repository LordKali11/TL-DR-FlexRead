## The latest developments

## What happened?

A destructive flash flood occurred in the Himalayas, in the border region between Nepal and China, on Wednesday, 8/26. Ice and rock broke off over a width of 1.4 kilometers from a glacier on Langtang Lirung, a mountain over 7,200 meters high in northern Nepal. A massive wave of water, ice and debris plunged about 2 kilometers down into the narrow Bhotekoshi Valley. In some places, the flash flood reached speeds of more than 100 kilometers per hour.

Surveillance camera footage shows how coaches, trucks and buildings were swept away as if they were toys. On the Chinese side of the Gyirong border crossing, people ran for their lives and were washed away. Later drone images show total devastation; entire settlements have disappeared.

## How extensive is the disaster?

Authorities in Nepal have already confirmed more than 1,200 deaths, and approximately 4,900 people are missing. The number of dead and missing has been growing for days.

According to Red Cross estimates, more than 90,000 people were affected by the floods in total. Further floods are feared, and trucks carrying aid supplies were temporarily blocked by a newly formed reservoir. According to the UN Children's Fund Unicef, at least 17,000 children urgently needed humanitarian aid.

There are also numerous hydropower plants in the valley; according to the responsible association, a dozen plants in operation or under construction were damaged. In Nepal, all electricity is produced by hydropower. According to the Reuters news agency, 12% of Nepal's total electricity generation capacity was now out of action.

## What is known about the missing persons?

Among the missing were Nepalese men and women, as well as numerous tourists from 29 countries. A large number of them were Hindu pilgrims from India and Nepal who wanted to go to the holy Mount Kailash on the Tibetan side of the border. Among the missing were also a few Germans and Swiss. How many there were exactly and where they were located remained partially unclear.

Recently, the search for hundreds of missing workers had been intensified, many of whom were still presumed to be in the underground tunnel systems of devastated hydropower plants. The plants were located several floors beneath the mountain. Nearly 900 workers were initially unreachable; according to the Nepalese government, around 300 had since been rescued. A week after the tragedy, more than 600 remain missing.

## How do the rescue operations proceed?

Nepalese and Chinese security forces were working with specialist teams from India, South Korea and Australia. They were trying to open the tunnels of the hydropower plants and reach the workers. The entrances were partially buried by the flash flood, and some tunnels were flooded.

The government stated that it mobilized almost 21,000 rescue workers from the army and police. Since the flash flood swept away numerous bridges and washed away roads, many places could only be reached by helicopter. They were dropping tents, food and other aid supplies. Several villages, including in the northern Indian state of Bihar, were evacuated. A reservoir was drained to make room for the masses of water.

Barely a week after the disaster, rescue workers were able to restore road networks to the Nuwakot region, which was particularly severely affected by the floods. Aid could now slowly reach the population by land as well.

## What dangers are now looming?

The number of dead was so large that hospitals were quickly overwhelmed. There was no space in the morgues, but in the humid monsoon heat, the dead could not be stored for long. The authorities therefore began to bury them in a wooded area. Only a few victims could be identified beforehand. For storing the dead, the government requested additional refrigeration units from abroad.

Experts also warned of the outbreak of dangerous diseases. Employees of the aid organization Save the Children reported catastrophic hygienic conditions in the disaster area. Body bags lay along the road leading from Kathmandu to the largely destroyed city of Trishuli Bazar. The floods had undermined sewage treatment plants, and persistent rain further exacerbated the spread of feces.

According to the organization, children with fever and respiratory infections were being treated in the emergency shelters. There was particular concern about diarrheal diseases such as cholera and skin infections. Standing water also provided ideal breeding grounds for mosquitoes, thereby increasing the risk of dengue fever.
