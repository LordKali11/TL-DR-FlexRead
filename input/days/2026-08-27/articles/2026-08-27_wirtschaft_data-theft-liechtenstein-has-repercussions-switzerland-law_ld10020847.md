# The data theft in Liechtenstein has repercussions in Switzerland. A law professor says: «If there's a scandal abroad, it inevitably spills over to us.»

*Following the cyberattack on Liechtenstein's financial center, calls for a reform of the Swiss family foundation system are growing louder. However, there are also critics.*

Following the hacker attack in late July, the Liechtenstein government remains in the dark. On the night of July 30, unauthorized individuals accessed the «Register of Beneficial Owners» and stole 31,000 data records. The register lists the individuals behind foundations, companies, and trust vehicles in Liechtenstein.

It remains unclear who the perpetrators were. «Unfortunately, there is no new information here yet,» Stephan Agnolazza, personal assistant to Liechtenstein’s head of government Brigitte Haas, said upon inquiry. «The criminal investigations and international inquiries are ongoing.»

The data theft in Liechtenstein primarily targeted so-called private benefit foundations. These allow wealthy individuals to easily transfer assets to the next generation and continue to access their funds within the foundation’s purpose. This is not possible with Swiss foundations, however.

Many Swiss clients are likely affected by the data theft. This is fueling demands to introduce a similar foundation structure in Switzerland, similar to Liechtenstein's. There is likely sufficient demand, as around 100 billion Swiss francs are inherited in this country every year.

## Federal Council's protracted process

The desire for such a vehicle in Switzerland is not new: FDP Council of States member Thierry Burkart submitted Motion «Strengthening the Swiss Family Foundation. Abolishing the Prohibition of Maintenance Foundations» in December 2022, which the Council of States adopted in December 2023 and the National Council in February 2024. However, a draft bill from the Federal Council has been pending ever since.

«Switzerland lacks this important instrument for wealth and estate planning,» Burkart says in an interview. He adds that the family foundation may only be used very restrictively, while the maintenance foundation is prohibited. «That is completely outdated,» Burkart says.

Dominique Jakob, Professor of Foundation Law at the University of Zurich, sees this similarly. He has been advocating for years for the liberalization of the Swiss family foundation. Currently, those in need of planning are forced into foreign vehicles, such as Liechtenstein foundations, which we recognize in Switzerland without control, he says. In this way, we relinquish assets, but above all governance, and leave the value creation to other states, he contends.

## «Outdated case law from the 1950s»

According to the law professor, only a small amendment to the Swiss Civil Code (ZGB) and possibly additional governance provisions are needed to revive the family foundation. He notes that the family foundation is already regulated in Article 335 of the ZGB. «It is interpreted so restrictively by the courts due to outdated case law from the 1950s that it cannot be used meaningfully,» Jakob states. He adds that despite decades of criticism from academia, the case law has not reacted.

With such a family foundation, Switzerland would offer a previously missing instrument for intergenerational wealth transfer and business succession – not only for the very wealthy but also for broader segments of the population, Jakob says.

Politically, private benefit foundations and family maintenance foundations have had a difficult past in Switzerland. The topic is strongly associated in public with privileges for the very wealthy. According to Jakob, many people do not understand exactly what it is about – namely not privileges for «super-rich» individuals but, on the contrary, the strengthening of the rule of law, governance, as well as the foundation sector and the financial center. He maintains that the legislator now has the opportunity to correct this oversight.

## Federal Council's protracted process

Meanwhile, the deadline for FDP Council of States member Burkart’s motion, within which the Federal Council must submit a draft bill, has already expired six months ago. «It is unclear why this is being delayed so much,» Jakob says. If the draft bill comes, it will be interesting to see whether it is also designed to be practical, he adds. There is resistance in the left-wing parties to the changes envisaged in the motion, Thierry Burkart says. General reservations about inheritances and inherited wealth are not to be overlooked, he maintains.

But there are also legal scholars who are critical of demands for family maintenance foundations in Switzerland. These include Hans Michael Riemer, Professor Emeritus of Private Law at the University of Zurich and a specialist in foundation law.

Riemer offers a written stance. He lists various concerns about the introduction of family maintenance foundations in Switzerland.He writes that the legislator at the time wanted to avoid feudalistic wealth accumulations such as the former family entails. He explains that these were assets intended to be inherited within a family for an indefinite number of generations.

Riemer writes that the legislator therefore prohibited the re-establishment of such family entails and, accordingly, comparable family maintenance foundations that would be allowed to provide benefits without preconditions.

## «Free disposal of inheritance hindered»

He further states that with the introduction of maintenance foundations, the legally prescribed and socially desirable divisions of inheritance would also be prevented. He argues that heirs would be deprived of their full legal claims to these assets – apart from the founder’s first generation of heirs – which would hinder their free disposal of their inheritance and their personal and economic development. He adds that heirs would instead be tied to the foundation and dependent on the will of the foundation board, which could also lead to intra-family differences.

Riemer also fears that such foundations could deprive creditors of their liability substrate – i.e., assets that creditors could access in the event of bankruptcy or debt collection. He notes that debtors could be tempted to park or hide their assets in such maintenance foundations, especially since the new transparency law, which provides for the disclosure of beneficial owners, exempts foundations from its scope.

He continues that such foundations could also impair tax progression and the principle of taxation according to economic capacity. He contends that founders could finally distribute their assets to various foundations in several cantons. He argues that this would administratively complicate the aggregation of taxable assets and burden administration and courts.

Furthermore, Riemer also expresses concerns that such foundations could possibly attract funds from abroad that would be undesirable for various reasons and could cause foreign policy damage to Switzerland. He sees the risk of tax evasion, money laundering, or terrorist financing.

## «No new opportunities for tax evasion»

Dominique Jakob and Thierry Burkart disagree here. Jakob says that the liberalization of the Swiss family foundation is by no means about creating new opportunities for tax evasion or money laundering. He maintains that it instead creates more legal certainty for clients and more governance for the market.

He says this would also reduce the risk of data theft. Then everything would be in the hands of Swiss authorities and courts according to Swiss rules instead of abroad, he argues. In the current situation, Switzerland has no control whatsoever over structures established outside Switzerland, he adds. «If there’s a scandal abroad, it inevitably spills over to us,» Jakob says.

Burkart counters the fear that the Swiss family foundation could be misused as a tax avoidance vehicle after the reform. He says this is not possible in Switzerland. He notes that there is already an established tax practice. He adds that, moreover, foundations in Switzerland are public, registered in the commercial register, and closely monitored by the foundation supervisory authority.
