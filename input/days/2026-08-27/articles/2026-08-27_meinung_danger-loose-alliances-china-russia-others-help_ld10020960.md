# The danger of loose alliances: China, Russia, and others help each other. A formal alliance is not necessary for this

*Historically poor mutual support among authoritarian states: A changing dynamic, perhaps due to the lack of a formal alliance.*

China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia are not bound by a formal alliance. Yet actions often speak louder than words, and seen in this light, the message to the United States and its allies is clear.

Xi Jinping undertook his first foreign trip in 2026 to North Korea. There, he and Kim Jong Un agreed on an expansion of cooperation, without denuclearization having been mentioned. The meeting took place just weeks after Vladimir Putin’s 25th visit to China. Simultaneously, media reports detailed how Beijing and Moscow indirectly support Iran, for instance with rocket fuel and likely also with satellite images.

All of this does not yet constitute an official alliance system. Skeptical observers argue that cooperation remains superficial and that relations between the states are characterized by conflicting interests and mutual distrust. Even American security analyses hold a similar view in annual reports. In 2026, they conclude that relations between China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea are «limited and primarily bilateral.»

However, the absence of a solidified bloc does not mean that cooperation remains without consequences. On the contrary, the loose ties and bilateral structures may represent an advantage.

History teaches that alliances among illiberal countries are fragile, especially when more than two countries are involved. What is emerging between these four powers seems more flexible – and in some respects, more dangerous. A loose but effective network of military, technological, and political cooperation could be capable of surpassing the strength of a traditional alliance.

There is much to suggest that this is the case. In terms of technology transfer and military exchange, the four states have recently cooperated more closely than most previous authoritarian partnerships. For the West, the task now should be to understand and take these areas of cooperation seriously.

## Since the Ukraine War

Only since 2022 have China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia developed significant cooperation. The trigger was Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. After the attack, Moscow was confronted with harsh sanctions and dwindling ammunition depots. As a result, the Kremlin showed itself willing to disclose advanced military technologies and its operational know-how in exchange for military support.

North Korea initially refused significant support after Russia’s invasion but soon became an important supplier of artillery ammunition. In 2024, Kim Jong Un dispatched more than 10,000 soldiers to support Russian military operations – the first large-scale foreign deployment of North Korean combat troops. In return, Russia supported North Korea in modernizing its military, including satellites and air defense systems. Iran also became a central military partner for Russia through its drone deliveries.

China, however, has not made direct arms deliveries to Russia, but according to American analysts, it supplies dual-use technologies, including microelectronics, industrial machinery, and optical components. In return, Russia provides its partners with support that, American analysts say, could significantly alter the regional balance of power. For example, American Admiral Samuel Paparo warned at the end of 2024 that the delivery of submarine technology to China had the potential to end U.S. dominance in submarines.

These states also help each other mitigate Western sanctions. The indirect result is a parallel security and economic system that is less vulnerable to coercive measures by the U.S.

## Historical Precedents

The fact that this rapprochement is bilateral in nature and does not constitute a formal, cross-national alliance is no reason to underestimate it. On the contrary, it is difficult to imagine that cooperation between China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia would be more significant if it were to develop into a formal alliance following historical precedent.

In the 19th century, there was practically no joint military planning among allies. Alliances were mostly defensive structures. Unified command structures or a common doctrine were lacking. In contrast, China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia are actively strengthening each other today and increasingly sharing resources and know-how.

Even the most feared authoritarian alliance in modern history, the Axis powers of Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan, and Fascist Italy, was comparatively weak. In 1939, the three countries signed the Tripartite Pact, which committed them to mutual support in the event of an attack by a power not yet involved in the war.

Yet there was neither operational planning nor coordination. Germany did not inform Japan of its plan to invade the Soviet Union, and Japan kept its plans for Pearl Harbor secret. The Axis powers never carried out a joint military operation. Japan even concluded a neutrality pact with Moscow in 1941 and ignored Germany’s war on the Eastern Front until the final weeks of the war.

## Differences in the Alliance

Naturally, there are tensions between the four countries. For example, according to the «New York Times,» the Russian domestic intelligence service feared Chinese influence. Western scholars observe how Russians and Chinese each criticize the neighboring country. However, the heads of state and government determine the agenda. Their representatives meet regularly to resolve points of conflict in their relations.

At the same time, it seems highly unlikely at the moment that the authoritarian rivals of the West would launch a coordinated attack or directly support each other if the United States or NATO were to take military action against one of them. However, their cooperation represents one of the most significant and serious entanglements of authoritarian militaries in modern history.

As long as the current leaderships in these countries remain in power, the West has little chance of breaking up these relationships. However, Washington can take measures to limit the effects of the loose ties. For example, third countries can be mobilized to exert indirect pressure – this happened in 2024 when Saudi Arabia urged Russia not to supply the Houthi militia with modern missiles.
