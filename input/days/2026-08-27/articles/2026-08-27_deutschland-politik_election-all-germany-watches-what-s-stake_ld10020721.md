# An election all of Germany watches: What's at stake in Saxony-Anhalt?

*The consequences of the state parliament election in Saxony-Anhalt are likely to concern all of Germany. An analysis.*

On Wednesday evening, the TV debate for the state parliament election in Saxony-Anhalt, scheduled for Sept. 6, took place. The moderators introduced the debate between CDU lead candidate and Minister-President Sven Schulze and his challenger Ulrich Siegmund of the AfD with strong words. They said it was an election that «all of Germany is watching,» and that the election had the potential «to change not only Saxony-Anhalt.»

The debate between the two candidates then proceeded in a similarly polarizing manner. Minister-President Schulze said that the people Siegmund politically associated himself with were «highly dangerous, not only for Saxony-Anhalt, but also for Germany.» Siegmund said in the debate that he would «make every single political decision based on only one criterion: What is right for our own country and our own people.» The two candidates frequently interrupted each other during the 30-minute broadcast, exchanged accusations and engaged in an extremely heated debate. Towards the end of the broadcast, Siegmund said that Saxony-Anhalt was «dependent on the completely insane decisions in Berlin.» He added that «We must finally stop playing along with that. It is over now.»

Is that possible? And what would be the consequences if the AfD were to win the election? For months, the right-wing party has led in polls by an enormous margin and could even achieve an absolute majority. This would mean that the AfD could govern without a coalition partner. This would be a momentous event – for the parties, Saxony-Anhalt and the Federal Republic as a whole.

## 1. Implications for the individual parties

The election in Saxony-Anhalt is the first of three elections taking place this fall. On Sept. 20, citizens in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern will also elect new state parliaments. The parties hope to gain momentum for the subsequent elections with the best possible result.

For months, it has appeared that the election in Saxony-Anhalt is likely to be a success for only one party: the AfD. In polls, the right-wing party polls at up to 43% and could therefore – depending on the final election result – govern alone. The CDU polls at around 23%, followed by the Left Party at 13%, the SPD at 7%, and the Greens at 5%. Currently, it appears that the BSW will narrowly miss and the FDP will clearly miss the necessary 5% hurdle.

Regarding whether the AfD could achieve a sole government, the following rule of thumb applies: The fewer parties that enter the state parliament, the more likely a sole government is. The more parties that enter, the less likely a sole government is.

For the AfD, it would be a historic success, marking the first time the party assumes governing responsibility at the state level. At the same time, it would be an enormous test. For the first time, it would have to appoint ministers and state secretaries and prove that it could do better than the established parties it has criticized for years. Even within the AfD, some are uncertain whether that would work or whether participation in government would come too soon. Observers question whether Siegmund, who would become Minister-President, would be up to the task.

For the CDU, which has been the strongest force in Saxony-Anhalt continuously since 2002, the election is likely to be a blow. In 2021, an AfD victory already seemed imminent in the state election, before then-Minister-President Reiner Haseloff rallied with an anti-AfD stance and won the election. Such a comeback is hardly imaginable given the lead of around 20% points. For the Christian Democrats, the goal will likely be to gain a few more % points before the election, preventing the AfD from governing alone. All other parties rule out a coalition government with the AfD, and AfD lead candidate Siegmund recently said that he only wanted to govern alone.

For the Left Party, which is likely to become the third strongest force, the stable poll results of 13% are encouraging. But in Saxony-Anhalt, the Left Party was once stronger: in 2016, it reached 16%, and in 2011 and 2006, around 24%. For the Social Democrats and the Greens, the election will merely be about clearing the 5% hurdle. Should they fail to do so, this is likely to have consequences, especially for the Social Democrats' federal party. Observers suggest the question, already raised, of whether party leaders Lars Klingbeil and Bärbel Bas are the right people, is likely to be debated even more intensely.

## 2. Implications for the state of Saxony-Anhalt

The state domestic intelligence agency classifies the Saxony-Anhalt state branch as a confirmed right-wing extremist endeavor. The party rejects this classification. Should the AfD govern, it would itself be responsible for the police and the state domestic intelligence agency, which raises concerns among politicians. It could replace the head of the domestic intelligence agency and set different priorities: away from right-wing extremism and toward Islamism or left-wing extremism.

Even today, there are various considerations in Berlin political circles about whether it would be possible to keep security-relevant information from the other state domestic intelligence agencies and the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution secret from Saxony-Anhalt. Critics of the AfD warn of possible misuse of intelligence information. In addition to the police, education and culture are also state matters. In schools, the AfD could also set different priorities, for example, focusing more on the German Empire instead of World War II. Furthermore, the AfD opposes inclusive schools and wants to reorganize public broadcasting.

Economically, an AfD government could also affect Saxony-Anhalt. The Leibniz Institute for Economic Research Halle (IWH) concludes in an analysis that at least 2.2 billion euros are missing to implement the AfD’s election program. The institute also assumes that economic growth in Saxony-Anhalt could decline by about half a to 1% per year, as there could be less immigration and fewer workers. The German Economic Institute Cologne (IW) also recently analyzed that in East Germany, without immigration, there would be a shortage of workers. It remains to be seen whether these predictions would prove true in reality. The AfD’s program ranges from economically liberal to seemingly socialist demands.

Even if the AfD does not govern, it is likely to win more than a third of the seats. This would give it the so-called blocking minority, with which it could block central processes of the state parliament – such as constitutional amendments or reappointments to the state constitutional court.

## 3. Implications for the Federal Republic

If the polls prove true in the election on Sept. 6, this is likely to have implications for the Federal Republic as a whole. Germany’s 16 federal states coordinate, for example, within the framework of the Minister-Presidents’ Conference (MPK) or the conferences of specialized ministers. Within the MPK, the state leaders regularly meet four times a year to coordinate important matters regarding cooperation and the alignment of political measures.

The new head of government of Saxony-Anhalt will immediately come into focus a few weeks after the election: The eastern German state will take over the chairmanship of the Minister-Presidents’ Conference from Oct. 1, 2026, to Sept. 30, 2027. This provides a federal state primarily with political weight, coordination tasks, and a representative host role.

Observers question whether an AfD government would constructively engage here and cooperate with other state governments and the federal government. They also wonder whether and how the governments of the other federal states would cooperate with Saxony-Anhalt.

In addition, the federal states have a say and sometimes a veto right in all-German legislation through the Bundesrat, the second chamber. The state government of Saxony-Anhalt has 4 of 69 votes in the Bundesrat. Thus, it alone could not prevent federal laws, but it could be decisive in close votes.

If, following election defeats for the CDU and SPD in Saxony-Anhalt, Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern were also lost, pressure on the federal government is likely to increase. Observers suggest that it would then even appear possible for the coalition to break apart and Federal Chancellor Friedrich Merz to be replaced.
