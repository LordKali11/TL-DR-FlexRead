Underwear crumpled behind the sofa for weeks, an iPad buried in the bedding, a pizza box, complete with gnawed crusts, stuck on the shelf. On the floor, strewn with discarded sweatshirts, might reveal a ticket for fare evasion (unpaid), an unfinished German essay (not handed in), chewing gum wrappers, vape pods: When today's parents cast a fleeting glance into a teenager's room, they are usually shocked.

Some fear that the teenage chaos can only lead to a problematic adult life, while others wonder how their years of input regarding respectful coexistence and a sense of order could have come to nothing. Still others are simply aghast that their carefully applied parenting style is now only met with «Chill out, dude!»

The disheartened parents then ask themselves: Is this age-appropriate laziness? Or does the disorder in the room reflect the disorder in the offspring's mind, and is it perhaps even a terrible omen for their future development?

One father, an academic and engaged individual, tells how he and his wife regularly despair when their two daughters visit from their prestigious studies abroad, and the parents have to urgently clean a devastated kitchen the next morning, complete with stacks of pans, overflowing ashtrays, and a plundered refrigerator. A mother, a fashion stylist, creative and approachable, turned over the granny flat to her teenage sons, 16 and 19, half a year ago. The result: dried-up houseplants, frequently overslept alarms, friends who swarm in like locusts, leaving desolation.

What went wrong?

## Stressed parents' advice: «Chill out, dude!»

Family counselor Nicole Wilhelm, a mother of four adult children and a trainer for relationship-oriented parent education at familylab Germany, says not much at all. Overwhelmed mothers and fathers regularly seek help there. These parents, Wilhelm observes, value education, a willingness to perform, and good coexistence, but in their children's teenage phase, they panic about whether they have missed something essential. The elternwissen.com network also notes that conflict over a semi-navigable teenager's room triggers one in four parent-child arguments.

Of course, parents have had the problem with untidy children's rooms for a long time – at least since the end of authoritarian «Struwwelpeter» (a cautionary children's book character) pedagogy and corporal punishment. But has the pendulum perhaps swung too far? Have children become lazy and irresponsible as a result?

Wilhelm quickly brushes aside such derogatory adjectives. She notes that «we increasingly live in a service society. No one can drive from Cologne to Dortmund without phone help, or cook a recipe from memory. Everything is outsourced online.» The consequence, she explains, is that the essential basic need to master something is no longer met. She adds that «this primarily affects adults, but children are also no longer allowed to do anything on their own.»

Wilhelm cites examples: children chauffeured everywhere by cargo bike, elaborate holiday suitcases packed by their mother, or a father hastily cooking while parking the kids in front of the laptop. «Parents today provide a lot of service,» she says. This is well-intentioned, she explains, but they actually treat their children «like guests in a hotel.» Then they wonder when their children actually behave accordingly, she adds.

«We are increasingly taking away developmental opportunities from our children; we are actively discouraging them from being part of a community and contributing to it independently,» Wilhelm says. The counselor does not offer this as an accusation. She merely observes the societal current in which we all more or less consciously swim.

## Parents' role in children's negative feelings

From her perspective, a factor comes into play that is fundamentally welcome but also has a catch: parental empathy. Wilhelm gives top marks to the overprotective generation, which is currently struggling helplessly with its «hotel kids,» for their empathy. This, she says, is «wonderful» to begin with. They pay very close attention not to violate any boundaries, she adds.

Such rising emphasis on empathy is evident, for example, in evaluations of parenting guides or survey results on parenting goals: The trend in recent decades clearly moves away from authoritarian and obedience-oriented parenting toward an autonomy-promoting, partnership-based approach. This is also reflected in newer trends such as Attachment Parenting.

But then, Wilhelm says, a «confusion had taken place» in society. If a child was a little frustrated, she explains, «we immediately thought: Now they are unhappy.» We didn't want that, so we became hyperactive, she adds. Bluntly put, today we can hardly bear our sons' and daughters' negative feelings about an unemptied bicycle trailer, shoes that are difficult to tie, or laundry that needs to be hung up, and – zap – we do it ourselves.

The dilemma: We believe we are protecting the child's integrity, but we are preventing them from learning something – such as how to peel carrots, fold laundry, or check off a shopping list at the supermarket.

## Family life: A race against time

The parenting shortcut, «Oh, then I'll just do it myself!,» is also due to another circumstance: Parental life has become more stressful for various reasons, as a study by the German Körber Foundation shows. Professional challenges and the expectations of parenthood are more demanding. Today, women automatically shoulder both job and care work, while men are far more called upon to be involved at home. Wilhelm estimates that while the workload for a traditional couple was 100%, «today it is more like 180%, even if still unequally distributed.»

The consequence: Family life is increasingly a race against the clock. In other words, if it takes little Diego forever to make his sandwich before daycare, a parent quickly grabs the knife themselves. This saves the conflict and the mess. «Parents are under extreme pressure, so they keep children out of the daily hustle. That's easier,» Wilhelm says.

So now, with a newly trained eye, look again at the youngster's den. You have done a lot right. Wilhelm points out that the equal treatment of children is actually non-negotiable. «We wanted and should definitely do things differently than our parents,» she says. «We have understood what the old culture of obedience caused,» she adds. And yet, she notes, it costs an incredible amount of energy to develop new parenting behaviors. «We are pioneers,» she says. «We experiment and make mistakes.»

## A relaxed dialogue, not submission

But how do we now deal with the out-of-control situation in Hotel Mama (a German idiom for living at home with parents where everything is provided)? First, acknowledge that we have indeed left far too little room for development and personal responsibility. Wilhelm says that understanding this dynamic «could really help parents progress.»

She dismisses shouting parents who dwell on a lack of respect. She asks how a young adult, who has been trained in exactly this system for years, is suddenly supposed to understand that everything is different now. She recommends a maximally relaxed dialogue, stating, «Submission does not work here.» She adds that it is «better to count it as a parenting success that young people now defend their dignity.»

One might pause and suspect that authoritarian commands cannot work. Better to explain oneself, formulate one's own boundaries, and listen. Because only in a good, friendly atmosphere can changes be negotiated. For example, how a washing machine works, why you don't want to constantly trip over shoes in the hallway, or that you don't want mold behind the bed for hygienic reasons. «If you furiously throw laundry into the room, you have war. No one wants that,» Wilhelm says. She emphasizes that parents are still responsible for the quality of the relationship. But, she adds, adolescents have an immense interest in good contact with their parents.

## How to get teenagers out of silent rebellion?

It is important to remember: The teenage brain is far from fully developed – areas in the prefrontal cortex responsible for structure, planning, or self-organization are still developing. This is also why some children are overwhelmed during puberty and must first learn how to manage disorder independently.

Parental anger about tidiness can then seem either senseless, stressful, or even hurtful. Educational scientist and pediatrician Herbert Renz-Polster also finds the conflict bothersome. He states that «what parents suddenly demand tends to trigger an internal resignation, a silent rebellion against the diminished autonomy of action.» He provocatively asks, «How would exactly the same children organize their rooms if they no longer lived in their parents' household and had responsibility for their daily lives?»

Renz-Polster is bothered that it is ultimately adults who run things according to their ideas: they make the program, they set the bar high, and they want children to participate, but only according to their plan. This does not align well with the autonomy program of adolescents.

The pediatrician draws a comparison that is quite profound: «I like to say here: Anyone who rides with training wheels as a child only learns to ride independently later – and only when the training wheels are gone,» Renz-Polster says.

So: take a deep breath and let go. Ideally. Wilhelm offers comfort, saying, «They have lived with their tidy parents 24/7 and learned how it works.» «It's a bit like a native language,» she adds. This aligns with what a mother recounts, who for years suffered from the unsightly mess in her younger son's room, either secretly tidying up or publicly protesting (both actions made the teenager punch the wall in anger). The now 22-year-old has lived in another city. When she asked him what his shared apartment looked like, he replied, «Mom, what do you think? Everything is absolutely spick and span here.»
