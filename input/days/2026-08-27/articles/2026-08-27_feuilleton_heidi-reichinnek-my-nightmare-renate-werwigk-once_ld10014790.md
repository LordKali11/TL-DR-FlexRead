# «Heidi Reichinnek? My nightmare» – Renate Werwigk once sat in a Stasi prison, today she fights against the historical revisionists of the Left and the AfD

*Thousands of citizens were arrested for political reasons during the GDR dictatorship, hundreds murdered. Eyewitnesses are becoming rare. Two of them remember.*

A sound Renate Werwigk-Schneider cannot stand is clicking locks. «It gives me goosebumps,» she says, «even at the airport, I can’t bring myself to lock the bathroom door.» Werwigk-Schneider is 87; she sits in the conservatory of her house in Berlin-Lichterfelde, where strong filter coffee is served. Outside, geraniums are blooming; on the table lies a «Tagesspiegel» next to a stack of yellowed files from the GDR State Security.

Renate Werwigk-Schneider developed her fear of keys only as a young woman. That was when she was imprisoned in the Stasi prison of Hohenschönhausen in 1963, for six weeks completely alone, in absolute silence. Without a visit from a lawyer or a relative. And without knowing where she was. Werwigk-Schneider calls it «isolation torture.» In prison, she was just a number, arrested on Feb. 20, 1963, while attempting to flee to West Berlin with her parents through a secret tunnel.

## Witnesses slowly dying out

«The only thing I wanted was to live in freedom,» she says. «To decide where I live, to travel to Paris or New York, to freely express my opinion and read what I want.» Renate Werwigk-Schneider speaks without resentment or bitterness. The fear of clicking locks is merely «my door damage» to her. However, the retired pediatrician is very serious about politics.

She is one of the 170,000 to 280,000 people who were arrested and imprisoned for political reasons during communist rule in East Germany from 1946 to 1989. For attempted escapes, undesirable opinions, or simply because scapegoats were needed. The officially progressive and humanitarian regime of the Socialist Unity Party of Germany (SED) sentenced around 220 accused individuals to death.

Tens of thousands were deported to the Soviet Union, to camps and prisons, especially in the first post-war years. Not just old Nazis, as GDR apologists continue to suggest to this day, but also uninvolved individuals or political opponents such as Potsdam CDU mayor Erwin Köhler, who was shot in Moscow in 1951 with his wife for «anti-Soviet agitation.»

The witnesses to these crimes are slowly dying out. And many of them fear that with them, awareness of the fragility of democracy will also be lost. Especially when democracy is threatened by people who, like the SED and its ideological heirs, claim to stand for peace, justice, and anti-fascism.

## Heidi Reichinnek: «My nightmare!»

«Unfortunately, there is a lot of ignorance and Ostalgie,» says Renate Werwigk-Schneider. «Many people had made themselves comfortable in the GDR and kept quiet. Now they claim it wasn’t so bad after all.»

The pensioner has been fighting against this ignorance since the fall of the Wall. She calls it «contemporary witness work.» She appears before school classes and at memorial events; last year, she published her biography. «There’s No Such Thing as a Little Dictatorship» is the title. This is essentially a truism, but it is an understandable warning given the electoral successes of left-wing and right-wing historical revisionists.

The AfD and the Left Party, which officially succeeded the SED after the collapse of the GDR, are of particular concern to Werwigk-Schneider. The Left Party could become the strongest party in the Berlin House of Representatives election in September; according to surveys, it receives 49% of the vote among younger voters.

This is probably also thanks to its new figurehead, Heidi Reichinnek, who makes old-fashioned socialist slogans suddenly seem attractive again. For Renate Werwigk-Schneider, Reichinnek is simply «my nightmare.» She recently thought the Left Party had run its course. «Now that it has unfortunately risen again, I have to continue,» she says.

## Grandfather never returns

Born Renate Grossmann in 1938, Werwigk-Schneider grew up in a Christian environment that maintained contacts with artists and Nazi opponents. Her father, Gottlieb Grossmann, was a pastor who was trained as a doctor during the war. According to Werwigk-Schneider’s Stasi file, he was an NSDAP member for several years but was expelled from the party because he refused to swear an oath to the Führer.

After the war, the family in Teupitz experienced the communist takeover, which gradually established a dictatorship in East Germany with Soviet help. Renate’s maternal grandfather, also a pastor, was arrested by Russians one day directly after church service and never returned.

Repression was most brutal in the post-war years, still marked by Stalinism, with show trials, torture, and judicial murder. When the population rebelled against the SED regime on June 17, 1953, at least 34 people died. Several were sentenced to death after the suppression of the uprising, and hundreds were sentenced to prison terms. The SED, together with Western communists, claimed they had thwarted a «fascist provocation,» but the propaganda hardly took hold.

Thanks to secretly received Western broadcasts, the GDR population was informed about the events. It was shocking, Renate Werwigk-Schneider writes in her book, to see «tanks driving through Berlin again» eight years after the war.

## Escape from allotment garden

Nevertheless, she experienced the 1950s as a relatively carefree time. She refused to join the communist Free German Youth (FDJ), became involved in the Christian Young Congregation, and was briefly expelled from school because of it. Despite her reluctance «to become active for the social order of our republic,» as stated in a school report in 1956, she was allowed to study medicine thanks to her father’s intervention.

And since the Wall had not yet been built, GDR citizens at that time could still travel unhindered to the West, to the Zoological Garden, to the library, to the cinema, to the theater, or to the KaDeWe department store. «I wore Western clothes and high heels, rode my GDR scooter through the city, which my parents had given me for my Abitur,» Renate Werwigk-Schneider remembers. «With the building of the Wall, all that was over.»

When the construction crews arrived on Aug. 13, 1961, she was 22 years old and lived near Bernauer Strasse. She describes the scenes she experienced there in her book as follows: «People tried to jump from their houses or rappel down. The saving West began on the sidewalk in front and was only a few meters away. As I stood there, I was seized by anger and disbelief. Above all, I was sad. Families were separated, couples torn apart.»

With the erection of the «anti-fascist protective wall,» the SED succeeded in stopping the mass exodus from its alleged workers’ and farmers’ paradise. By 1961, around 2.8 million people had left the Soviet-occupied zone of Germany.

For Renate Werwigk-Schneider, the sealing of the border was a turning point. «Now we knew we were trapped. But to decide only between the Ore Mountains and the Baltic Sea, that was out of the question for me.» Her younger brother was the first to flee. In an allotment garden, he discovered a border crossing secured only by barbed wire and escaped with the help of a ladder.

Renate’s parents hesitated at first; her father, in particular, did not want to abandon his congregation and his patients. But when a student from West Germany told Renate about a secret underground tunnel being built under Bernauer Strasse, the family decided to flee.

## «Something is wrong with the tunnel. Get out!»

«Nervous, excited, and full of uncertainty,» Werwigk-Schneider writes in her book, her parents arrived at their apartment in Berlin on Feb. 14, 1963. Together, they drove to Brunnenstrasse 45, where they were supposed to knock on a door with about thirty other would-be escapees and ask, «Does Lindemann live here?» However, the answer «Yes, you were lucky» did not come. Instead, a pale escape helper rushed up and shouted: «Something is wrong with the tunnel. Get out!»

![A woman escapes through the window to West Berlin, September 24, 1961.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/26/51570c50-c15c-4c69-b728-c1dde736260e.jpg?crop=2547,2449,x0,y0)
*A woman escapes through the window to West Berlin, September 24, 1961.* (Ullstein / Getty)

A few days later, Renate and her father were arrested. They only learned after the fall of the Wall that the Stasi had been informed about the tunnel construction from the beginning, thanks to a young informant from Leipzig who had charmed one of the helpers.

In court, the DDR prosecutors placed the family’s case in the broadest global political context. Since, from their point of view, a decent person would never flee socialism, they interpreted the attempted tunnel escape as part of a plan by «West German militarists and revanchists» who were preparing aggression against the GDR.

![Renate Werwigk-Schneider](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/25/c5ebb4ba-e518-4bf9-9e95-c320f82816a1.jpg?crop=1401,1868,x0,y13)
*Renate Werwigk-Schneider* (PD)

The judges wisely identified agents and the «agitation» of the West German press as the cause of the escape attempts, rather than the accused’s individual love of freedom.

The accused were driven to the trial in a camouflaged Barkas delivery van. Renate’s van bore the inscription «Fruit and Vegetables.» She received a prison sentence of two and a half years; her father had to serve three and a half, and her mother one year.

## Second escape leads to «Murder Castle»

«My father was broken by the imprisonment,» she recounts. «He was traumatized, constantly felt persecuted by Stasi agents even after his release.» She herself was weakened but not broken after her release in Jan. 1965. What gave her hope at the time was that the GDR leadership had discovered a new business model: it was selling political prisoners to the «fascists» of the Federal Republic of Germany.

The central figure in this human trafficking was Wolfgang Vogel, a GDR lawyer who liked to drive up in a golden Mercedes and negotiated with the West on behalf of the Stasi. Renate Werwigk-Schneider met him at Teupitzer See, and he advised her to attempt a second escape to get herself imprisoned again, because only then would she have a chance to get on the list of dissidents to be exchanged.

So, in 1967, Werwigk-Schneider tried to escape during a trip to Bulgaria. The escape failed, albeit unintentionally, and the young doctor had to return to prison. This time to the notorious Hoheneck women’s prison in Saxony. Here she got to know another side of GDR penal enforcement.

While the interrogating officers and guards in Hohenschönhausen, after the brutality of the Stalin era, had focused more on psychological pressure and isolation torture, the political prisoners in Hoheneck were deliberately exposed to the terror of criminal fellow inmates, she says.

«The crowd was indescribable,» she says, «I sat with child murderers who bumped into us political prisoners and dumped dirt in front of us that we had to wipe away.» In the Saxon «castle,» around 24,000 women were imprisoned until the end of the GDR, about a third for political reasons. Two former concentration camp guards also served their sentences there.

The women in the «Murder Castle» had to sew bed linen and stockings – one reason why Renate Werwigk-Schneider despises not only clicking keys but also sewing machines – which were then sold to the West. For the quality of the food, Werwigk-Schneider has only one word: «disgusting.»

When asked how she managed not to go crazy during her multiple prison stays, she replies: «My rage at being in prison like a criminal was enormous. So I hardly had time for demoralizing thoughts.»

## Storming SED party headquarters

Günter Toepfer, too, survived his imprisonment with this attitude. The 84-year-old now lives in Berlin-Karlshorst, in his parents’ house. «You can keep your shoes on, provided you haven’t stepped in anything,» he says by way of greeting. On the living room table, he has two old keys ready; one of them is a souvenir from Hohenschönhausen that he acquired shortly after the fall of the Wall. «When they banged on this,» he says with a gesture, «the guards could enter the cell without any warning.»

Toepfer first went to prison in 1953, at the age of twelve, but more or less voluntarily. In elementary school in Jena, he and his comrades saw the principal whispering with the teacher. When she came into the classroom distraught and explained that something terrible had happened and they should go home immediately, the students were all the more drawn to the city center.

There they found themselves in the middle of the popular uprising of June 17. «The crowd had just stormed the SED district leadership,» he recounts. «They found rifles and threw them onto the street; I handed them to a woman who then stuck the barrels into the gutter.» In front of the prison, the mass of protesters pushed him into the building; «there I carefully looked at everything. Of course, I had no idea that I would actually be imprisoned eight years later.»

Günter Toepfer’s father was an expert in building damage in Berlin at that time. Like Renate Werwigk-Schneider’s father, he could offer his family a certain protection because the GDR state, suffering from emigration, tried to retain qualified people. Günter Toepfer needed this protection.

«The paternalism and repression made me an opponent of this system even as a teenager,» he recounts. «When Stalin died in March 1953, we all had to be sad on command. In the stairwell, they put up a bust that we were supposed to salute. I refused to do that.» He also refused to join the Young Pioneers.

## Indictment for «endangering world peace»

The building of the Berlin Wall on Aug. 13, 1961, also marked a turning point in Günter Toepfer’s life. At that time, he was an architecture student. «The GDR was now officially a prison with an exit. Or without an exit, if you ended up in jail.» Shortly after the initial shock, Toepfer discussed a possible escape with a friend. Together they wanted to hide in a bus that transported American soldiers from East Berlin to the West via Checkpoint Charlie.

Toepfer considered the undertaking too risky. His friend, however, went to the Checkpoint for a second look – and was promptly arrested. «They probably beat him up for a night,» Toepfer recounts. «The next day, the Stasi picked me up here in this house.»

The then 20-year-old spent 192 days in solitary confinement, including in Hohenschönhausen. Without any contact with the outside world. The Barkas delivery van that transported him between investigative prisons and detention centers bore the inscription «Fresh fish for every table.» As tensions between East and West reached a peak in 1961/62, they wanted to indict the young student for «endangering world peace through military border penetration.»

«Endangering world peace, I ask you,» Toepfer says. He managed to divert attention from himself by refusing to participate in a show trial and got away with a one-year prison sentence, he adds. In prison, he contracted dysentery, among other things, and in one prison, he was threatened by criminals who wanted to tattoo him. «I never knew before falling asleep if I would still be alive the next day,» he says.

## Billions earned from human trafficking

After his release in 1962, Toepfer was comparatively lucky. Initially a laborer, he was able to continue his studies and work in construction thanks to the mediation of lawyer Wolfgang Vogel. He never truly felt free, more «like a serf.»

When the GDR collapsed in 1989/90, Toepfer joined the CDU. In the Berlin House of Representatives, he advocated, among other things, against the statute of limitations for Stasi crimes and for the renaming of streets named after Walter Ulbricht and other GDR dignitaries.

By that time, Renate Werwigk-Schneider had long been living in the West. On July 14, 1968, she was unexpectedly picked up from Hoheneck Castle and driven by Stasi agents to the border with the FRG. Here, Wolfgang Vogel awaited her with his golden Mercedes. Upon arrival in the FRG, Vogel received a suitcase with 100,000 Deutschmarks from West German intelligence agents. «That’s how much my life was apparently worth,» she says. «Incidentally, the GDR got a spy back.»

In total, the GDR sold over 33,000 political prisoners to the West German state, earning around 3.4 billion Deutschmarks. «A kilo of human flesh for a thousand marks, that was better than selling oysters,» says Günter Toepfer, who also considered a second escape attempt after prison.

Toepfer and Renate Werwigk-Schneider do not know each other, but there are many parallels. Both have been awarded the Federal Cross of Merit for their commitment in the reunited Federal Republic. And both observe with concern how the past is glorified, victims are forgotten, and perpetrators make careers or are at least morally acquitted.

## AfD as new home for Stasi officers

According to historians’ estimates, around 1,000 people died at the inner-German border during the Cold War. Only a single shooter went to prison for murder after the fall of the Wall. Most of the 250,000 official and 624,000 unofficial Stasi employees got away without punishment. «Many of them ended up receiving a higher pension than the victims,» says Günter Toepfer.

Despite 100,000 investigations, the alleged «victor’s justice» of the FRG sent only 46 representatives of the former GDR regime to prison, among them the last GDR Council of State chairman, Egon Krenz.

Krenz is now an author and a star in circles that mourn socialism, view Putin as a victim of Western aggression, and admire dictatorships like China. The mouthpiece of this scene is the newspaper «Junge Welt,» which commemorated the 50th anniversary of the Berlin Wall’s construction in 2011 with the headline «At this point, we simply say: Thank you!»

Representatives of the Left Party, which long served as a gathering point for old Stasi members and GDR functionaries, are now trying to distance themselves from the GDR. However, they propagate the same «anti-fascism» with which the SED legitimized its crimes. The Linksjugend, like the SED, fraternizes with Arab anti-Semites and the Castro dictatorship in Cuba. Recently, GDR flags were waved and slogans like «Egon Krenz is hot!» were shouted at a demonstration.

The Bündnis Sahra Wagenknecht and, above all, the AfD have also been courting old GDR nostalgics for several years. Hubertus Knabe, the former head of the Hohenschönhausen Memorial, recently stated in an interview that the AfD now had more compromised personnel in its ranks than the Left Party. Among other things, a former Stasi officer was running in the elections in Saxony-Anhalt.

«In the GDR, we were governed by a syndicate of criminals,» says Günter Toepfer. «Every communist can travel to Mallorca today; we weren’t even allowed on the highest mountain in the GDR because it was a restricted area. After the fall of the Wall, I never thought people would forget that.» He has a simple explanation for the increasing fascination with socialism among younger voters: «No wonder, they didn’t experience it.»

Renate Werwigk-Schneider quotes a letter in her book that the board of the Hohenschönhausen Memorial wrote to her after she was awarded the Federal Cross of Merit. «You are stronger than your former persecutors,» it says. «For that,» she says, «I want to fight until the end.»
