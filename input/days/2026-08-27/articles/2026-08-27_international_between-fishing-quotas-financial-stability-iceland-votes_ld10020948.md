# Between fishing quotas and financial stability: Iceland votes on resuming EU accession negotiations

*Those in favor of joining the EU hope for economic stability and security in an uncertain world. Opponents of the referendum see Iceland's sovereignty as being at risk. Polls predict a close result.*

The Icelandic electorate faces an important decision on Saturday: «Should Iceland resume accession negotiations with the European Union?» is written on the ballot. An eventual yes to this question is not a yes to accession. After successful negotiations, Icelanders would have to definitively decide on EU accession in a second referendum.

The Atlantic island unilaterally froze accession negotiations in 2013 after about three years and a change of government; in 2015, it officially asked Brussels to remove Iceland from the list of candidate countries. The fact that the EU question is now back on the agenda is also the result of a change in power. Prime Minister Kristrun Frostadottir, who has been in office since the end of 2024, had planned to inject new life into the accession process in 2027. There are several reasons why the referendum is coming a year earlier.

## Trump's changed assessment of Greenland

One reason is American President Donald Trump's intention to bring Greenland under his control by all means. The fact that he repeatedly confused Greenland with Iceland, and that the designated American ambassador jokingly referred to his future workplace as the 52nd U.S. state, was not found funny in Reykjavik. Doubts grew as to whether Iceland could count on one of its most important alliance partners. Iceland has no military of its own, relying instead on security guarantees from NATO (of which it is a founding member) and an agreement with the U.S., which maintained a military base on the island until 2006.

In view of the geopolitical upheavals and the tense security situation in the Arctic, EU proponents see Iceland's anchoring in Europe as a logical step. Referendum opponents, however, say the U.S. is not interested in Iceland, offering the consolation that the situation will ease with a new occupant in the White House.

## The EU as a guarantor of financial stability

The second argument of the yes side is the hope for long-term financial stability. Households and homeowners are groaning under rising inflation and high interest rates; the central bank has just raised the key interest rate to 8%, the third increase since March.

When Iceland set sail on an EU course in 2009, it was against the backdrop of a severe banking and debt crisis. Replacing the weak and volatile Icelandic krona with the euro seemed – and still seems – to be the remedy for EU proponents. Opponents do not accept this argument. Instead, they demand that the government take economic policy responsibility itself.

The almost 400,000 Icelanders are divided on the EU question. A majority of the population does not want to carelessly jeopardize the independence gained in 1944 – after 564 years of Danish rule – to merge into a union of states with 450 million people. The Atlantic island is internationally one of the absolute leaders in political stability and democratic development. Of Iceland's eight parties, six reject EU accession or are skeptical about it. Foreign Minister Katrin Gunnarsdottir is among the Euro-enthusiasts; her liberal Reform Party split from Iceland's dominant force, the conservative Independence Party, ten years ago because the latter had ended rapprochement with the EU. Prime Minister Frostadottir of the Social Democratic Alliance is more moderate. The 38-year-old is trying to convince her compatriots that a yes to negotiations on Aug. 29 is merely a trial balloon. Iceland wants to find out how much it can «get out of» the negotiations with the EU before the people definitively take a stand on the accession question, she says.

## Fisheries as a sticking point

As a member of the European Economic Area (EEA), the island nation enjoys the freedoms of the European single market, with which it conducts most of its trade; it also belongs to the Schengen Area. Icelandic legislation is about three-quarters adapted to EU law.

The major sticking points in the last negotiations between Reykjavik and Brussels were fisheries and agricultural policy, and they will be again if there is a yes vote on Saturday. Fishing was Iceland's most important economic sector for centuries and still contributes one-eighth to its economic output today. Iceland has no incentive to abandon its proven management system, bow to EU fisheries policy, and negotiate sustainable fishing, common quotas, and mutual access to waters in Brussels.

## Tense glances in Brussels and Norway

According to opinion polls, the referendum is likely to be extremely close, with a minimal lead for the yes side. The result on Saturday is eagerly awaited not only on the island itself but also in the EU. A desire for negotiations from the rich country, whose GDP per capita is among the highest in the world, could restart the enlargement process, which has stagnated for 13 years. An Icelandic yes would be a signal «for the growing attractiveness of the EU in a rapidly changing world,» said Marta Kos, the EU Commissioner for Enlargement, to Reuters.

According to Kos, accession talks could be concluded within one to two years. If the people then approve accession, the island nation could join the EU together with North Macedonia, observers say. An Icelandic no on Saturday, however, would play into the hands of EU critics.

Norway is also following the renewed EU discussion in Iceland with interest. If the EFTA member decides in favor of the EU, the Norwegians will remain the only Nordic country outside. Although the Norwegian people have already rejected a possible accession twice, neither the two largest parties – the social democratic Labor Party and the conservative Høyre – nor large parts of the economy have buried the EU dream. After the failed EU votes in 1972 and 1994, however, politicians are reluctant to ask the people about accession a third time, as long as EU skepticism prevails.
