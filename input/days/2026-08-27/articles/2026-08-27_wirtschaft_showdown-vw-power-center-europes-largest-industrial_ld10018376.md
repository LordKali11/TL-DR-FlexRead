# Showdown at VW: How the power center of Europe’s largest industrial group works

*CEO Oliver Blume's cost-cutting plan faces slim chances in politically charged supervisory board*

The fate of Europe’s largest industrial group could be decided at the beginning of September. Next week, VW CEO Oliver Blume will once again ask the supervisory board for approval of his «Future Plan».

Blume already promised investors at the beginning of the year a radical restructuring of his group to make the company competitive again. However, concrete implementation remains open. Measures such as an additional 50,000 job cuts or closing four plants in Germany, which media reports say are being examined by management, are controversial.

How the future of VW is being debated behind closed doors in the supervisory board is a closely guarded secret. The group does not even publicly announce when the decision-makers meet at the corporate headquarters in Wolfsburg. However, because many supervisory board members maintain ties to the media, the dates often leak to the public anyway.

One thing is certain: Blume faces an almost impossible task.

## Institutionalized state control

Every German public limited company has a supervisory board. However, in hardly any company is this body more powerful than at VW. There are historical reasons for this.

Volkswagen was state-controlled for decades. The company’s origins date back to the 1930s, when Adolf Hitler commissioned engineer Ferdinand Porsche to develop an affordable car for families. After the Second World War, the company was continued by the British military administration in the occupation zone, which then placed it under the trusteeship of the state of Lower Saxony.

State influence remained firmly entrenched even after privatization in 1960. The federal government and the state of Lower Saxony each acquired a 20% stake. They regulated their control rights with the «Law on the Transfer of Shares in Volkswagenwerk Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung into Private Hands.» This «VW Law» still applies today in a weakened form, even though the federal government has sold all its shares. It also guarantees trade unions rights that go far beyond the usual co-determination in other German corporations, as they had only agreed to Volkswagen's privatization under certain conditions.

Today, VW is a global group. But unlike other public limited companies, in which purely economic interests often take precedence in the supervisory board, the body at the Lower Saxony-based automaker is strongly influenced by local political considerations. In addition, unusual alliances often arise: some supervisory board members, who actually represent the capital side, argue on key issues in the same way as the employee side.

Instead of the usual twenty, the VW supervisory board currently has only nineteen members – which means that the employee representatives currently hold a majority of the votes. But even if Heiss were to fill the vacant seat at the beginning of September, employee representatives would remain influential.

## Clear power dynamics within the supervisory board

The «VW Law» stipulates that decisions on the «establishment and relocation of production facilities» require a two-thirds majority in the supervisory board, that is, fourteen out of twenty votes. This gives the employees a de facto veto over plant closures, which they consider a «relocation to nowhere.»

Since the two representatives of the state of Lower Saxony currently align with the trade unions, clear majorities of twelve to seven or twelve to eight emerge on many issues on the supervisory board when it is fully staffed.

This makes the starting position clear before the next meeting: Blume is unlikely to get significant measures such as site closures or further job cuts, which could reduce the group's production costs, through the supervisory board.

## Controversial attempts at circumvention

Legally, there are two scenarios in which the group's executive board, led by Oliver Blume, could circumvent the decision-making process in the supervisory board and instead present the austerity plans to the shareholders at an extraordinary general meeting.

Both paths have a catch. Under stock corporation law, they are considered far-reaching resolutions, which means that usually 75% of shareholders would have to approve them. At VW, this figure is even higher, thanks to the «VW Law.» More than 80% would have to agree. This means that the state of Lower Saxony, which owns 20%, has a blocking minority.

Minister President Lies and his deputy Hamburg have already indicated that they would not support such steps. They said: «Co-determination is not a competitive disadvantage, but an essential part of Volkswagen’s success story.» Anyone who wants to sustainably strengthen the group must not undermine employee rights, they added. The trade unions also announced protests should the VW executive board attempt to push through austerity packages without their consent.

## A CEO change would achieve little

Christian Strenger is a professor at the Corporate Governance Institute of the Frankfurt School and a founding member of the German Corporate Governance Code Commission. He says: «The VW Group can hardly implement the urgent cuts anymore.»

Strenger considers circumventions of the «VW Law» that would enable simpler solutions for the group’s executive board unrealistic, arguing they would fail due to the resistance of the state of Lower Saxony. Institutionally, the starting position is clear, Strenger says: «Solutions can only exist if the representatives of the employees and Lower Saxony support them in the supervisory board.» Based on the public statements of those representatives, he does not expect the supervisory board to make groundbreaking decisions at the meeting on Sept. 4.

This would further increase the pressure on Oliver Blume. Would he perhaps soon have to step down from his post? Strenger would not expect much from that, saying: «Even a new group CEO would very quickly encounter the same resistance.»

At VW, there is only one way to the austerity plan: The group's executive board must seek compromises within the existing power structure – even if time is running out.
