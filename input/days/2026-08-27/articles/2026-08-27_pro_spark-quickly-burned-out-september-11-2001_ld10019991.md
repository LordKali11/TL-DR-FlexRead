# A spark that quickly burned out: September 11, 2001 deepened the rift between Russia and the USA

*After September 11, 2001, there was a brief glimmer of hope for a new order in which Russia and America would work together to combat international terrorism. This proved to be an illusion and acted as a catalyst for the deepest rift since 1989.*

The telegram from Moscow arrived at noon on September 11, 2001, American local time, and appeared like a ray of hope on that grim day. Russian President Vladimir Putin was the first foreign head of state who sought to reach then-President George W. Bush by phone. When he got through the next day, he expressed his solidarity with Bush in the fight against international terrorism.

The world was surprised. Would the largest attack on the U.S. since Pearl Harbor achieve what had not been possible since 1989, namely, bringing Russia and America together? Was this to be the beginning of a new world order, focused on a common goal: combating international terrorism?

The main actors themselves recalled the joint Soviet-American fight against Hitler in World War II. This time, al-Qaeda, the Taliban, and the scattered Islamist offshoots were the common enemy. Putin already saw himself fighting this enemy in the North Caucasus. The second Chechen War, starting in 1999, drew much criticism in the West. Now, the then-young Kremlin leader hoped for more understanding.

## Unexpected move by Putin

That Europeans and NATO initially and unequivocally sided with America was only natural. Putin's move, however, came unexpectedly.

Hopes in Russia and the West for a completely new relationship after the fall of the Iron Curtain had not been fulfilled. Much of what some on both sides had dreamed of remained illusory. The West saw itself as the victor in the Cold War. The Central and Eastern European states freed from Soviet embrace and the former Soviet republics that had gained independence wanted to go their own way and looked westward, not eastward.

Russia wanted to remain a great power and be able to exert influence on the reordered continent. The clearer it became that this would not succeed, the more Russia's politicians and society mourned their past greatness.

At the same time, the U.S., under its newly empowered neoconservative leadership, tried to challenge established norms. Among other things, they questioned the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty, thereby unsettling the Kremlin.

## Russian approval for U.S. presence in Central Asia

The first meeting between Bush and Putin in the summer of 2001 in Slovenia had been positive. But it had not looked like an embrace between the two powers. After the September 11 attacks, Putin suddenly made the unthinkable possible: among other things, he offered overflight rights for military supply flights and the exchange of intelligence, and gave his consent for the U.S. to use military bases in Central Asia for the fight against the Taliban in Afghanistan.

This was a geopolitically remarkable and accommodating gesture: Central Asia, described by British geographer Halford Mackinder in the early 20th century as the «heartland» and «pivot» for controlling Eurasia, is claimed by Russia as part of its sphere of influence.

Putin had good reasons for his apparent change of heart. He seized the opportunity and probably tied two main hopes to it: He saw the chance to portray the Chechen War as part of the fight against international terrorism, thereby seeking recognition and acceptance in the West for his actions. At the same time, he speculated that, thanks to this common struggle, he would finally stand on an equal footing with the U.S. and be treated accordingly – as an equal great power.

His generous offer, however, met with resistance in the Russian general staff, in politics, and in society. The September 11 attacks had also shocked Russians. But there could still be no talk of sympathy for the Americans. Mistrust remained high. In the eyes of many, Putin had made concessions without firm quid pro quos. Their suspicions were reportedly confirmed.

## Divergent ideas, common ground eroded

Fundamentally, Putin's hope and the brief euphoria of global political observers were based on a mutual misunderstanding. America gladly accepted any support but also considered it self-evident given the immense scale of the catastrophe. Exploring Russian sensitivities and expectations seemed inappropriate for the situation. The U.S. saw it as equally beneficial for Russia and themselves to eradicate terrorist nests in Afghanistan and thereby free Russia's neighborhood from the Islamist threat.

At the same time, Bush and Putin did not understand the same thing by terrorism and the fight against it. Both perceived al-Qaeda and the Taliban as their adversaries. But the Palestinian Hamas, the Lebanese Hezbollah, and the Middle Eastern rivals Iraq and Iran were not terrorists or troublemakers for Russia. By broadly classifying Chechen resistance as international terrorism, Putin also diverted attention from his own failures in the North Caucasus.

Under these circumstances, expectations were not met. Russia felt a lack of appreciation for what it considered its far-reaching concessions. America's view of the successor state to the Soviet Union did not change significantly. This was demonstrated in the following months and years, and it confirmed the suspicions of Russian skeptics. Already in December 2001, Washington terminated the ABM Treaty. NATO's eastward expansion continued as planned. As America moved toward the Iraq War, the formation of fronts seemed as rigid as before September 11, 2001.

It became increasingly clear that the West and Russia pursued different interests. Russia accused America, in particular, of wanting to overthrow leaders it favored – in Serbia, Georgia, Ukraine. Conversely, observers noted, it was an illusion to expect that Putin would attach greater importance to the rule of law, freedom, and human rights in Russia. The disappointment over unfulfilled expectations, in retrospect, even became a catalyst for the deepening of the divide.

Vladimir Putin's infamous speech at the Munich Security Conference in February 2007 referred, in part, to developments triggered by the misunderstandings and fallacies of 2001. 9/11 continued to reverberate into the future: the rift of 2014 over the Maidan in Kyiv, the annexation of Crimea, the instigation of conflict in Donbass, and the rupture in 2022 with Putin's full-scale invasion of Ukraine were already laid out in 2001. Putin envisioned Europe's security differently than the U.S., and their unwavering adherence to NATO expansion and missile defense buildup strengthened him in his rearmament projects.

## Not merely a reaction to the West

Putin's alignment with Bush in September 2001 was a spark that quickly faded. The mutual dreams of common goals and overcoming differences quickly proved illusory. The ideas and interests in the West and the East were too different. Western reflections on the relationship between America, Russia, and Europe often portray Russia's subsequent policy as a mere reaction to Western decisions, as if Presidents Yeltsin and Putin had solely responded to the West and pursued no independent political line. That is an oversimplification.

Mistakes were made on both sides. A confrontation always requires at least two parties. But the belief that aspirations were aligned overshadowed the more complicated reality. After all that has happened since, this applies even more to the new attempts, initiated this time by American President Donald Trump, to find common ground with Russia and forge a partnership.

The path to the Ukraine war and the Ukraine war itself highlight the very different ideas of European security. For Europe, peaceful coexistence with Russia must be the goal. However, there will be no common Russian-European security policy with a Russia that is prepared to assert its great power claims, if necessary, by force. As 25 years ago, Trump and Putin threaten to fail because they cannot meet each other's expectations.
