*Cold starts in plug-in hybrids are well protected thanks to modern lubrication and pump technology. But there are pitfalls when using the internal combustion engine in a hybrid drive.*

**Reader question: I have heard that there is a risk to the internal combustion engine in plug-in hybrid cars. It automatically switches on when high power is demanded and then has to immediately develop power. Should I now buy a car with a diesel engine without a hybrid to avoid engine damage?**

The concern that internal combustion engines in plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) suffer damage from cold starts is technically largely unfounded. Today's manufacturers take precise precautions for this scenario: The internal combustion engine does not start without lubrication when there is a sudden demand for power. Electric oil pumps ensure the necessary oil pressure before or during its engagement with the drive.

Plug-in hybrids also use particularly low-viscosity engine oils with special lubricant film additives that immediately adhere to friction surfaces and minimize internal combustion engine wear.

## Old fuel a source of risk

That is only half the truth, however. The fuel in the tank ages faster in plug-in hybrids than in pure internal combustion vehicles because it is consumed less frequently. While driving almost exclusively electrically is particularly environmentally friendly and economical, fuel that remains in the tank for a long time can gum up or accumulate moisture. «This worsens cold-start and driving behavior and increases deposit formation in the fuel system during the longer warm-up phase of hybrid vehicle engines,» explains David Kaiser, Head of Research at lubricant manufacturer Liqui Moly.

In addition, very frequent and short uses of the internal combustion engine can prevent the engine oil from reaching its operating temperature. Unburnt fuel can thus get into the oil and impair its lubricating properties.

Another problem arises because hybrid cars combine two types of drive. If one of the two systems, electric or internal combustion, fails, the entire vehicle can come to a complete halt.

## Everyday use matters

Switching to a vehicle with a diesel engine without an electric drive is only worthwhile if the typical driving profile includes a high proportion of long-distance journeys. For short distances, a modern diesel suffers significantly more from particulate filter blockages than a plug-in hybrid.

Drivers who cover long distances with high power demands, for example, on highways, operate a plug-in hybrid car very inefficiently. In such cases, the internal combustion engine performs the primary propulsion work. However, it is sized smaller than in vehicles without electric assistance and reaches its performance limits faster. This results in high fuel consumption.

If buyers use a home charging station, a plug-in hybrid remains a reliable choice. However, a purely battery-electric vehicle (BEV) offers maximum simplicity in everyday use and maintenance.
