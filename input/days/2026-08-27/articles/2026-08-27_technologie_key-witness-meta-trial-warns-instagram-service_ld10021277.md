Arturo Béjar was one of the key figures in the wave of lawsuits against Instagram's parent company, Meta. From 2009 to 2015, he led the protection and integrity team at Facebook, which dealt with anti-bullying tools and child protection, among other things. He later returned as a consultant for Instagram, driven by his daughter's experiences with repeated sexual harassment on the platform.

Béjar's central importance in the lawsuits and investigations against Meta lay in his dual role as both an insider and someone personally affected. He served as a star witness in the lawsuit because he could prove that he had personally informed Meta's leadership, including Mark Zuckerberg, about the harmful effects of the platforms on young people.

**Arturo Béjar, Meta is ramping up child protection and is now paying American states up to $18 billion in damages. Are you satisfied?**

The settlement is an important milestone. But it won't make Instagram any safer for young people. So parents and authorities shouldn't mistake the agreement for an all-clear signal.

**What's still missing?**

At first glance, the proposed order obliges Meta to implement meaningful protective measures. But on closer inspection, it's questionable whether it actually demands anything substantially different from what Meta already claims to be doing. Experience has shown that Mark Zuckerberg's apologies are as meaningless as his assurances that he is trying his «best.» In the past, independent reviews have shown that while security features exist, they are ineffective due to their design and other characteristics.

In addition, the agreement apparently accepts Meta's own measurement of the harm caused. Yet Meta has repeatedly suppressed internal studies showing that its measurement systems underestimate the true extent of the harm by a factor of at least 100.

**What are you demanding specifically?**

We need external oversight of the effectiveness of these protective measures. This means there must be independent measurement of the harm young people actually experience. Ultimately, Meta must be held accountable for results, not just for its efforts. I hope the court can still amend the order before approving it. If not, the agreement threatens to simply cement much of the security theater Meta has engaged in for the last few years.

**Can you be specific: What measures are you calling for?**

Safety by design, meaning a product that puts the safety of young people at its core.

**What might that look like on a platform like Instagram?**

Instagram is a platform that connects young people with strangers. That's dangerous. We found that for some young people, 90% of unwanted sexual advances would not have occurred if Instagram didn't exist. Instagram is a service that invites strangers into our children's bedrooms at night. If you want to create a safe product for children, it has to be more about real connections. We need a product that treats strangers as strangers.

**That has largely been implemented in Instagram's teen accounts. What other changes are needed in addition?**

The platform must not encourage compulsive use. Young people shouldn't have an infinite scroll function, algorithms that gauge their interests or stories that disappear after a short time and make them feel they are missing out. Furthermore, there should be no popularity counters – no likes, no views, no streaks. Imagine you're 13 and you start at a new school. Every kid there has a badge on their chest showing how many people are looking at them. That puts enormous pressure on young people. They consider posting photos of themselves wearing fewer clothes to get more views and likes. Instagram is full of harmful incentives.

**Meta has now promised some of these measures: a two-hour time limit, a night mode, no beauty filters and no autoplay. Still, it's questionable whether this will help young people. After all, it has never been scientifically proven that social networks do more harm than good to the overall well-being of young people.**

A product like Instagram causes harm. I have spoken with more than 40 parents whose children took their own lives. On the platform, there is glorification of suicide, portrayals of eating disorders and misogyny. It doesn't have to be this way. We have to ask ourselves: How do we minimize the harm?

**Can the harm really be prevented? There will probably always be children who bully others, both online and offline.**

We have observed bullying for years, and it's true, you can't prevent it. But for many children, online bullying is even worse than what happens in the schoolyard. When children are alone with their smartphones and see hurtful content, they have no one to help them regulate their emotions. Meta's help functions are inadequate. Anyone who experiences bullying should go to a person they trust. Nevertheless, many cases of bullying would be less painful if the product were designed with children's safety at its core.

**What laws do you consider necessary?**

We are in a public health crisis. Companies must be forced to be more transparent. They need to issue regular reports on the harm their products cause. This works for financial figures, after all. They produce detailed reports on profit and revenue every three months. There is no reason not to produce similar, externally audited reports for user well-being.

**How realistic are your demands?**

I think we are moving in a good direction. Several countries are discussing a higher minimum age. Bans for young people are desirable because they solve the problem of social pressure: Children want to be on the platform because all their friends are there.

**But bans also have negative effects. And they take away a way for young people to express themselves and be part of our society.**

We could raise the minimum age until we know the products are safe for children. We have to understand these products. Social networks trigger dopamine kicks, similar to alcohol or cigarettes.

**However, there is no scientific consensus on the question of whether social networks cause addiction.**

Many children have absurd amounts of screen time. All that time online distances them from their friends and family members. I would say 45 minutes to an hour a day is enough. The way children relate to these products has to change.

**Are there platforms that have done a better job with child protection?**

I hope so. But I haven't had enough time to look at smaller platforms. I believe that technology can connect people. I've worked on that for 30 years of my life. But that requires platforms that are based on the principle of safety by design.

**How has your life changed since you came forward as a whistleblower in the fall of 2023?**

It changes everything: how I look at my life and what I have done. It also changes the behavior of the people I interact with.

**Do you feel guilty, or do you regret anything?**

The people I worked with directly at Meta had good intentions and an ethical compass. I have made my peace with my own work. At the same time, it's true that the company I worked for for a long time has caused harm. A part of me feels guilty. Today, I am working to improve the product.
