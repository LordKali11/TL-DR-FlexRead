*Moscow is testing the defense readiness of NATO and the EU using a variety of methods – and is even openly considering attacks. Europe remains poorly prepared for the diffuse threat emanating from Russia.*

The incident barely made headlines but could have had catastrophic consequences: On Aug. 20, a naval drone equipped with explosives was discovered off the coast of Romania – just a few hundred meters from the Neptun Deep offshore production platform, where hundreds of workers were located. The facility is scheduled to start operations in 2027 and make Romania the largest natural gas producer in the EU. F-16 fighter jets destroyed the unmanned vehicle before it could cause damage.

Had the naval drone in the Black Sea gone off course? Or was it rather about to carry out an attack on Neptun Deep? Regardless, Romanian President Nicusor Dan accused Russia of endangering his country's critical infrastructure and condemned the «intensification of irresponsible incidents.» European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen also spoke of an «escalating campaign of threats» by Russia.

Moscow has not yet commented on the episode. But it was by no means the only recent incident that presumably bears the Kremlin's signature. In recent weeks, Russia has increasingly challenged Europe both militarily and rhetorically.

## A Russian drone campaign

Romania, an EU and NATO member, appears to be a particular focus. In late July alone, Romanian fighter jets shot down three suspected Russian kamikaze drones within three days. In August, at least two more drones entered Romanian airspace.

Moldova also recorded two similar incidents in August. In late July, a missile struck Polish territory during a major Russian attack on Ukraine – according to Warsaw, it was a Kh-101 cruise missile. And on Aug. 20, the Defense Ministry in Helsinki reported a suspected violation of Finnish airspace by a Russian military aircraft.

In addition, there was the failed attack with explosive drones at Leipzig/Halle Airport, which targeted an Antonov cargo plane that was supposed to deliver armaments to Ukraine. On Wednesday, several media outlets reported that investigators had found traces of a total of four drones. Here, too, Moscow is suspected.

The accumulation of incidents is reminiscent of the fall of 2025, when several European states experienced airspace violations by Russian drones and aircraft, and dozens of small drones were sighted near airports or nuclear power plants. Russia now appears to be continuing and diversifying this strategy.

This approach is strategically calculated. In a recent study, the British think tank International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS) describes a «Russian drone campaign over Europe.» The IISS says this campaign aims, among other things, to test NATO's response times and decision-making processes, and to identify vulnerabilities around critical infrastructure. It also seeks to normalize airspace violations without crossing the threshold into open confrontation, the IISS adds. In short, Moscow can learn how truly prepared for defense the EU and NATO are.

## Threat of «semi-military» reaction

At the same time, Russia is also verbally escalating its threats. On Monday, British Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced that he would allow Ukraine to produce the British-French Scalp cruise missile on its own territory. Following this, Kremlin advisor and former Deputy Foreign Minister Andrei Fyodorov warned on the BBC that sooner or later there could be a «visible reaction from Russia» against Great Britain.

When asked, Fyodorov explained: «It could be a so-called technical reaction, perhaps a cyberattack from an unknown source, not at an official level, of course,» he said. However, he could not rule out a «semi-military» reaction. Kremlin spokesman Dmitri Peskov, for his part, warned after Burnham's announcement that the Russian army was already collecting information to be able to destroy missile production facilities.

Peskov did not mention whether this referred only to factories in Ukraine or also to European manufacturers. However, the Russian Ministry of Defense had already published a list of addresses of several European drone manufacturers in April. These were «potential targets for the Russian armed forces,» Dmitri Medvedev, Russia's former president and deputy chairman of the Security Council, declared.

Indeed, there have been repeated fires in arms factories in recent months, for example in March at LPP Holding in the Czech Republic or in August at the Bulgarian ammunition manufacturer Emco. On Tuesday, the Slovak police reported that they had prevented an attack on a drone factory and arrested three men. Although Russian involvement has not been proven in any of these incidents, the mere suspicion ultimately creates a sense of latent threat – and thus plays into Moscow's hands.

The Kremlin apparently hopes its actions will create tensions in the EU, unsettle the population, and ultimately undermine Europe's will to support Ukraine. But so far, the EU and NATO have reacted rather cautiously to the threat.

## «Not up to the threat»

After dozens of drones entered Polish airspace last fall, Poland had activated Article 4 of the NATO treaty, which provides for consultations among member states. Now, however, reactions are rather hesitant. NATO has not even commented on the naval drone incident in Romania. The alliance last issued a communiqué on Aug. 12, in which it condemned the airspace violations.

The other question is how well Europe is prepared for attacks. The EU adopted the «Defense Readiness Roadmap 2030» in October 2025, which aims to significantly improve European air and drone defense and border surveillance on the eastern flank. NATO, for its part, launched Operation «Eastern Sentry» to strengthen defense capabilities in the East.

But a year later, defense expert Ulrike Franke draws a mixed conclusion. She writes in an analysis that Europe is still far from effectively defending the eastern flank. She notes that due to the multitude of EU, NATO, and national projects, duplication and inefficiency threaten to undermine efforts. Franke also points out that Russian drones are still primarily shot down with fighter jets – which she says is the most costly option. And the experts from the IISS also maintain: «Europe's current architecture for defending against drones is not yet up to the threat.»
