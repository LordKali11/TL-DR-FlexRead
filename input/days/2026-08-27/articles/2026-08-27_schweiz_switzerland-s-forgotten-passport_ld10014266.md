# Switzerland's forgotten passport

*The Münstertal celebrates the 125th anniversary of the Umbrail Pass road. Its most formative period was from 1914 to 1918, when Switzerland defended its neutrality during the war. A hike with the man who preserves the military historical heritage.*

**Corina Gall, Münstertal** · Schweiz · 2026-08-27 · 1977 words · [ld.10014266](https://www.nzz.ch/schweiz/der-vergessene-pass-der-schweiz-ld.10014266)

The Umbrail is the highest navigable pass in Switzerland. From the small village of Santa Maria in the Münstertal, the pass road leads over narrow hairpin bends and switchbacks up to 8,200 feet (2,500 meters) above sea level. David Accola, from Canton Graubünden, knows every curve here. During a drive into the mountains in mid-July, he speaks like a mountain and tourist guide. He is 63 years old, early retired, tanned, with a three-day beard and hiking clothes.

The road is heavily trafficked in summer. Racing cyclists, motorcycle groups, and car travelers reach the legendary Passo dello Stelvio, the Stelvio Pass, Italy's highest mountain pass at 8,858 feet (2,700 meters) above sea level, which connects South Tyrol with Lombardy, via the Umbrail. At the top there are restaurants, hotels, and souvenir shops. Italo-pop blares from a convertible, and the smell of bratwurst, fries, and gasoline hangs in the air.

In the restaurants, David Accola greets the employees by their first names, cracks casual jokes and tells good village stories as if he had spent his entire life here. But Accola grew up in Flims and was a career officer in the Swiss Army for 35 years. He was a Colonel in the General Staff, and as Chief of Staff, he was responsible for the deployment of ground troops at the WEF in Davos for many years.

Accola is interested in the Umbrail Pass because of its unique military history. He is president of the Stelvio-Umbrail 14/18 association, which he founded in 2000 with friends from the Münstertal. Now the pass road over the Umbrail is 125 years old, the valley celebrated grandly in the summer, and Accola is in his element.

The Umbrail is Swiss history. In the First World War, from 1914 to 1918, it was the Swiss Army's first line of defense. The Austro-Hungarian Empire and the Kingdom of Italy fought each other in the adjacent high mountains with artillery, at over 9,800 feet (3,000 meters) above sea level, in rock, snow, and ice. The Swiss soldiers maintained the neutral border in the tri-border area.

On the peaks and ridges, the ruins of the Swiss, Austrian, and Italian troop camps still stand. Trenches trace wavy furrows in the earth, rusted barbed wire lies around. The relics from the mountain war remained unnoticed for decades. Until David Accola and his association colleagues rummaged through archives and soldiers' diaries. And turned the Münstertal into a destination for military tourists.

## A life for the army

The history of war has accompanied David Accola for half his life. In the mid-1990s, he wrote a study on mountain warfare at the military academy. His diploma thesis dealt with the «Significance of Neutrality in Border Areas in the Context of Conflicts.» He read about the events at the Umbrail, and like Alice in Wonderland, Accola metaphorically fell down a rabbit hole. The more he knew, the more interested he became.

The current Umbrail Pass road opened in 1901, and the First World War broke out in 1914. To prevent Italy from advancing into Austria via Switzerland, the army occupied the national border in August 1914. Switzerland sent soldiers to Santa Maria and later also to the Dreisprachenspitze, at 9,300 feet (2,843 meters) above sea level, directly on the border with Austria and Italy.

After Italy entered the war in spring 1915, the Swiss reinforced their presence. David Accola says that «the skirmishes between the Italian and Austrian artillery were so close that shell fragments landed on Swiss territory.» However, the Umbrail soldiers were proud that, compared to soldiers in other border sections, they had truly been in the war.

Accola treats himself to a Corretto Grappa upon arrival at the Stelvio Pass. After the effortless car ride to 8,858 feet (2,700 meters) above sea level, he says, it is important to first get used to the altitude. A little later, he stands at the Dreisprachenspitze and illustrates the national borders on the ground with his hiking poles. Back then, they formed a Y-shape, with the Swiss in the northern triangle, the Italian Valtellina to the west, and Austrian South Tyrol to the east. The adversaries had eye contact, Accola says; «occasionally they were within hand grenade throwing distance.»

The warring parties had an interest in the Swiss remaining, Accola says. The area on the border with Switzerland served as protection for them. To be hit there, he explained, the enemy would have had to shoot around the corner. Accola throws a stone over the hiking poles, for illustration. Once, the Swiss accommodation on the Dreisprachenspitze caught fire, he noted. «The Austrians rushed to put it out.»

## Diaries found in Vienna

The Stelvio-Umbrail 14/18 association was founded to make the traces of war accessible. Today there are 40 kilometers of hiking trails and 60 historical information boards. Accola drew the illustrations himself. A local artist created steel soldier figures, which now stand in the mountains. In Santa Maria, they have set up a museum, and Accola wrote the brochures for it. In winter, for more than 20 years, he has been building a relief of the terrain north and south of the Stelvio Pass. The shell is in the museum; it is 12 square meters large.

The association members did this voluntarily. But many of the founding members have passed away or are no longer able to actively help. The paths need to be maintained, the museum looked after, and the website updated, Accola says. «Friends and acquaintances help with the work in the field; without them, it wouldn't work,» he adds.

In addition, money must be raised. Around 30,000 Swiss francs have been invested this summer and last year, according to Accola. Most of the money comes from private donors, he states. He knows many of them from his active time in the army. He still benefits from his contacts in Bern. This summer, eight mountain specialists on a refresher course helped secure a challenging section of the path on Piz Umbrail with fixed ropes. «We are preserving a significant piece of Swiss army history, so Bern should also provide supportive assistance,» Accola says.

When the first Swiss troops were moved to the Umbrail Pass in 1915, the army did not yet have mountain infantry. «The soldiers first had to learn to ski,» Accola says. Or as the army called it: «moving forward on skis.»

In their diaries, the soldiers mainly report on the weather. The winters were long, with up to four meters of snow at minus 40 degrees, and the accommodations were barely insulated. They spent the summers chopping wood for heating, and in winter they cleared the chimneys of snow. «The alternatives were simple: freeze to death or suffocate,» Accola says.

Switzerland suffered few casualties; among the Austrians and Italians, there were significantly more. The cause of death was rarely a warlike event, but rather avalanches, frostbite, flu, lightning strikes, and falls into crevasses in the then still mighty glaciers.

Much of the information about life at the front comes from the commanders' diaries. Accola unearthed the Austrian reports in the State Archives in Vienna. The association also received diaries, photo albums, and letters from the soldiers' descendants. Accola himself is now an archive: years, front lines, curious anecdotes about love affairs in the soldiers' quarters – he knows and names them all.

## Cannons on the Ortler

When David Accola explains the mountains, the conceptually distant First World War suddenly seems very close. A few hundred meters from the Dreisprachenspitze, where the Swiss were positioned, stand the ruins of a large Austrian troop camp. The stone stove is still visible. Rusty cans lie around as if the Austrians had only withdrawn a few years ago.

*David Accola.* (NZZ)

Accola says he found a can near an Italian camp. He could still read what was inside: Portuguese sardines, he recounts. He once met Austria's last empress, Zita, in an ophthalmologist's waiting room, Accola recounts. She spent the last years of her life in Zizers in the Chur Rhine Valley. Accola describes such stories as insignificant, yet he has a joyful grin on his face when he tells them.

Accola is also the factual military expert when he says: «Due to my profession, I am primarily interested in conflicts from an operational, tactical, and combat engineering perspective.» Up in the mountains, he wears a baseball cap with a «Bleuet de France» pinned to it, a blue cornflower, the official symbol of France in remembrance of the victims of both World Wars. On his wrist, he wears a rubber band with the British commemorative symbol, the red poppy blossom. Accola bought the souvenirs during travels to military historical sites.

The front section at the Stelvio Pass was not decisive for the outcome of the First World War. But the way the warring parties fought each other in this inhospitable region was insane and surreal, Accola says. Back then, there were still mighty glaciers, and snow lay on the peaks year-round. «The area was hardly worth conquering for Italy,» Accola says. But for the Tyroleans, it was their homeland, which had to be defended with all their might.

The Austrians wanted to defend themselves in the valley; Hungarian reservists and 38,000 riflemen were called up. However, the Tyrolean riflemen wanted to secure their own farmsteads, which lay in front of the established valley barriers. The Tyroleans moved into the mountains with their weapons, and the Italians followed. For whoever is positioned higher has a better view of the enemy's positions in the valley. The Hungarian reservists saw snow for the first time in their lives.

An impressive episode occurred on the Trafoier Eiswand, a mountain on the then border between Austria and Italy, a few kilometers from the Swiss border. During the war, it was initially occupied by the Italians. In autumn 1917, the Austrians succeeded in driving the Alpini from the summit. The soldiers dug a tunnel through the steep ice wall, at more than 11,150 feet (3,400 meters) above sea level.

The Austrians also established the highest artillery position of the war on the nearby Ortler: involving Russian prisoners of war, they dragged several mountain cannons up to 12,800 feet (3,900 meters) above sea level. Accola raises his eyebrows when he tells this, as if he can hardly wait for the listener's astonishment.

## The key to the customs barrier

For the opening of the first three hiking trails, the association invited military delegations. Austrians, Italians, and Swiss. In mixed groups, uniformed but unarmed, they walked the trails. «The necessary approval from the Italian ministry took more time than the establishment of the hiking trails,» Accola says. Nothing moves quickly and pragmatically, he adds.

But Accola can inspire people with his ideas. When he talks about his successes, he grins proudly. The relics of the mountain war lie on Italian and Swiss soil. In Italy there is a national park, with an administration in Valtellina and one in South Tyrol, so Accola had to get the hiking trails approved twice.

The director from Valtellina requested a dinner in Bormio. «We only talked about the project for five minutes,» Accola says. The director only wanted to know who he was dealing with, Accola recalls. The gentlemen enjoyed a Braulio in Bormio, and the matter was settled. But Accola was running late after dinner. In 2000, there was still a customs barrier between Switzerland and Italy, and it closed at 10 p.m. Accola says mischievously: «The director reached into his pocket and gave me the key.»
