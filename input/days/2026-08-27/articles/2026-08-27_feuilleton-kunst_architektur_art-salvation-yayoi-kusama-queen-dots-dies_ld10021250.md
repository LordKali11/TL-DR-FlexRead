# Art her salvation: Yayoi Kusama, Queen of Dots, dies at 97

*Japanese star artist, world-famous for her giant pumpkins and polka dots. Her cheerfully colorful work shaped by lifelong fears and compulsions.*

**Kerstin Stremmel, Philipp Meier** · Feuilleton · 2026-08-27 · 952 words · [ld.10021250](https://www.nzz.ch/feuilleton/kunst_architektur/die-kunst-war-fuer-sie-lebensrettend-jetzt-ist-yayoi-kusama-die-koenigin-der-punkte-im-alter-von-97-jahren-gestorben-ld.10021250)

The dots are everywhere. Her polka-dotted art is currently flooding Europe. The last stop of Yayoi Kusama's major retrospective tour, which opened spectacularly last autumn at the Fondation Beyeler in Riehen near Basel, is the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam. There, the polka-dot show by Japan's most famous artist opens its doors on Sep. 11. Now, however, it serves as a farewell exhibition. Yayoi Kusama reportedly died on Aug. 14 at age 97, as was announced on her official website.

A long life, a hallucinatory artistic career: In the late 1950s, the then-27-year-old Japanese artist left her homeland. She found it too small, too feudalistic, too servile and too misogynistic. Kusama moved to New York, then the center of the global art scene. Before that, she had contacted the great American painter Georgia O’Keeffe, whose paintings she discovered in an art book. Kusama sent O'Keeffe some of her watercolors along with a letter. The contact lasted for many years.

After her art studies in Kyoto, Kusama was initially known primarily for her drawings. In America, she quickly explored other formats, covering giant canvases with uniform net patterns. In her «Infinity Nets» — images without a center — the eye finds no resting point. In the 1960s and 1970s, she began experimenting with textile extrusions, or «Soft Sculptures.» She covered objects or entire pieces of furniture with fabric bulges. In 1962, she exhibited her work «Couch Accumulation,» a sofa studded with phallic forms, alongside works by Andy Warhol and Claes Oldenburg in one of the first Pop Art exhibitions at the Green Gallery.

## More famous than Warhol

Success came quickly. In 1968, Kusama once said she had been more famous in New York than Andy Warhol. Without ever aligning herself with a particular art movement, Kusama staged happenings in the following years, including demonstrations against the Vietnam War, performances and body painting, where the naked bodies of her hippie friends were covered with dots. She always staged herself effectively as well. She had repeatedly covered her own naked body with entire patterns of dots. In this way, the bodies began to dissolve into a pixel grid. Polka dots, as these specks are called in English, became her signature stylistic device. She herself was known as the Polka-Dot Queen, Princess of Dots.

With this almost obsessive creative drive and the structural clarity of her works, Kusama became one of the most popular artists not only in Japan but on the international scene. She represented her home country at the Venice Biennale in 1993 with the «Mirror Room (Pumpkin).»

The motif appearing in it — a pumpkin — became her trademark. This is evidenced by numerous works in public spaces, such as the walk-in yellow giant pumpkin with black dots that greets art tourists on the Japanese island of Naoshima.

Kusama became known as a trailblazer not only between media and between East and West, but also between art and design. In 2012, the then-83-year-old artist designed a legendary collection for Louis Vuitton: leather goods, clothing, jewelry and watches were covered with her polka dots.

## Childhood hallucinations of dots

From 1977, Kusama lived voluntarily in a psychiatric hospital in Tokyo. Through her life-sustaining creative fervor, she found a way to make her hallucinations and visions the basis of her art. Her obsessions with sexuality and the tendency toward compulsive repetition, which characterize her fascinating work, had pursued her since childhood. Her mother reportedly incited her to spy on her father during his affairs with other women. Kusama described this in her autobiography, published in 2011.

Yayoi Kusama, born in 1929 into an old-established family, grew up in the countryside. Her parents ran a seed nursery. «I spent every day hidden under a table in the corner of our dark and crowded shop, making paper boxes for the seeds,» she later recalled about her childhood.

As a child, Kusama said she had been plagued by dot and pattern hallucinations. Regarding her penis sculptures, she once said: «The mere thought that a phallus could penetrate me is dreadful. That's why I create so many penises (...), so many until I am buried by their expressiveness. I call that obliteration.» Kusama's compulsion to cover the real world with dots and thus pulverize it is shaped by her «Philosophy of Self-Obliteration.»

«Artists usually don't talk about their complexes, but I make my complexes and fears the subject of my artistic work,» she once wrote. Kusama turned her fears into art. In one of her poems, she formulated her wish: «I hope to leave beautiful traces at the end of my life.» She succeeded in doing so with her manic yet poetically suggestive art of repetition.
