According to Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon's defense policy official, America can be relied upon in the Western Pacific. Addressing allies in the region, he said in the Philippines in early August: «We will neither boast nor moralize. We will continue to speak softly. But we will continue to carry the biggest stick in the world.»

## America's declining power in the Western Pacific

Colby alluded to Theodore Roosevelt's famous saying, which he used to describe his expansionist foreign policy in the early 20th century. With a combination of soft words and a big stick, one could go far, Roosevelt said years before his election as president.

However powerful Colby's reference to Roosevelt may seem, America's stick in the Western Pacific is increasingly shrinking to a dry twig. This is because President Donald Trump views regional allies as parasites who make themselves comfortable at the expense of the United States and neglect their own defense.

The latest example is Trump's broadside against joint maneuvers with South Korea. These were too expensive, Trump wrote. He added that they harmed his good relations with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. Shortly thereafter, the American military, caught off guard by the order, announced that the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise, which had already begun, would be cut by half.

Joint maneuvers with allies not only serve to strengthen combat power and refine coordination between armed forces. They also send a signal to the adversary: «See what awaits you if you dare to attack.» For example, the latest maneuver was also designed to counter tactics that North Korean troops learned in Russia's war in Ukraine. In the last two years, Pyongyang has sent thousands of soldiers to the front to support Moscow.

## Trump's disregard for deterrence

In the language of strategists, this is called deterrence. Colby is an expert on this. Before being appointed to the Pentagon, he wrote a book titled «Strategy of Deterrence – America's Defense in an Age of Great Power Conflict.» On almost 400 pages, it describes how China's rise to hegemon in the Western Pacific is to be prevented.

But little of that is visible. For their campaign against Iran, the U.S. has withdrawn military assets from the Western Pacific – for example, no American aircraft carrier has been present in the region for decades. Allies like Japan, pressured by China, must wait for ordered weapons like Tomahawk cruise missiles because stockpiles are empty due to the war with Iran. And when Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi was pressured by Beijing last year over a statement regarding Taiwan, there was not even rhetorical backing from Washington.

Trump is right that America's allies must do more for their own defense. But unlike many NATO member countries, South Korea has never relied on the so-called peace dividend after the Cold War. Trump's Defense Secretary Hegseth acknowledged this in a speech in May, when he described South Korea's contribution to the bilateral alliance as exemplary.

The fact that Trump's anger now targets Seoul confirms for all doubters that the U.S. cannot be relied upon. America's allies in the Western Pacific – besides South Korea, Japan, the Philippines, and Australia – are trying to support each other. At the same time, they have no choice but to come to terms with China. Without backing from America, the regional balance of power shifts to their disadvantage. China can exercise its power unhindered.
