# Europe's dangerous missile gap

*The West lacks interceptor missiles and long-range weapons for the war in Ukraine. The war against Iran exacerbates this deficit.*

It was the night of Aug. 4 of this year when Russia launched one of its most devastating air attacks of the entire war on Kyiv. 24 ballistic Iskander missiles and four Zircon hypersonic missiles flew toward the capital, accompanied by more than a hundred long-range drones. The Ukrainian air defense could not stop any of the 28 missiles because the interceptor missiles used against such weapons had become scarce.

The attack on Kyiv highlighted a problem that had long extended far beyond Ukraine. European states and the U.S. lack guided missiles with which they can defend themselves against rockets. Simply put, it is about finding and hitting a rapidly moving missile with an equally rapidly moving missile in the air within a few seconds.

Missile defense is among the most demanding products of the defense industry. The U.S. masters this technology, as do some European states, such as Germany, France and Norway. But their stocks are low, having dramatically shrunk in some cases. Hardly any Western country is willing to part with large quantities.

How could the West put itself, and especially Ukraine, in this situation? How can it be that Russia, in the fifth year of the war, is still superior to the Europeans in the ratio of ballistic offensive weapons to interceptor missiles?

The first part of the answer is quickly told. European governments invested too little in the military in the past. Interceptor missiles were procured only in small numbers. This led the industry, particularly in Europe, to dimension its production facilities and supply chains for small quantities.

The other part of the answer is more complicated. The production of a guided missile requires, among other things, solid rocket propellants, batteries, circuit boards, semiconductors, industrial gases and test stands. For some components, there is great dependence on foreign countries.

## Enormous dependencies on China

According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), around 90% of all circuit boards worldwide are manufactured in Asia, with a good 60% alone in plants of Taiwanese, Japanese, South Korean and American companies in China. Such circuit boards are among the numerous electronic components of modern guided missiles.

The authors also write that there are only a few suppliers worldwide for military-grade HTPB. HTPB is a binder for solid rocket propellants. The U.S. Department of Defense has since classified weapon-grade HTPB as a security-relevant material because it was previously procured from only one source.

But these are not all the dependencies and bottlenecks. A modern interceptor missile must detect a target moving at multiple times the speed of sound within a few seconds, calculate its trajectory and continuously correct its own course. This requires sensors, electronics and control technology. These components require rare earths, for which China is globally dominant.

Given these dependencies, it is likely to be difficult to do exactly what is needed in response to the shortage of interceptor missiles: increase production. But that is the plan. For example, the American defense contractor Lockheed Martin aims to increase the number of PAC-3 MSE missiles for Patriot from 620 the previous year to 2000 by 2030. The German missile manufacturer MBDA plans to commission a production line for PAC-2 GEM-T Patriot missiles this year, while Diehl from Überlingen is expanding the production of Iris-T guided missiles.

## Expansion of Russian missile production

There is a problem, however: the additional missiles will not arrive for several years, but the demand exists today. This is also because Russia has significantly expanded its missile production.

Already in Sept. of last year, the Washington think tank Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) reported that modern Chinese machinery had helped Russia triple the production of the ballistic Iskander-M between 2023 and 2024. At the same time, around 70% of Russian imports of ammonium perchlorate, an important component of solid rocket propellants, had come from China in 2024, it stated.

It is to be assumed that Russia has since further expanded the production of missiles and cruise missiles. The deputy head of Ukrainian military intelligence recently told Reuters that Russia's Iskander-M inventory stood at about 130 missiles. Russia alone had produced 65 new ones in July, he said.

These intelligence figures cannot be independently verified. However, a separate estimate by missile expert Fabian Hoffmann from the Norwegian Institute for Defence Studies points in the same direction. In July 2026, Reuters quoted him as saying that Russia probably produced around 600 to 800 ballistic missiles annually, mainly Iskander-M and Kinzhal.

The «Ukraine Air War Monitor» of the Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung reported for the previous year around 600 deployed Iskander-M and Kinzhal missiles, as well as about 1,300 cruise missiles. But Russia apparently does not fire its entire production. The «Ukraine Air War Monitor» assumes that Moscow is simultaneously holding back ballistic missiles and building up its stocks.

This explains the Ukrainian president's increasingly urgent pleas for interceptor missiles and, at the same time, the Europeans' reluctance.

## U.S. maneuvering into a bottleneck

Europeans can hardly rely on the U.S. here. The war against Iran has maneuvered the Americans themselves into a bottleneck. According to CSIS calculations, U.S. forces fired an estimated 1,060 to 1,430 Patriot interceptor missiles in the first 39 days of the war alone. The institute estimates the American pre-war stock of PAC-3 and PAC-3 MSE at around 2,330 units. The actual stocks are secret.

The Washington Post reported on Feb. 23 of this year that General Dan Caine, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the president's top military adviser, had warned Trump about the risks of an attack. The newspaper noted that his concerns had explicitly included the limited stocks of anti-aircraft missiles. Trump publicly presented Caine's stance differently and declared that his general considered a war against Iran easily winnable.

If, however, the military leadership warned Trump about scarce missile stocks, the question arises as to why the U.S. nevertheless started a war against an opponent whose central retaliatory weapon, besides drones, is primarily ballistic missiles. According to the CSIS, it could now take three years or more for the U.S. to reach pre-war stock levels again. This would primarily affect guided missiles for Patriot and Thaad, but also offensive weapons such as the Tomahawk cruise missile, it stated.

European Patriot production will also do little to change this in the short term. Germany, the Netherlands, Spain and other states have ordered more than 1,000 older-generation PAC-2 missiles. For this purpose, a new production line is being set up at the German guided missile manufacturer MBDA in cooperation with the American company Raytheon. It is scheduled to start operating at the end of this year.

The modern PAC-3 MSE, which are particularly needed for defending against sophisticated ballistic missiles, are not yet produced in Europe. The U.S. initially plans only a European maintenance center. At the NATO summit in Ankara in July, however, the U.S. government indicated that it was in principle open to external PAC-3 MSE production.

>
> **PAC-3 MSE:** An evolution of the PAC-3 with a larger range, higher intercept altitude and better maneuverability, especially against ballistic missiles.
>
> **PAC-2 GEM-T:** Larger, older Patriot missile with an explosive warhead instead of «hit-to-kill,» primarily against aircraft and cruise missiles, also against ballistic missiles.

However, Japan shows how difficult licensed production is. Mitsubishi produces PAC-3 missiles there but could not double its own production as planned. The reason is that Boeing could not supply enough of the seekers manufactured in Alabama.

## Tomahawks also missing now

The shortage of guided missiles is challenging enough in itself. But another problem is emerging. Germany wants to procure Tomahawks as quickly as possible to combat Russian missile systems deep in the hinterland. This could now take time, as the Americans first have to replenish their own stocks here as well.

The CSIS estimates that the U.S. fired more than 1,000 Tomahawks in the war against Iran, but refers to reports in American media. According to these reports, fewer than 200 Tomahawks had been produced annually recently. Even the now drastically increased American orders are, according to current plans, only scheduled for delivery from 2030 onwards. It is therefore uncertain when Germany will receive its Tomahawks.

The problem thus extends far beyond missing Patriot missiles. Already in 2017, American strategists warned against relying solely on intercepting incoming missiles during massive missile attacks. They argued that an effective defense must also include the possibility of interrupting the attack chain before launch.

## Europe's gap on the escalation ladder

Almost ten years later, Europe lacks both in sufficient measure. The European West possesses too few interceptor missiles to conduct a long Russian missile war, as currently in Ukraine, purely defensively. At the same time, Europeans lack long-range conventional weapons with which they can combat Russian launch sites, depots and other parts of the attack chain deep in the hinterland in large numbers.

This is more than a gap in arsenals. It is a gap on the escalation ladder. Because against an opponent like Russia, which has a large arsenal of ballistic missiles and also nuclear weapons, Europe lacks precisely those conventional options that lie between merely intercepting an attack and a significantly higher escalation level.
