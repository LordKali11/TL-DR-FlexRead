Leticia Wong and her partner sold books. Then they went to prison. Hong Kong police arrested the couple at the end of June. Authorities accused them of carrying «seditious works.» Both are now out on bail.

Leticia Wong was formerly a district councilor for the Civic Party, a party banned since 2023. She opened her bookstore, Hunter Bookstore, in 2020, a year after the mass protests against the Hong Kong government. The shelves contained books such as George Orwell's «Animal Farm,» as well as biographies of high-ranking Chinese party officials. At least the store is allowed to remain open for the time being.

It is not the only bookstore that has come under fire in recent weeks. Over the past 12 months, Hong Kong law enforcement authorities have arrested at least 11 booksellers. In July, authorities carted away boxes of books from the Have A Nice Stay bookstore. Shortly thereafter, the owners announced they had to close their bookstore due to «financial problems» and «unclear red lines.» A handful of journalists had opened the business in 2022.

A few days later, the 50-year-old Greenfield bookstore also closed. Two weeks earlier, police had arrested five employees. «Thank you for walking with us through wind and rain for over 50 years,» the owners wrote in a farewell message to their regular customers.

## Beijing's weapon: A law

The attacks against bookstores are just the latest step in the Hong Kong government's efforts to silence all critical voices in the former British Crown Colony. The National Security Law, introduced by Beijing in Hong Kong in 2020, grants authorities far-reaching powers.

What is «seditious»? A biography of Vaclav Havel? What is a «conspiracy with foreign forces»? If one accepts donations from a European aid organization? The wording of the individual paragraphs leaves law enforcement authorities much room for interpretation. And that is precisely what Beijing intends.

Shortly after the 2019 protests, authorities launched a campaign against the media based on the controversial law. Afterwards, the government turned its attention to non-governmental organizations. Last summer, one of the most important had to close. China Labor Bulletin (CLB) exposed abuses in Chinese factories, for example, when a company had not paid wages on time. CLB was founded by Han Dongfang. The wiry Chinese had already advocated for workers' rights in 1989 during the Tiananmen Square protests in Beijing.

Last Friday, a Hong Kong court again convicted two democracy activists. Their offense: They had organized commemorative events for the victims of the 1989 Tiananmen Square massacre in Beijing for years. The judges for the trial had been personally selected by Hong Kong Chief Executive John Lee.

Meanwhile, the Hong Kong government is even moving against churches. Authorities are pushing for churches to merge their charitable activities, such as those for disadvantaged workers, with those of the government. This would allow individual initiatives to be better controlled.

Hong Kong is well on its way to becoming, at least politically, a completely normal Chinese city. An organized opposition no longer exists in the former British colony anyway.

The «Hop Fat Cafe» is located on a small side street in the Mong Kok district. In this neighborhood in northern Hong Kong, the former journalists had opened their bookstore Have A Nice Stay, and Mong Kok was also one of the hotspots of the democracy protests in 2019.

## Beijing's «great mandate»

In public speeches, Hong Kong politicians always emphasize that the city stands out significantly from the rest of China and assure that this will remain so. In fact, they are in the process of turning the Special Administrative Region into a completely normal Chinese city. This is evident in many places. Recently, for example, European consulates and chambers of commerce had to submit a list of participants to the authorities for approval when planning events.

A European diplomat in Hong Kong recounts that at the event itself, men had stood in front of the halls and filmed the arriving guests. These are Beijing's methods in their purest form.

Foreign observers wonder what drives the local authorities in their aggressive approach. Several independent local observers say it is impossible that Beijing's representatives in Hong Kong directly instructed law enforcement officials on every single step against critics or a possible political opposition. Instead, a Chinese sociology professor in Hong Kong explains that there is only one «great mandate» from Beijing: A repeat of the 2019 protests must be avoided at all costs.

## The worst thing: Uncertainty

The fact that Beijing – consciously or unconsciously – draws no clear red lines explains the local authorities' crackdowns, such as those against bookstores. The most critical writings are certainly not found on bookstore shelves, but on the internet. But out of fear of making mistakes, local authorities prefer to do too much rather than too little. It is the classic Chinese motto: More is more.

Overall, a climate of fear and uncertainty prevails in the Hong Kong bureaucracy. When the head of Hong Kong's investment promotion agency, beads of sweat on his forehead, receives a foreign journalist for an interview, he nervously hands him a stack of information brochures. Finally, he answers every question with the statement that Hong Kong is a successful international financial center.

Another example: A cultural official absolutely does not want to decide whether to approve a harmless art event from a European country. Finally, after repeated probing by the applicant, it bursts out of her: she does not want to lose her job.

It would probably be easier for the local authorities if Beijing governed directly and gave clear instructions on every issue. Many foreign businesspeople wish for exactly this. «Then at least there is clarity» is a commonly heard phrase these days. The worst thing is uncertainty.

However, such direct rule would be problematic, as the principle of «one country, two systems» still officially applies to the Special Administrative Region.

## 200,000 Hong Kongers leave their home

The former editor-in-chief of a major Hong Kong daily newspaper says in an interview that Hong Kong has not yet found the right balance between internal security and development. Local authorities are still busy sweeping up the remnants of the 2019 protests, even after seven years, he adds.

The journalist says the «worst troublemakers» have long since left Hong Kong. Since 2019, around 200,000 Hong Kongers have turned their backs on the city.

## Hostility toward Chinese tourists

Mainland Chinese have arrived. They are truly taking over the city. Over the past five years, on average, more than 100,000 mainland Chinese have relocated to the former British colony each year. In addition, there are hordes of tourists. They are recognizable by the rolling suitcases they push through the skyscraper canyons of Central and Wan Chai. In the Tsim Sha Tsui district, they photograph themselves in front of the impressive skyscraper backdrop on the opposite side of the harbor.

Chinese tourists report in conversation about hostility and derogatory remarks from Hong Kongers. The cultural differences between a city that was a melting pot between East and West for more than a century and autocratic mainland China are apparently still great.

## The end of press freedom

But Hong Kong is gradually losing its soul. Hong Kong journalists were once known for their tenacity, but above all for their good contacts in the highest political circles of China. The Hong Kong government gave them the cold shoulder. Jimmy Lai, the founder of the China-critical newspaper «Apple Daily,» was sentenced to 20 years in prison in February. The government had already ordered his newspaper to cease operations in June 2021.

Also unforgettable are industry stars like Willy Lam, who, while working for the «South China Morning Post,» supplied the world for decades with exclusive, sometimes piquant news from the inner workings of the Chinese Communist Party. Over. Done. Lam has now lived in the U.S. for many years. Today, there are practically no independent newspapers, news portals, or radio stations left in Hong Kong.

One person who is nevertheless trying to carry on somehow is Ronson Chan. The journalist reported live from the mass protests against the Hong Kong government in 2019 for the news portal «Stand News.» Authorities closed the portal at the end of 2021.

Now Ronson Chan tries to explain what is still possible in Hong Kong media. When «Stand News» had to close, the journalist launched a YouTube channel. Twice a week, Chan interviews a prominent guest there – and tries to test the narrow limits. «We have to think about every word beforehand,» says the journalist.

Nevertheless, Ronson Chan cannot give up his passion. A few months ago, he invited the well-known LGBT and former democracy activist Jimmy Sham. Sham belonged to the famous Group 47 of Hong Kong civil rights activists and spent several months in prison last year.

But the conversation was a tightrope walk. Ronson Chan wanted to talk to Sham about the 2025 fire disaster. In the major fire in a high-rise residential complex at the end of November last year, 168 people lost their lives, partly because the responsible authorities had been negligent in fire protection. «But openly talking about the authorities' failures was not possible,» the journalist recounts. Chan says «one has to embed the criticism differently.» If the government says the bamboo scaffolding was responsible for the fire, Chan says it was not the bamboo scaffolding.

But that's all there is to the criticism. It's not much. But it fits into the new climate of a city that was once unique.
