# AI omnipresent in schools, but uncontrolled: «That achieves nothing, it’s even counterproductive,» says education researcher Stefan Wolter

*A new digitalization monitoring report shows that if teachers do not engage with artificial intelligence and take control, the learning success of students is at risk.*

How artificial intelligence (AI) affects the learning success of pupils, apprentices, and students is currently perhaps the most popular research subject for scientists. Hardly a day passes without a new study on the topic being published somewhere on the planet. Currently being hotly debated worldwide is a study from the U.S. that examined students' homework. The result: All participants received the full score of 100 for their work – except one, who only got 80 points. In the exam, this very student performed best. He was the only one who had written his work without the help of AI.

The danger AI poses: Independent thinking is delegated to a machine. In the most extreme case, it ceases entirely, as the prevailing idea is that AI is superior. Education researcher Stefan Wolter sees this somewhat differently. AI certainly has potential in educational institutions – but only if it is used correctly and purposefully, he argues.

In Switzerland, this is too rarely the case. This is demonstrated by the fifth iteration of the monitoring report on the «Digitalization of Education from the Perspective of Pupils and Students» by the Swiss Coordination Centre for Research in Education. Although digitalization in schools has been completed, artificial intelligence is being used incorrectly.

## Competence first, then AI

Wolter explains the optimal scenario as follows: Teachers would have to integrate AI into their lessons by showing their students what they should and should not do with it, he says. However, according to the investigation, this happens too rarely. On the contrary, if students use various tools, they do so on their own initiative, Wolter notes. Or, «even worse,» teachers tell them: Just do something with AI. «That naturally achieves nothing; in fact, it’s even counterproductive,» Wolter says.

However, productive interaction with AI – under the guidance of teachers – must first be learned, Wolter states, as teachers are not automatically up-to-date with the latest technology.

This carries the risk that students will rely on AI independently without guidance. Wolter says: «The challenge for teachers is to show students that AI not only does not replace their own learning, but that only their own skills increase the benefit of AI.» It is therefore crucial, he adds, that educators acquire better AI competencies as quickly as possible.

It can be doubted whether this message has already reached the Universities of Teacher Education (UTEs). Wolter gives an example. Recently, a profit-oriented spin-off of the University of Zurich launched a program that uses AI to correct students' essays. «Something like that should actually have been developed by the UTEs. They should be leaders in an area that so greatly influences their central task, the training of teachers.»

## Digitalization: Loss of motivation

This primarily affects schools from secondary level onwards, as pupils in primary schools hardly use AI yet – not even in their free time. It even shows that in the first six years of schooling, digital devices are used significantly less in class. However, as one gets older, almost 90% regularly use AI. Wolter says: «This can be useful, for example, for foreign language speakers: They use the tools for translations, as asking the teacher might be embarrassing.» However, this self-help can also be negative if one’s own learning falls by the wayside. «Therefore, it is an important task for schools to take the lead in the use of AI,» he adds.

However, it also appears that students themselves might wish for more supervision when using AI. While digital learning was initially received with euphoria, the «emotional return» is now flattening. Motivation and joy are increasingly absent. This is also an indication that thinking is more routinely outsourced to AI. Wolter says: «The monitoring shows that it is crucial for teachers to set the pace, as AI has already become entrenched in everyday school life without control, with potentially negative consequences for learning success.»
