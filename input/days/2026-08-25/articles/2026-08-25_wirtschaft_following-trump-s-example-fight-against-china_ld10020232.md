The scientist Jonathan Holslag is not a supporter of American President Donald Trump. Nevertheless, he believes Trump could serve as a role model for Europe on one point: the EU should consider imposing a basic tariff on Chinese goods and an additional levy on strategically important products. He argues that the problems China's highly subsidized industry creates for European companies can hardly be overcome otherwise. Tariffs against China: This discussion is gaining momentum in the EU, even though such a levy would burden companies that require Chinese preliminary products.

The 45-year-old Belgian Holslag follows the debate from two perspectives. From one perspective, until recently he was a professor of political science at the Free University of Brussels (VUB). He researched geoeconomic topics, including the relationship between China and Europe. Among other things, he wrote that competition leads to innovation. However, China distorts competition, which is why European companies sometimes lack the means for innovation. From another, Holslag has been the city president of Tienen, a Flemish city with 37,000 inhabitants, for almost two years. He experiences the global economy there in his daily life.

In Tienen, the chemical company Citribel has been producing citric acid since 1919. However, the company, which belonged to Roche from 1977 to 2003, is currently struggling. The reason is Beijing's industrial policy. In the Chinese province of Shandong, powerful manufacturers of citric acid have emerged over the past twenty years with state support.

These include companies such as Shandong Ensign, Taihe Biochemistry and Shandong Lemon Bio-Chemical. The companies primarily produce for export, as domestic demand for citric acid is comparatively low.

The companies from Shandong are putting severe pressure on Citribel. They reportedly offer prices that are 50 to 70% cheaper than those of the Belgian company. According to CEO Joris Merckx, Citribel has incurred losses in the past three years, and its plant with 250 employees is only operating at 50% capacity.

## Citric acid in shampoos and soft drinks

Citric acid is a typical chemical product: consumers usually only know it by name, but industry uses it frequently. Citric acid is found in cosmetics, shampoos, soft drinks and sweets. It provides a sweet-sour taste or lowers the pH value. Citribel produces citric acid from molasses. The viscous syrup is the residual waste product of a sugar factory in Tienen, which belongs to the German company Südzucker. Holslag says that the two factories are closely linked economically.

Citribel CEO Merckx actually wants to invest 65 million euros in the plant, among other things, to produce more sustainably. But the project is on hold – the competitive pressure from Chinese suppliers is too great.

Their strength is the result of high state subsidies. Western economists are convinced of this. According to the OECD, no sector in China received more state aid between 2010 and 2024 than the chemical industry, both in absolute figures and in relation to turnover. The global market volume for chemical products amounts to 4500 billion Swiss francs. China's companies have become the dominant supplier – at the expense of Europe and the USA.

Europe's chemical industry is bleeding, a manager from the chemical company Vynova recently told the publication «Politico» about the Chinese competition. However, that is only part of the truth. The continent's chemical industry would be less vulnerable if there were not also homegrown problems.

«In Europe, conditions for the chemical industry have continuously deteriorated relative to other regions,» says Henrik Meincke, chief economist at the German Chemical Industry Association (VCI). Bureaucracy is excessive, he says, and above all, gas and electricity have become massively more expensive since 2020.

## Dumping prices a concern for the EU since 2007

The EU has only recently begun to address bureaucracy, but China's allegedly unfair trade practices have occupied the organization for much longer. As early as 2007, the EU initiated an anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese citric acid imports. The EU concluded that Chinese export prices were below production costs. It therefore imposed tariffs on manufacturers from the Far East, which average 37%.

Since April, the EU has been investigating for the third time whether China's practices continue to justify import duties. Merckx said that the situation for European chemical producers had recently eased somewhat due to the Iran war, as Chinese products had become more expensive, partly due to higher transport costs. The Citribel CEO added that anti-dumping tariffs should still be 75% instead of 37%, especially since the USA demands a tariff of 155% on Chinese citric acid.

Europe's second manufacturer of citric acid, which also asked the EU for the investigation, is vague in its statements. This is the company Jungbunzlauer, based in Basel. «We believe that a competitive industry in Europe is essential for supply chains to remain resilient,» says department head Klairie Gounaridi.

The two companies will learn what tariff will ultimately apply at the earliest in spring 2027. Industry representatives complain that the EU's anti-dumping procedures take far too long. The dossiers are piling up in Brussels. Cases from the chemical sector, in particular, have recently increased sharply. «The EU should urgently hire more specialists to process the cases,» says foreign trade expert Matthias Blum from the VCI.

## China's move up the value chain

Surprisingly, economic data at first glance does not convey the impression that Europe's chemical industry is immediately on the brink. Germany, for example, is still projected to achieve a small trade surplus in the chemical business with China in 2025. However, Meincke from the VCI says that the pressure exists, even though this is not yet fully reflected in the data. The low prices of Chinese imports are squeezing the margins of European manufacturers, because they have to adjust prices downward.

According to Meincke, Germany's surplus also primarily comes from specialty chemicals. However, China is now particularly promoting these in order to manufacture more products with higher added value. From a Chinese perspective, this is necessary, as basic chemicals are not a business for companies there either.

But is Europe not simultaneously succeeding in developing innovative products, i.e., managing structural change? City President Holslag is skeptical.

He says that Tienen has repeatedly painfully experienced what is happening between Europe and China. In 2001, a factory for solar modules was founded in the city. It was a spin-off of the well-known research institution Imec.

However, the factory had to cease operations in 2012, as competition from China had become overwhelming. Meanwhile, there is no significant solar module industry left in Europe. China dominates the business.

Holslag says that such connections are not a big issue politically in Tienen. He does advocate for industry. However, he thinks that if he wants to be re-elected as city president in five years, it would benefit him more if he endeavored to attract affordable retail chains to Tienen. Consuming at good prices is a concern for the population. Interest in industry, on the other hand, has diminished – across Europe.
