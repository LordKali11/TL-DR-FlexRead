# Israel's bombing of Syria, targeting Turkey

Dan Schueftan cultivates a dry sense of humor. What happened in Syria last week was primarily a message to Turkey, says the Israeli security expert, who served as an advisor to several heads of government. In the Middle East, however, such messages are not sent with diplomats. «If you need an envoy, an F-16 or an F-35 is the better choice,» he says. That way, the message reaches the other side faster.

What Schueftan is referring to is the bombardment of Abu al-Duhur airfield. Israeli fighter jets attacked the military base in the Syrian province of Idlib last Tuesday. The runway and apparently storage buildings were hit. Immediate outrage ensued: Damascus called it an «act of aggression,» and Washington an «unnecessary escalation.»

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, in turn, explained his motives that evening. His country and Syria had agreed on a security status quo, according to a statement from his office. However, the Israelis regarded Damascus’s invitation to Ankara to station Turkish soldiers there as an imminent breach of that informal agreement.

## Dispute over the Mediterranean

Syria has thus definitively become the scene of the Israeli-Turkish power struggle. This had already been hinted at ten years ago. At that time, the Turkish army moved into northern Syria. Officially, the operation has protected the border from Islamist terrorists and Kurdish militias ever since. In Israel, however, this was also always seen as Turkish expansionism.

Especially as the two states repeatedly clash elsewhere. Recently, President Recep Tayyip Erdogan offered, for example, that Turkey could become part of a new security order in southern Lebanon. The UN force Unifil, which has monitored the border with Israel since 1978, will cease its work there at the end of this year. Turkish soldiers, he suggested, would then stand on Israel’s northern border.

In Gaza, too, Ankara had eagerly offered to participate in a peacekeeping force. Israel brusquely rejected the offer. Donald Trump, however, considered Turkish soldiers most suitable for disarming Hamas.

Most immediately, however, the two confront each other in the eastern Mediterranean. For years, Ankara has pursued the doctrine of the «Blue Homeland» there and claims maritime areas that Greece and Cyprus assert for themselves.

Turkey was therefore excluded from the Gas Forum, an energy alliance founded by Israel, Cyprus, Greece, and Egypt. Whoever dreams of restoring empires, Netanyahu said last December, would find that this would not happen.

## Erdogan's increasingly strident rhetoric

Erdogan, for his part, repeatedly lashed out in recent years. Shortly after the Hamas massacre of Oct. 7, 2023, he called Israel a «terrorist state.» Two years later, he wished that God would bring «destruction and misery» upon the country. Israel, he added at the beginning of June, was «a threat to all humanity.»

Schueftan identifies two motives behind the Turkish head of state’s actions and words. Firstly, he says, it is a strategic one. Israel, he adds, increasingly appeared to the Turkish leadership as an obstacle to Ankara’s ambition to expand its influence in the Middle East.

This neo-Ottoman thinking is deeply rooted in Erdogan’s government. At the beginning of June, Interior Minister Mustafa Ciftci said at an event of the ruling AKP that Turkey would one day «liberate» Jerusalem. «As before, these territories will be ours again,» Ciftci said. He added: «God willing, they will be under our rule again.»

In addition, Erdogan’s hostility also has ideological-Islamist reasons, Schueftan believes. Until 2010, the president had essentially presented himself as a reasonable European head of government, he notes.

Then came the incident concerning the «Mavi Marmara,» an activist ship that sought to break the Gaza blockade. During its storming by Israeli soldiers, ten Turkish activists were killed. «Then Erdogan’s deeply antisemitic element became increasingly prominent,» Schueftan says regarding the Turkish president’s increasingly drastic condemnations of the Jewish state.

Ultimately, Israel had learned an important lesson from the 2023 Hamas massacre, Schueftan says. The country, he adds, no longer waits for its opponents to attack but instead destroys their military buildup. This applies, he observes, to Gaza, Lebanon, and now also to Syria.

## Doubts about the government's approach

However, there are also voices in Israel that warn against the weaknesses of the new doctrine, not least with regard to Syria. When Bashar al-Assad’s regime collapsed at the end of 2024, Israeli troops moved into the buffer zone on the Golan Heights, a strip UN observers had monitored since 1974. Since then, they have occupied it and some areas behind it.

This brought little security, Danny Citrinowicz of the Institute for National Security Studies (INSS) recently wrote. He added that the longer it lasted, the more expensive it would become. Without a political way out, he contended, occupied land would eventually become a burden. Not only Damascus and Ankara, he noted, were annoyed about this, but also Washington and Riyadh.

His proposal, Citrinowicz wrote, was for Israel to return to the 1974 agreement with Syria. In return, Damascus, he stated, must accept restrictions, both for its own rearmament and for cooperation with Turkey. It must also prevent Iran, he added, from again supplying weapons to Hezbollah through Syria. Channels for this already existed, he pointed out, and senior Syrian officials were speaking directly with Israeli security personnel.

Citrinowicz also points to a second contradiction. Israel, he notes, is among the few states that want to weaken the new ruler Ahmed al-Sharaa. His past as an Islamist, he adds, gives cause for caution, but he must be judged by what he does. So far, his government, he observes, has sought no confrontation. If it were overthrown, he warns, Iran could return to Syria, and that would be the opposite of what Jerusalem wants to achieve.

## Is a new war looming?

Schueftan, meanwhile, remains skeptical about a restrained, compromise-oriented approach towards the neighborhood. The situation could certainly be improved, he says, as the peace agreement with Egypt showed. And yes, he concedes, such agreements are also possible with Syria and Lebanon in the future. However, he cautions, there will be no final solution. Schueftan explains: «In the Middle East, the rule is: If someone is not afraid of you, you must be afraid of them.»

This applies not least to the Turkish president, he adds. «Erdogan is a barbarian, and one can only deal with a barbarian in this way.»

Schueftan, however, does not anticipate a military confrontation between the two states. «I don’t believe that Erdogan or anyone else in Turkey is so dramatically irresponsible as to drive things towards war,» he says.
