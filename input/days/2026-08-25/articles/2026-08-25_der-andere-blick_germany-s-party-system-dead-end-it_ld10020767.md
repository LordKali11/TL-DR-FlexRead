*Germany's parliamentary system no longer adequately reflects the will of the voters. This harms the parties and the country. Alternatives to rigid coalitions should be considered.*

Germany's party system is in flux. The once-large parties are shrinking, once-small parties are growing, new players are emerging, and others are disappearing. This is a sign of democratic normality, even vitality. That is the good news. The bad news is: Germany's party system is at a dead end. This is because election results and concrete policy are increasingly diverging. The familiar coalition governments are therefore barely functioning.

This is evident, to the detriment of the parties involved, but above all to the country, in the current German government. Chancellor Friedrich Merz is, after just over a year, more unpopular than any of his predecessors. His government's approval ratings are at rock bottom. This stems from Merz’ broken election promises and his shortcomings. His flashy demeanor and the results clearly do not align.

Above all, it is obvious that the political shift demanded by voters cannot be implemented with the struggling Social Democrats. Yet, not everything is bad, as shown by migration policy and pension reform.

The problem is therefore bigger than Merz. Like his predecessor Olaf Scholz of the SPD, he is simply a transitional Chancellor. Since Merkel's departure in 2021, her successors have been searching for convincing legitimacy. The traffic light coalition under Scholz, in its distress, issued itself a modernization mandate that voters did not grant, but failed due to internal contradictions. The current government even styled itself as democracy's last resort before an AfD takeover. Such a claim is bound to fail.

What the «traffic light» coalition had to recognize before its expiration date, the black-red government still confronts: they are not models for the future, but rather obsolete concepts. The future will belong to other forms of parliamentary majority-building than accidental coalitions like the «traffic light» or grudgingly entered «grand» coalitions like the current one.

This has been apparent for some time.

## CDU's 2005 electoral setback

Former German Chancellor and CDU leader Angela Merkel is often described by her critics as the one who forced Germany's party system into this imbalance. Her critics say that before she took office in 2005, there were clear alternatives between center-left and center-right: black-yellow versus red-green. That, they contend, has long been over – to Germany's detriment.

These critics argue that the CDU's leftward shift, aimed at lulling Social Democratic voters into complacency – «asymmetric demobilization» – had destroyed a functioning system of democratically legitimate political change. Merkel, they claim, coolly sacrificed this for the party-political goal of preventing any government formation against the CDU.

That is true – and not true. For it was the German voters themselves who sent the party system into imbalance. The formation of the first grand coalition in 2005 was not because the CDU offered them a center-left option under Merkel's leadership.

On the contrary. As opposition leader, the later «refugee chancellor» sounded like AfD leader Alice Weidel on migration issues, though without her foaming at the mouth. Merkel also wanted to impose a market-liberal shock therapy on the country, a policy the CDU committed to at its Leipzig party conference in 2003.

However, Germans were not ready for the market-liberal shift in 2005. This was also evident in the fact that the Agenda reforms of her Social Democratic predecessor Gerhard Schröder gave rise to a new left-wing force. The East German party PDS soon became the all-German Left Party.

In view of this, Merkel indeed began her consequential center-left course. This, in turn, then created a representation gap that the AfD, founded in 2013, began to fill. The liberal professors' party led by Bernd Lucke, however, was only the beginning. What began as a corrective to the CDU gradually became a genuine right-wing party.

The firewall erected against it has since forced Germany's party system into cross-party alliances and thus into a politics of the lowest common denominator. This is ill-suited to a country undergoing structural change. Fragmentation coupled with a firewall cannot end well.

## Strengthening parliament

It would be better to rely on flexible majorities. This would shift the political center of gravity away from coalition committees and towards parliament. The government of the day would have to seek majorities on a case-by-case basis. The AfD would also need to be included.

Case-by-case majorities naturally also necessitate compromises. This would not preclude horse-trading, as in coalition negotiations. But political scientist Christian Stecker rightly points out that the political common ground in individual cases would be greater between changing partners than it is now, where two, and probably more partners in the future, have to agree on compromises from which neither they nor the country benefit.

That a system primarily geared towards preventing the AfD has no future is obvious. The established parties at the federal level already lack a parliamentary two-thirds majority to amend the constitution.

But instead of thinking about alternatives despite ingrained habits, the established parties prefer to drift on a melting ice floe towards an uncertain future. Things might go well for a few more legislative periods. But in the long run, it is highly unlikely.

The French are more flexible than the stability-loving Germans. They have repeatedly adapted the Republic to changing times in the past. Germans should at least consider new forms of majority-building. In any case, a political change in mentality is unavoidable. It must precede changes in practice.

The Netherlands and Sweden have been on this path for some time without collapsing. And in Germany's neighboring country Denmark, minority governments have been the rule since the Second World War, without the country descending into chaos.
