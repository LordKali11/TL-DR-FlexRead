In the U.S., another vehicle without a steering wheel or pedals will soon be on the road: Tesla plans to launch its new Cybercabs in Austin as early as August. The tech portal «The Information» recently reported this, citing Tesla employees. The Cybercabs are Tesla's first vehicles designed from the ground up as robotaxis: the two-seaters have no steering wheel and no pedals. Instead, passengers look at a large screen and have ample legroom. The vehicle drives autonomously.

The Cybercabs are Tesla's first self-driving cars without a steering wheel or pedals. They play a key role in CEO Elon Musk's plan for Tesla to take a dominant position in the robotaxi market. He sees autonomous driving and AI as central building blocks for the company's future. Musk first presented the new vehicles to the public about two years ago at a robotaxi event at Warner Film Studios in Los Angeles. Series production began in April.

Meanwhile, Tesla has already launched a robotaxi service in Florida and Texas, but it is based on the existing Tesla Model Y and the driver assistance program. The Cybercabs are the first robotaxis designed as such from the ground up.

Initially, the Cybercabs are intended to transport only company employees at the Austin headquarters, but soon also residents. Expansion into other cities is also planned. It is unknown whether Tesla has already received the license to charge for such a transport service. It is also unclear whether the new Cybercabs are meant to supplement or completely replace Tesla's existing robotaxi fleet in Austin.

## Tesla's remote control of vehicles in emergencies

Tesla has been operating a robotaxi service in Austin and other cities in Texas and Florida for several months. Both states regulate robotaxis very liberally. The vehicles in this service are all Model Ys, which cruise through the streets solely with Tesla's Autopilot software and without a driver. However, Tesla employees monitor the cars remotely and can take control in an emergency, like a remote-controlled toy car. For this purpose, the Model Ys were equipped with self-cleaning cameras, additional GPS sensors, and remote control technology.

Tesla also offers robotaxi services in San Francisco. However, the company still lacks the license for the cars to operate truly driverless there. This leads to the bizarre situation where a safety driver must always sit in the supposed robotaxi but is instructed not to talk to passengers, as several customers told the NZZ.

With the Cybercabs now introduced in Austin, remote operators can also control the vehicles from a distance in an emergency.

Tesla has apparently been testing the Cybercabs on Austin's streets for weeks: according to the website Robotaxi Tracker, 54 Cybercabs in Austin have completed 170 trips without human intervention in the past two weeks. Tesla itself publishes no information about its robotaxi fleet. Cybercabs have also been repeatedly seen on public roads in other cities in Florida and Texas for months.

## Market leader Waymo's nearly 4,000 robotaxis in the U.S.

Tesla is entering a market that, to date, is clearly dominated in the U.S. by Waymo, Alphabet's robotaxi division. Its robotaxis are already available for public use in eleven American cities. In Austin alone, the company operates 300 driverless vehicles. Across the entire U.S., there are almost 4,000 – up from just 700 a year and a half ago.

Especially in Waymo's home state of California, the service is actively used by residents in the San Francisco Bay Area and Los Angeles. The responsible traffic authority recently expanded the areas where Waymo vehicles are allowed to operate. This means that 25 million Californians can now use their robotaxis.

Unlike Tesla's Cybercabs, Waymo uses conventional vehicles from Jaguar and the Chinese manufacturer Zeekr and equips them with cameras and sensors. Pedals and steering wheels are still present. This approach is much cheaper than a vehicle designed from scratch.

Behind Waymo, Amazon's subsidiary Zoox is the second-largest player in the U.S. Similar to the Cybercabs, Zoox's vehicles are also designed as robotaxis through and through; they have neither a steering wheel nor pedals and not even windshield wipers. The company currently operates a free ride service for the general public only in Las Vegas; the NZZ tested it last fall.

In San Francisco, Zoox vehicles are already cruising the streets, but so far they are reserved for friends and family of company employees and selected test drivers. Zoox also has expansion plans: in Miami, Austin, Dallas, and Phoenix, the company is already mapping the road network with cameras and sensors so that robotaxis can navigate driverlessly in these cities too.

## Technological differences in Tesla's robotaxis

From a regulatory perspective, the requirements for robotaxis in the U.S. are loosening; President Donald Trump is known to be a big fan of the technology. For example, the American regulatory authority National Highway Traffic Safety Administration recently granted Zoox a license for a paid taxi service. In Las Vegas, Zoox will now charge for its services.

Technologically, the approaches of Tesla and Waymo and Zoox differ. Musk relies entirely on neural networks: his artificial intelligence driver assistance program (Full Self-Driving, FSD) was trained with video footage millions of Tesla drivers record daily in traffic.

Based on this database, Tesla's autonomous driving program attempts to mimic human behavior by evaluating real-time camera and sensor data using neural networks. In this way, Tesla's driver assistance program can also operate in areas that have not yet been meticulously mapped.

Zoox and Waymo, on the other hand, operate only in regions that have been precisely mapped in 3D beforehand using cameras, radars and lidar sensors (geofencing). On the road, the robotaxis constantly compare what they register in real time with the available map data. From this perspective, Tesla's approach is, first, cheaper per vehicle and, second, faster to scale – once the company successfully enters the market. Waymo currently maintains its market dominance with years of experience.

## Robotaxis and San Francisco traffic congestion

However, robotaxis have repeatedly made negative headlines in recent months. When a major power outage occurred in San Francisco in December and several traffic lights stopped working, the Waymo vehicles simply stopped at intersections and caused traffic chaos.

> [embed: youtube ]

At street festivals with many pedestrians, the vehicles repeatedly reach their limits, causing chaotic scenes. This was the case, for example, after the fireworks shows in San Francisco on the national holiday on July 4.

Philip Koopman, an emeritus professor of autonomous vehicles at Carnegie Mellon University, recently told the «New York Times» that the technology cannot cope with new situations. «And when the cars are in an unfamiliar situation, they tend to be overconfident and make spectacularly dumb failures,» he added.

Waymo has recalled all vehicles for a software update three times since December. Robotaxis apparently also have problems with parking spaces: Waymo vehicles in Austin alone have received parking tickets worth more than $9,000 since 2024, the «Wall Street Journal» found – partly because the cars parked in disabled parking spaces. According to Waymo, these fines are paid like those of any other road user.

To counter criticism of the existing problems, Waymo points out that its robotaxis are significantly safer than human drivers. In fact, an independent study by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety concluded that the vehicles are involved in fewer accidents per mile on average than humans.

With more than 50 million miles of driverless operation in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Austin, Waymo's accident involvement was 68% lower than that of human drivers, according to these figures.

Directly comparable figures for Tesla's previous robotaxis are not available, as statistics include vehicles with human safety drivers. It is clear: the bar for the Cybercabs is high.
