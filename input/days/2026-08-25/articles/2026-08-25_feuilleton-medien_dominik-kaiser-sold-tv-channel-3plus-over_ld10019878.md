# Dominik Kaiser sold his TV channel 3plus for over 100 million Swiss francs: «I first had to become aware of what wealth actually means.»

*As the founder of the 3plus Group, Dominik Kaiser became the king of Swiss private television. In this interview, he talks about how he made «The Bachelor» a success and how his life has changed since selling the company.*

With his Hawaiian shirt and relaxed manner, Dominik Kaiser seems more like a surfing instructor than a media entrepreneur. Twenty years ago, he launched his private station 3+ and, with shows like «The Bachelor» and «Bauer, ledig, sucht . . .,» succeeded where everyone before him had failed: running a profitable private television business in Switzerland. He sold his station group to CH Media seven years ago, after which he disappeared from the public eye. Now he is speaking out for the first time.

**When was the last time you watched television?**

I don’t know, it’s been quite a while.

**Does the medium no longer interest you?**

I’m no longer involved. If I watch TV, it’s time-shifted. Since I’m not interested in soccer, the World Cup wasn’t a live event for me either. Professionally, I’ve closed the chapter on television.

**You sold your station group to CH Media in 2019 for a reported price of around 150 million francs. Many people say that this was your greatest achievement.**

I’m not allowed to comment on the sale price. The basis for the sale price was the high ratings and economic success of the stations. What was done with the stations afterward had nothing to do with me. During the sale negotiations, I was asked for my assessment of the future. I said that I expected several more years of stable advertising revenue for private Swiss stations. That has more or less proven true – with the unforeseeable dip during Covid.

**Let’s talk about the beginnings. When you started the station 20 years ago, you were an unknown, and all the experts predicted a swift failure.**

In 2001, Switzerland’s two national private stations – TV3 from the media group Tamedia and Tele24, a station founded by Roger Schawinski – ceased operations. I saw that as an opportunity. I closely studied their viewership figures, spending on in-house productions and licenses, and advertising revenue. In doing so, I realized that their revenues were actually good, but their costs were too high. I became convinced that with a different approach, profitable private television was possible in Switzerland. After a lot of preparatory work, I also found a major investor, but he pulled out a few months before the planned launch.

**And then?**

I had to change plans and cut costs further, designing the programming so that I could finance a large part of it myself. The first major in-house production came a few months after the launch. In retrospect, the investor's withdrawal proved to be a stroke of luck. We quickly broke even and were then able to reinvest everything each year and learn a lot.

**In the beginning, you mainly ran cheap American series.**

The idea was to acquire the Swiss rights for American series that were already running very successfully on German private stations, for example «CSI.» We just aired them on a different day. Anyone who missed an episode on RTL could then watch it on our station. That worked brilliantly.

**But it was mainly your reality shows like «The Bachelor» or «Bauer, ledig, sucht . . .» that got people talking.**

The first real in-house production was «Superstar,» a music casting show. Shows like that were very popular at the time, like «Deutschland sucht den Superstar.»

**The show flopped, even though you managed to get Chris von Rohr as a judge.**

The show did okay, but it wasn't a big hit. We learned a lot from it.

**You soon became the king of Swiss trash TV, the bogeyman of all cultural pessimists. The SRG director general at the time, Roger de Weck, said: «I find ‹Bachelor› to be contemptuous of both women and men.»**

There were quite a few statements like that. It didn't bother me. Everyone is entitled to their opinion, that's the beauty of democracy.

**You’re laughing.**

Statements like Roger de Weck's were a badge of honor for us. We had a good laugh about all the critics. It was more difficult at the beginning, when all the experts said our business model was doomed to fail. That unsettled us a bit, even though we had calculated everything precisely. Our clients also began to have doubts. After the first big ratings successes, that was no longer an issue. We had fun with our work, the ratings were good and we were commercially successful – that was the most important thing.

**The shoots for «Bachelor» must have been a lot of fun.**

I was never there.

**You were never at the shoots for your most successful show?**

It wasn't necessary. I had a great team. It wouldn't have been any better if I had interfered. I also didn't have the time.

**But during the final review, you did make sure there were enough funny and embarrassing scenes, right?**

Of course, we had planned in detail what we wanted to do before filming. The storytelling is very important. And casting is crucial. That's why we built up a large, expensive casting department that scouted people like Vujo Gavric, who brought us top ratings as the Bachelor. The American version of «The Bachelor» is very serious. We deliberately turned it into more of a comedy format. The battle for love should always be a bit tongue-in-cheek on our shows. The rights holder, Warner Brothers, was not very happy with our approach. But when they saw how successful we were, our version became an international model.

**How did you come up with the format? The first globally successful reality TV format was «Big Brother,» then came «Dschungelcamp.» You opted for «The Bachelor.»**

We studied the viewership figures in countries where the format was already running and which are culturally similar to us. Of course, we also had our doubts about whether this strange situation, with 20 women vying for one guy, could work here. But when we visited the set of the American «Bachelorette,» we realized that this constellation triggers something in the human psyche.

**You sold your station group seven years ago. Why?**

That was a process. As CEO of the 3Plus Group, I worked an average of around 80 hours a week. And with great pleasure. About two years before the sale, I noticed I wasn't enjoying going to the office as much in the morning. Due to our strong growth, I had increasingly become a manager. In addition, Netflix and other streaming platforms were emerging at that time. It was clear to me that I would have had to rethink our business model in the long term, and we were working on that. I only realized how tired I was after the sale. Looking back, I have to say: I probably would have gotten sick if I had continued like that.

**You disappeared from the public eye after the sale. What have you been doing?**

I decided not to start anything new for a year. I allowed myself to make notes on new ideas, but no research. Gradually, I realized that it's great not to work for a while, but to read books, travel and discover other cultures. I love to learn and am very curious. So I started taking a lot of courses on strategy, economics, statistics, but especially on AI. At first online, because of Covid. I really enjoyed that. After that, I attended numerous other courses at top American universities. More than 30 in total.

**You never had enough?**

Learning has become a passion. I had never gone to university before and realized how much fun I have doing it. I also did a few consulting projects abroad, and eventually the desire to be an entrepreneur again slowly returned.

**In what area?**

I don't know yet. Probably something with entertainment again. I interviewed students in Korea, Taiwan and Japan to understand the culture and trends there. The U.S. used to be the pioneer in this area, but now I find Asia more exciting.

**What are your findings from the interviews?**

Two trends particularly fascinated me. In these countries, artificial intelligence is often used as a fortune teller. People want to know from the AI what the future holds, which job they should take, when to quit, whom they should date, even what dress they should wear to have a great day. Fortune telling is a huge topic.

**Are you getting into the fortune-telling business?**

Probably not, but I found it interesting because the topic is emotionally charged. There are already much bigger players working on AI as a life coach. For many people, ChatGPT and Claude are already their best friend, best advisor and therapist.

**And the second trend?**

That one is from Japan, pretty crazy. I came across a pronounced fan culture there among young women that goes much further than what I knew from Europe. They maintain a fictional relationship with a star. A photo of this star hangs on their backpack, and in a café, they place the photo on the table so they can have tea with him. At the women's request, the AI then creates romantic stories that these women experience together with their star. If the story is too tame, they type in «more spicy,» and the story becomes more titillating. But for most, the AI doesn't go far enough. That's not so far removed from «The Bachelor» . . .

**The challenge now is to develop a business model.**

I find that much more difficult than when I founded 3+. Back then, parameters like distribution, the advertising market and viewer behavior were quite clear and reasonably stable. Today, media and entertainment are changing rapidly. To found a company, you have to be able to see an approximate target of where the world will be in five years. What will AI be capable of then? Will distribution channels fragment even more? Which ones will be relevant? What will people's entertainment needs be? Pursuing that is very exciting, but finding answers is complex.

**Does classic television still have a future, apart from sports broadcasts?**

I think so, if it continues to evolve. It's still about launching hits and offering and marketing them on all possible channels. How you do that has changed. But a lot is the same as it used to be.

**With the sale of 3plus, you became a hundred-millionaire overnight. How did that change you?**

I'm not allowed to comment on my share either. I worked so much before that I didn't think much about my own money. Actually, I could have sold a small stake earlier and become wealthy. The idea didn't occur to me; I was too focused on 3+. When it happened, I first had to realize what wealth actually means. That was also a process. It's a huge privilege to be in a situation where you can do only what brings you joy or what you find interesting.

**You didn't buy a villa and a Ferrari?**

My interests are a bit different. I still live in the same place as before, and I still don't have a car. My luxury is being able to travel a lot and take courses at the best universities. I have the opportunity to learn from the best people — to pursue my unbridled curiosity. I read a lot, I swim a lot, I have great friends and I'm developing my next project.

> **He had his first company at 16** — Dominik Kaiser, 56, trained as a radio and television electrician. At 16, he founded his first company, which imported computers. Before launching the television channel 3plus in 2006, he was Managing Director of the music channel Viva-Plus in Germany and a board member of the Street Parade in Zurich. He also produced comedy programs for SRF (Swiss Radio and Television) with his company and ran a successful techno music label.
