# Must a municipal president foresee a natural disaster? A trial on alpine risk

*Graubünden Regional Court: Question of guilt after Bondo rockfall*

At the end, Anna Giacometti once again turned to the judge to answer the trial's central question about the Bondo deaths for herself: «I am not aware of any guilt,» she said, «and I hope you see it that way too.» Her voice had briefly faltered beforehand as she reported that the rockfall had left deep marks on her. «From one second to the next, the peaceful and quiet life in our valley was over,» she added.

On August 23, 2017, when she heard the noise of the rockslide, the then-mayor of Bregaglia set off for Bondo to evacuate people from their homes. This is what her lawyer told the court on Tuesday. By then, the massive rockfall that had broken away from Piz Cengalo had already buried eight people. They had been hiking through the Val Bondasca, on trails that were marked with warning signs but were not closed.

Since then, Anna Giacometti has been both praised and accused. She was called a «prudent crisis manager» after the disaster, sources said. Beyond Bondo, she gained great popularity and, two years later, a seat in the National Council. At the same time, she has faced accusations of multiple negligent homicides for nine years. She entered the courtroom in Pontresina on Monday through the back door.

Is Anna Giacometti, the then-mayor of Bregaglia, partly responsible for the eight deaths in Bondo – because she should have foreseen the landslide and therefore closed the hiking trails? That is the question the Maloja Regional Court must answer these days. It touches upon our understanding of a volunteer position at the lowest level of government and the inherent risks in the Alps.

On the morning of the second day of the trial, the giant screen above the judge shows Piz Cengalo. Snow-covered, in a seemingly majestic calm. The image looks both calming and admonishing at the same time. Below it, the accused in the courtroom look very small.

## The anti-specialist

Anna Giacometti's very biography is meant to demonstrate her innocence: because she simply cannot comprehend the power of geology. Her defense attorney recounts in detail her youth in the Bergell region, business school, consular service, and a fresh start as a small-scale farmer and citizen politician. Giacometti, therefore, was «absolutely forced» to rely on the recommendations of experts from the cantonal Office for Forests and Natural Hazards. What other purpose would such an office serve in Graubünden?

Giacometti herself stated in court: «I have not studied natural hazards or geology.» While she could indeed have had the hiking trails closed, she said, «you don’t just close a hiking trail out of the blue.» She had strictly adhered to the advice of the specialists, she added.

This is how she characterizes herself: as a municipal president, like many others in the mountain regions of Switzerland. A generalist who acts to the best of her knowledge and conscience, but whose knowledge does not reach into the complex structural geology of the Alps. An anti-specialist, as the honorary office requires.

The defender of the municipal forester of Bregaglia, who is also accused alongside Giacometti, also emphasized his client’s knowledge gaps. He argued that a forester is not a geology expert. His client had only completed a four-day course on natural hazard consulting for his municipality, the defender said. He was simply «not in a position» to assess the danger of an impending rockfall. Nature is generally difficult to calculate, the defender added, quoting mountaineer Reinhold Messner: «Mountains are neither fair nor unfair, they are just dangerous.»

## Specialists' appearance

If anyone could have foreseen this rockfall, the defenders of the municipal generalists argued, it was the specialists who are also in the dock in this trial: an external geologist who monitored Piz Cengalo at the time, as well as a geologist and a natural hazard specialist from the cantonal Office for Forest and Natural Hazards. One is a former mountain guide, another wears a fanny pack and Scarpa shoes. These are men who have made it their profession to gain power over nature. In court, however, they too, represented by their defenders, emphasized their helplessness.

His client lacks the authority to close the hiking trails in Val Bondasca, explains the lawyer for the cantonal geologist. In any case, the risk cannot be reduced to zero, «otherwise large parts of Graubünden would have to be declared uninhabitable.»

His client is a trained forestry engineer, explains the defense attorney for the natural hazards specialist. It was impossible for him to have foreseen the collapse of a mountain that had even calmed down in the days leading up to it, before sending out unmistakable warning signs just fifteen minutes before the catastrophe.

Memoranda and emails cited in the indictment reveal how the specialists consulted in the weeks leading up to the rockslide. Radar measurements showed unusual activity on Piz Cengalo, a reconnaissance flight by helicopter was conducted, and warnings and recommendations were issued to close the Val Bondasca, though these were postponed until a more detailed assessment on September 1st. A potential rockslide was expected «in the coming weeks and months,» the documents stated. But the mountain came crashing down on August 23rd.

## Residual risk

And so the most frequent word of the defenders is: residual risk. It is socially accepted, the defenders argued – because life in the mountains would otherwise not be possible at all. In fact, a conviction in this trial could overturn a Swiss certainty: that one must always expect uncertainty in the mountains. Hiking trails are only closed in rare cases, because otherwise it would be suggested that open hiking trails are safe.

One of the accused geologists, a former mountain guide, testified in court about the death of a close friend, a «cautious man,» who had died two weeks earlier on a simple hike in good weather. He had worked with natural hazards for many years, the geologist said, trying to identify dangers and minimize risks, but there was no such thing as zero risk.

## «No rockfall comes suddenly»

The prosecutor also said in court: «Absolute protection against all conceivable risks is neither possible nor necessary.» And yet, in his opinion, the accused should have closed Val Bondasca or recommended its closure, he said. He referred to an expert opinion that retrospectively explained what had not been seen beforehand.

«No landslide comes suddenly. They all send out warning signs,» the prosecutor quoted the legendary Swiss geologist Albert Heim, a member of the Swiss Alpine Club, who investigated the devastating landslides of the 19th century (Elm, Goldau). But a defense attorney later countered with the second part of Heim’s quote regarding the landslides: «…and in all cases, an increasing number of warning signs signifies the approach of catastrophe!» This, he argued, was precisely what had not happened at Piz Cengalo: an increasing number of warning signs.

In the course of the trial in Pontresina, the momentous rockfall from Piz Cengalo transforms from an event that humans should have been able to foresee with their measuring instruments into a fateful blow of nature.

Can anyone say which it was more?

In his 1932 essay, «Rockfall and Human Life,» Albert Heim fought against the fatalism of his time. «Instead of the previous dull watching, making excuses, and denying the danger, we want to create proper observation and insight,» he wrote. «We want to enter the age of knowledge.» Only to immediately qualify: «We can only oppose all-powerful nature to a small extent.»

The Maloja Regional Court must reach a definitive answer: Are there any perpetrators responsible for the deaths in Bondo, or not? The verdict is expected on September 4th. All defendants are presumed innocent.
