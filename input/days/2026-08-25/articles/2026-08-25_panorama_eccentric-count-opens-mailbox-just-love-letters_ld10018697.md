# An Eccentric Count Opens a Mailbox Just for Love Letters – Strangers From All Over the World Still Fill It Today

*The curious and – mostly – true story of Ángel Greses's poetic adventure in the Canary Islands: His daughter continues his legacy after his death.*

The seventh Count of Velhoco was a magnanimous ruler. He enjoyed inviting people to his home, ran a bar in the courtyard, and entertained visitors with stories of ghosts, crimes, and other mysteries of the house. He could not bring himself to throw customers out and sometimes did not even collect payment. Therefore, his relatives shut down the family business again after a short time. However, the Conde de Velhoco remained true to himself; he simply liked company, eccentricity, and spectacle. There was only one thing he truly disliked: bills and official letters.

One day, the Conde, whose given name was Ángel Greses, decided that these annoyances should end. He painted above the letter slot on the wall of his house on the Canary Island of La Palma: «Solo cartas de amor» (Spanish for «Only for love letters»). And indeed, people took him at his word. Neighbors, teenagers from the nearby school, tourists – they all began to drop love letters into his box.

The Conde died three years ago. At his funeral, the republic's anthem («Long live Velhoco, fertile land of equality») was played, and its flag with the star of freedom was hoisted. His ministers and friends sadly bid him farewell. Only the love letters continued to arrive.

The story sounds like a fairy tale, and in part, it is. The dynasty and the republic were entirely Ángel Greses's invention; Velhoco is, in reality, a district of Santa Cruz de La Palma with barely 500 inhabitants. The anthem was self-composed, the flag self-designed and commissioned from the local tailor, and the noble title self-bestowed.

However, the bar in his courtyard did exist. And above all, there was the mailbox and the letters.

## The Father's Secret Collection

Guadalupe Greses, his eldest daughter, has collected them in recent years. She and her partner travel a lot for their work. But her aunt, who still lives on the island, keeps the letters and then passes them on to her. Greses has scanned and edited them and published them as a book: Solo cartas de amor.

During the Zoom interview, she shows the thick folder; some letters fall out. There are some with drawings, one on a bar napkin, some in envelopes or as postcards. They are written in Spanish, but also in English, German, and French.

«They speak of longing, sadness, or simply the joy of writing,» says Guadalupe Greses. She cites, for example, a letter from Víctor, which metaphorically tells how a girl rises into the air and how he pursues her, even if she remains out of reach. People write to real and imaginary people, and sometimes it is simply about letting out feelings, she adds. The mailbox then serves as a psychologist, she says. «It means something different to everyone, and I particularly like this creative aspect,» she says. «The mailbox inspires people to write, and that is always worthwhile.»

Anyone who has a pen and paper in front of them enters a different mental state than when writing a text message or a WhatsApp message, Greses believes. «Ultimately, it is a craft,» she says. «Hopefully, it will never be lost.»

However, nothing contributed to the popularity of the love letter box as much as social networks. «In the beginning, the letters came mainly through word of mouth,» Greses recounts. «Or from curious tourists who dropped in a message because they saw it as a kind of ceremonial act at the end of their vacation.» But after her father's death, when Guadalupe Greses found his folder and published some letters on Instagram, the correspondence increased considerably.

Ángel Greses never replied to the letters and hardly commented on them within the family. His daughter only understood what they meant to him when she found many underlines and highlights in the letters. «It was always difficult for him to talk about deeper feelings,» his daughter said. «His extravagant sides were probably attempts to cover that up.» As is well known, the funniest clowns have sad souls.

## A Life Full of Imagination

The father worked as a lawyer in Valencia when his daughter and her brother were born. Even then, he began to embellish his tough professional life with his fantasy world, and after his return to the Canary Islands, he expanded the playful concept into a republican county. He appointed a notary friend from Barcelona as viscount. Ángel issued his own laws and wanted to have his own coins minted. On the one hand, it was all «nonsense,» as his daughter said. But on the other hand, it was a metaphorical rebellion against a society in which he felt a lack of laughter and love.

«I was certainly never bored,» his daughter said about her childhood. Of course, there were also moments when her brother and she would have preferred to sink into the ground: That typical Papa, no, no, not that . . . . We were both very shy and grew up with such an extroverted and sometimes quirky figure, she explained. When he held court, he often tried to involve them in his performances, she said: Come here, Lupita, sing. But I never sang; I hid, she recounted.

The bar in the house was called «El Portugués Café,» the Portuguese's café, because his grandfather had come from Madeira to La Palma. Ángel Greses saw it as a meeting place for the island's bohemians. The city's theater is directly across the street. The café was very popular with people, but from a business perspective, it was a «disaster,» as Guadalupe Greses says. Her aunt, who was also a nurse, and she herself during the summer holidays, were employed as staff. «I was 19 or 20, but you really have to understand something about gastronomy,» Guadalupe Greses said. No one did, and no one could sleep until the last guest had left. The bar only survived one season.

However, Ángel Greses's poetic view of life will always be remembered. And Guadalupe's gender revolution with her aunt, when the Count wanted to favor the younger brother in the line of succession. «What a macho! I wanted to be the eighth Countess of Velhoco and got terribly upset; there were constant discussions,» she says. Her father never acknowledged her claim during his lifetime, but after his death, Guadalupe Greses found a novel he had begun to write among his belongings. «In the foreword, he dedicated it to Guadalupe, the eighth Countess of Velhoco,» she says. «That deeply moved me,» she added.

Most of the letter writers also play along and address their letters to the Conde de Velhoco, just as it was written below the slot. His daughter reports that the letters come in roughly equal proportions from women and men, boys and girls. However, it often remains unclear who is writing, who is confessing their love to whom, and whether it is a true story. «The aesthetics of writing,» says Guadalupe Greses. «As in literature, there is often a mystery; when you read the letters, you doubt whether it is fiction or reality.»

This was the case with Víctor, who discovered his letter in the book and then contacted Guadalupe Greses. When they met in person, she expected him to tell her a life story of futile searches and disappointed love. But it was all completely different; the metaphor of the girl rising into the air was invented. «He said that he simply enjoys writing and that the letter was a creative exercise,» she says. Meanwhile, Víctor has also met another writer, and the two regularly exchange poems, Guadalupe Greses says. Via the mailbox in Velhoco.
