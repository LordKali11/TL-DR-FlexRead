# Kunsthaus: more cost-consciousness, less room-closing threats – city support still needed

*Museum performance compared to city cultural institutions: greater effort needed to break even*

Zurich aims for a leading role in art. The promises were grand when the Kunsthaus opened the monumental extension by renowned British architect David Chipperfield in 2021. The extension was expected to increase the museum's international appeal, enable collaborations with the world's best institutions, and offer more space for its valuable collections.

Five years later, the extension has indeed strengthened Zurich's art scene. The doubled exhibition space attracts more visitors, and is projected to record in 2025 the second-highest attendance figures in its history.

But the good news ends there. In recent years, the Kunsthaus has made headlines less for its exhibitions and more for the dispute over the Bührle Collection and its financial problems. Last year, the museum reported a deficit of 1.4 million Swiss francs. The preceding years were no better. Costs for the expanded operations were significantly underestimated, particularly for personnel. And in the coming years, building renovations costing millions are scheduled.

Two years ago, the Kunsthaus leadership, under director Ann Demeester and president of the Zürcher Kunstgesellschaft Philipp Hildebrand, raised the alarm, saying they were urgently dependent on higher subsidies. Otherwise, they warned, individual rooms might have to be closed and staff laid off.

In other words: the City of Zurich, as the largest financier, is expected to save the Kunsthaus. Voters will decide on Sept. 27 on an annual additional credit of 7.3 million Swiss francs. Of this, 4 million francs will go to the Zürcher Kunstgesellschaft as the museum's operator, and 3.3 million francs to the Zürcher Kunsthaus Foundation. As the owner and leaseholder of the properties, the foundation is responsible for their maintenance and preservation of value. The subsidies would thus amount to a total of 25.8 million Swiss francs – a considerable sum.

The city has already generously supported the museum in recent years. It paid 88 million Swiss francs for the Chipperfield building and approved higher subsidies. In addition, the City Council recently approved 3 million Swiss francs for another research project to examine the provenance of the works in the Bührle Collection.

## Addressing poor planning

One might rightly ask why public funds should once again provide a financial injection. Especially since the Kunsthaus is run by professionals: Economist Philipp Hildebrand is a former National Bank chief and now serves as Vice Chairman of asset manager Blackrock. Demeester is a cultural scholar and, before moving to Zurich, headed the Frans Hals Museum in Haarlem, Netherlands.

Nevertheless, it would be wrong to abandon the Kunsthaus due to recent miscalculations. Zurich wanted a museum with charisma and got one. Its transformation into a renovation case after the expansion is vexing. The increase in personnel, the biggest cost driver, is particularly notable. According to the annual report, the museum currently has 250 full-time positions, and 20 more positions are to be created by 2027.

Under pressure from the left, 1.8 million Swiss francs are earmarked for «improved working conditions.» In this context, it is not very helpful that the Kunsthaus asserts it is making efforts to save money, while its director receives a salary of 310,000 Swiss francs – more than the city president.

The Kunsthaus leadership must demonstrate greater cost awareness instead of threatening to close rooms. The poor planning, which dates back many years, must also be rectified.

Under this premise, it is right that the city supports the Kunsthaus with a higher contribution. In return, it must deliver. Only an attractive museum is a successful museum. The general public primarily attends blockbuster exhibitions. Last year, the show by performance artist Marina Abramovic attracted almost 120,000 visitors – it was the most successful exhibition in twelve years.

One thing is clear: the Kunsthaus cannot currently afford experiments. It relies on exhibitions that generate significant revenue. Cultural tourism can be a lucrative business. In Paris, people queue outside the Louvre to see the «Mona Lisa» once in their lives. In Zurich, treasures also frequently grace the walls. But big names cost money. To bring Abramovic to Zurich, the Kunsthaus paid 2.3 million Swiss francs.

Of course, it is not solely the city's task to get operations back on track. The Kunstgesellschaft's efforts are also required. For years, it has been looking for a third major sponsor – so far without success, even though Philipp Hildebrand has contacts in the highest economic circles. Nevertheless, the volume of donations increased last year.

Has the public-private partnership model behind the Kunsthaus failed? On the contrary. The Kunstgesellschaft raised almost half of the 200 million Swiss francs in costs for the extension from private donors. Without this commitment, the Kunsthaus would probably not exist in its current form – or the city would have had to finance it entirely alone and bear deficits alone. Even in ongoing operations, costs are split roughly fifty-fifty. This is rare in the cultural sector.

Nevertheless, such collaborations are unpopular in red-green Zurich. Recently, the left prevented the construction of an athletics hall because a private sports club would have had sovereignty over the facility.

The furor against public-private partnerships is surprising, because it has been shown, particularly with the Kunsthaus, that parliament can exert influence in such joint projects – though not always for the best. At the behest of the red-green majority, a research committee is examining the provenance of the Bührle Collection for the umpteenth time. The focus is on works that changed hands during the Nazi era. Since the extension's opening, it has been treated more like a scientific project than an art treasure. Currently, the Kunsthaus presents the permanent loan tightly packed in a small space.

Parliament also had a say in the higher subsidies. In the proposal, which will now go to a vote in September, Parliament demands that public funds be used carefully. The City Council must report on this to the council. The contributions to the Kunstgesellschaft are limited until 2033. In addition, Parliament stipulates that Zurich residents should be able to visit the exhibitions free of charge four times a year. Access to the collection is already free every Wednesday.

Free admission for the entire population in the canton is not very helpful in the fight against the deficit. But fundamentally, it is appropriate that the city sets conditions when it allocates so much public money. It does not do this everywhere. Each year, it distributes around 150 million Swiss francs in cultural subsidies.

By far the most money, 40 million Swiss francs, goes to the Schauspielhaus. Unlike the Kunsthaus, it can afford to be complacent when things go badly. This is because the funds flow indefinitely, even though the previous management alienated the audience with its approach. In the 2024/25 season, occupancy was only 53%. The city's contributions account for over 80% of operating income.

## Kunsthaus: greater efficiency needed

In comparison, the Kunsthaus operates significantly more successfully and requires fewer state funds. It can finance about half of its operations itself – through admissions, membership fees, its shop, and art sales. This is a good foundation. Now the Kunsthaus must become more efficient and commit even more strongly to attracting private patrons. By the end of 2027, management aims to present a balanced budget.

To achieve this, the Kunstgesellschaft has a responsibility. It benefits if the cost-sharing ratio between private parties and the state remains at 50/50. With higher subsidies, the red-green parliament will demand even more say. Where this leads has been seen with the Bührle Collection. Due to the ongoing back-and-forth concerning the collection, the Bührle Foundation is openly considering withdrawing the paintings. This would be fatal for the museum.

The expanded Kunsthaus deserves the city's support. The Chipperfield building has created an architectural gem. Just walking through the rooms is a pleasure. Finally, the institution can present its collections in a dignified setting and has more space and flexibility for large exhibitions. The Kunsthaus must be able to maintain its grandeur, even if it incurs costs – a pared-down version is not an alternative.
