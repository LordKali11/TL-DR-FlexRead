# How the shock of 9/11 made flying safer

*Since the 2001 terrorist attack, further attacks involving airplanes have been prevented. Various changes on the ground and on board contributed to this. The question, however, is whether this success will endure.*

Twenty-five years ago, more than half of the nineteen terrorists who hijacked four airplanes and used them as weapons were initially stopped at security checkpoints. However, after a brief secondary inspection, all were permitted to pass and board their flights, despite carrying items such as sharp box cutters.

How could this happen? This occurred despite the devastating findings of a 1990 investigation conducted by a U.S. presidential commission after the catastrophic bombing of Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland. Eleven years prior to the September 11, 2001 attacks, the commission concluded: «The security system of American civil aviation shows serious weaknesses and has failed to offer the necessary protection to the traveling public. It needs fundamental reform; rhetoric is no substitute for strong, decisive measures.»

Back then, much was considered normal that is difficult to fathom today: only about five percent (compared to one hundred percent today) of checked baggage was inspected for explosives. In the U.S., anyone could proceed to the gates after checking in at the checkpoint – even without a flight ticket. In Scandinavian countries, there were often no security checks at all on domestic flights. And pilots in the cockpit worldwide were separated from the cabin only by thin folding doors. These served primarily as privacy screens and often remained open during flights.

After September 11, 2001, significant changes occurred regarding aviation security. Aviation security, in English, refers to the prevention of interference with air traffic.

The list of measures from the last two decades is long, and the U.S. was consistently a pioneer: by 2003, all cockpit doors worldwide had to be massively reinforced and equipped with an access system. Additionally, the U.S. was the first to arm pilots, beginning in 2003. Following foiled attacks, such as that by the «shoe bomber,» a rule requiring the removal of shoes during screening was implemented in the U.S. from 2006.

## Flight security officers aboard flights

Flight security officers, the so-called Air Marshals of the newly formed Transportation Security Administration (TSA), soon began their service, from 2006, also on flights aboard American aircraft departing from Europe. In the same year, restrictions on liquids in carry-on luggage took effect, and in 2008, full screening of all checked baggage was introduced. Beginning in 2010, passengers were screened by full-body scanners, and since 2018, their carry-on luggage has been processed by new CT scanners. Typically, unpacking is no longer required. Liquids are also once again permitted in many locations.

«Technology-driven improvements such as CT scanners and better data exchange have proven much more effective than crude methods akin to security theater – such as the compulsion to remove shoes,» says Samuel Ramani, who advises the governments of Great Britain and the U.S. on geopolitical risks for a London-based think tank.

«Thanks to new CT scanners, the rule about removing shoes could be lifted,» Ramani says. He illustrates that smarter technology can replace outdated rituals – with the same or even higher security levels. However, he warns that terrorists are also constantly upgrading, for example with 3D-printed weapons or new chemical explosives.

«It will be crucial to understand technological developments early, continuously reassess risks, and accordingly further develop existing protection concepts,» Swiss said in response to an NZZ inquiry.

Increased security also meant significant adjustments for crews, as Thomas Steffen, airline captain and media spokesperson for the pilots' association Aeropers, confirms: «The most drastic rule was probably that the cockpit door must be closed whenever possible.» This complicated cooperation with the cabin crew and interaction with passengers, the Aeropers spokesperson adds.

Among Swiss airlines and pilots, there is consensus: «For security, the cockpit door rule and door reinforcement were the most important,» Steffen notes. He adds that developments in passenger and cargo screening also had an immense share in the improvement seen today.

Swiss also experienced significant changes. The significantly tightened requirements for identity verification and passenger screening (so-called «passenger vetting») had been particularly formative for previously valid security procedures, the Swiss airline stated. This also included the consistent securing of the cockpit and additional security requirements for flights to the U.S.

«The fact that an event like 9/11 has not been repeated in the past 25 years is an indication that the international security architecture works,» a Swiss spokesperson emphasizes. However, individual measures alone have not been decisive, but rather the entirety of the package of measures, he adds.

On some flights by Swiss airlines, flight security officers continue to accompany. «For us, Inflight Security Officers are a clear asset for air traffic security,» Thomas Steffen of the pilots' association Aeropers emphasizes. They have since become a normal part of aviation, he adds.

«The multifaceted measures after 9/11 demonstrably had a positive impact on security. In this area, civil aviation has made immense progress in the last 25 years,» Steffen summarizes.

The Swiss aviation security expert and historian Jonathan Zimmerli, who previously worked for both United Airlines and various Swiss authorities in the field, also says: «Personally, I feel very safe when I fly. The attention to fending off possible attacks on air traffic is very high today.»

## Danger not yet banished

The focus today is on new and future dangers. «Cyber threats are not just a future concern for aviation but are already intensely occupying it today,» Swiss explained. Artificial intelligence, the airline added, is likely to act in both directions: it can create new risks, but at the same time also offer new opportunities to recognize risks earlier and further improve security.

However, aviation remains a prominent target for external attacks, a Swiss spokesperson says. «Today we have a very comprehensive and multi-layered security system. At the same time, we must not rest on this success,» the spokesperson adds. It is important to recognize new threats early and continuously develop the security architecture, he notes. Passengers should report anything unusual, «when in doubt, it is better to report something too often than not often enough,» he advises.

The expert Jonathan Zimmerli also emphasizes that humans remain a decisive factor in preventing dangers: «Even with today's AI-supported security technologies, one must not forget the human element. One must also invest there and provide good training for employees, deploying them equipped with human insight.»
