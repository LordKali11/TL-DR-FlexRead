# Lucerne Festival: Rihm's Roar, Strauss's Storm, and the Magic of Quiet

*Three concerts featuring very different works push the boundaries of volume and silence at the KKL Lucerne. This balancing act offers a new perspective on the monumental «Alpine Symphony.»*

The Lucerne Festival recently developed a fondness for maximum contrasts. When Wolfgang Rihm's Poème dansé «Tutuguri» thundered across the KKL stage on just the third day of the summer program, audiences had to adjust their standards afterward. Enveloped, indeed downright overwhelmed, by dynamic discharges typically heard at techno or heavy metal concerts, they gained a new understanding of what loudness means. It was likely one of the first concerts in the festival's history where earplugs were made available to visitors as a precaution.

It's easy to debate the merits of such excesses. But even with hearing protection, the music, conceived by the wild young Rihm as a continuation of Stravinsky's «The Rite of Spring,» achieves its goal: all the archaic drumming, hammering, and thumping becomes physically palpable, vibrating walls, seats, and the floor, not just as an acoustic phenomenon. However, this equally extreme and fascinating sound experience, bordering on noise, was surpassed a week later by its exact opposite.

## At the Threshold of Hearing

The German-French composer Mark Andre, to whom several Festival Academy concerts are dedicated as part of a residency, explores the world of silence and ultra-low sounds, right up to the threshold of hearing. Merely rubbing one's palms together already counts as one of the louder sounds in this special cosmos. But like Rihm's boundary-pushing, Andre's delicate tonal language, threatened by every cough or rustle, also changes perception. Audiences involuntarily prick up their ears so as not to miss any of the incredibly subtly interlocking sound and noise elements – especially since it is often unclear which instruments produce them.

Andre's cycle «. . . im . . .,» which has been in creation since 2022 and is expanded this afternoon by the commissioned work «Im Entsiegeln 1» for soprano and orchestra, offers an excellent lesson in listening. For in every fragile gesture, this music – as conductor Baldur Brönnimann, singer Ilse Eerens, and the Lucerne Festival Contemporary Orchestra clearly demonstrate – is a radical counter-design to the real and digital din of our time.

Those who remained for the fifth appearance of the Lucerne Festival Orchestra (LFO) at the KKL afterward likely considered using the «Tutuguri» earplugs again. Richard Strauss's «Alpine Symphony,» performed here under the baton of Czech conductor Jakub Hrůša, is one of the loudest orchestral works of the 20th century. Normally.

## Glaring Light

Yet Hrůša, who is said to belong to the inner circle of candidates to succeed Riccardo Chailly at the helm of the LFO, seems to have been attuned to Mark Andre's works. Or perhaps, with senses so advantageously sharpened by that experience, audiences now hear this symphonically (and philosophically) elevated depiction of a mountain hike with different ears.

Hrůša, in any case, uses all proven conducting tricks to temper the piece's bombast. For example, he brightens the orchestral texture, precisely balances the individual groups, and does not allow the massive tutti of 120 players to blare uncontrollably, not even during the «Thunder and Storm» section.

He can rely on the outstanding wind soloists of the LFO, specifically first trumpeter Reinhold Friedrich and principal horn player Ivo Gass from the Tonhalle Orchestra. With fearlessly executed high notes, they lend the piece that icy, glaring light, making the majesty of the mountain world audible. Even for the musicians of the LFO, this is a breathtaking balancing act.
