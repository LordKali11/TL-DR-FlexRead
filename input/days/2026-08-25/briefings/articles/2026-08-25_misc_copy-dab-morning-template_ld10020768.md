by Oliver Maksan

Deputy Editor-in-Chief NZZ Germany

Germany's party system is in flux. The once-large parties are shrinking, once-small parties are growing, new players are emerging, and others are disappearing. This is a sign of democratic normality, even vitality. That is the good news. The bad news is: Germany's party system is at a dead end because election results and concrete policies are increasingly disconnected. As a result, the familiar coalition governments barely function anymore.

This is proving detrimental to the parties involved, but above all to the country, under the current German government. After just over a year, Chancellor Friedrich Merz is more unpopular than any of his predecessors . His government's approval ratings are abysmal. This is due to Merz's broken campaign promises and his shortcomings. His flamboyant public persona and the results clearly don't align.

Above all, it is obvious that the policy shift demanded by the voters cannot be implemented with the struggling SPD. However, not everything is bad, as migration policy and pension reform demonstrate.

The problem is therefore bigger than Merz. Like his predecessor Olaf Scholz of the SPD, he is simply a transitional chancellor. Since Merkel's departure in 2021, her successors have been searching for convincing legitimacy. The traffic light coalition under Scholz, in its desperation, granted itself a modernization mandate that the voters had not granted, but ultimately failed due to its internal contradictions. The current government even styled itself as the last bastion of democracy against a takeover by the AfD. Such a claim is doomed to failure.

What the «traffic light» coalition had to recognize before its expiration date, the black-red government still confronts: they are not models for the future, but rather obsolete concepts. The future will belong to other forms of parliamentary majority-building than accidental coalitions like the «traffic light» or grudgingly entered «grand» coalitions like the current one.

This has been apparent for some time.

Former German Chancellor and CDU leader Angela Merkel is often described by her critics as the one who forced Germany's party system into this imbalance. Before she took office in 2005, there were clear alternatives between the center-left and center-right: a black-yellow coalition versus a red-green one. That is long gone – to Germany's detriment.

These critics argue that the CDU's leftward shift, aimed at lulling Social Democratic voters into complacency – «asymmetric demobilization» – had destroyed a functioning system of democratically legitimate political change. Merkel, they claim, coolly sacrificed this for the party-political goal of preventing any government formation against the CDU.

That is true – and not true. For it was the German voters themselves who sent the party system into imbalance. The formation of the first grand coalition in 2005 was not because the CDU offered them a center-left option under Merkel's leadership.

On the contrary. As opposition leader, the future «refugee chancellor» sounded much like the AfD leader Alice Weidel on migration issues , though without resorting to her vitriolic rhetoric. Merkel also wanted to impose a radical free-market reform on the country, a commitment the CDU had made at its Leipzig party conference in 2003.

However, Germans were not ready for the market-liberal shift in 2005. This was also evident in the fact that the Agenda reforms of her Social Democratic predecessor Gerhard Schröder gave rise to a new left-wing force. The East German party PDS soon became the all-German Left Party.

In view of this, Merkel indeed began her consequential center-left course. This, in turn, then created a representation gap that the AfD, founded in 2013, began to fill. The liberal professors' party led by Bernd Lucke, however, was only the beginning. What began as a corrective to the CDU gradually became a genuine right-wing party.

The firewall erected against it has since forced Germany's party system into cross-party alliances and thus into a politics of the lowest common denominator. This is ill-suited to a country undergoing structural change. Fragmentation coupled with a firewall cannot end well.

It would be better to rely on flexible majorities. This would shift the political center of gravity away from coalition committees and into parliament. The respective government would have to seek majorities on a case-by-case basis. The AfD should be included.

Even occasional majorities naturally necessitate compromises. This doesn't preclude horse-trading, as seen in coalition negotiations. However, political scientist Christian Stecker rightly points out that the common ground between shifting partners would be greater in individual cases than it is now, where two, and likely more in the future, partners must agree on compromises from which neither they nor the country benefit.

That a system primarily geared towards preventing the AfD has no future is obvious. The established parties at the federal level already lack a parliamentary two-thirds majority to amend the constitution.

But instead of defying all conventions and considering alternatives, the establishment prefers to drift towards an uncertain future on a melting ice floe. It might work for a few legislative terms, but in the long run, it most likely won't.

The French are more flexible than the stability-loving Germans. They have repeatedly adapted the Republic to changing times in the past. Germans should at least consider new forms of majority-building. In any case, a political change in mentality is unavoidable. It must precede changes in practice.

The Netherlands and Sweden have been on this path for some time without collapsing. And in Germany's neighboring country Denmark, minority governments have been the rule since the Second World War, without the country descending into chaos.
