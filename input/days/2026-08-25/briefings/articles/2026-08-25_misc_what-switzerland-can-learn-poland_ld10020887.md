# What Switzerland can learn from Poland

**** ·  · 2026-08-25 · 1584 words · [ld.10020887](https://www.nzz.ch/ld.10020887)

Georg Häsler

Editor for global security policy

Almost every evening, a discussion panel somewhere in the country is currently debating Swiss neutrality. The campaign for a referendum on an initiative that aims to elevate a foreign policy instrument to a fundamental value and define it precisely in the Federal Constitution has begun. The decision will be made on September 27th.

These panelists are mostly in agreement: depending on their political leanings, they are either strictly for or strictly against the proposal. Nuance is rare, as are any truly opposing debates. Opponents and proponents seem to avoid each other. When a debate does take place, it's on social media – and then in a correspondingly hostile tone.

The way a vocal minority currently insults each other in online comment sections is something these same people would hardly ever talk to in person. Perhaps this is also why organizers of political discussions find it so difficult to get both sides to the table: because then real people meet, not digitally self-promoting figures.

This development gives me pause for thought.

The Swiss people's routine of debating substantive issues before referendums and then accepting the decision is actually part of our country's secret to success and contributes significantly to its resilience. Direct participation in public life is a safeguard against all forms of authoritarian tendencies – and a guarantee against the alienation of the population from state institutions, as can currently be observed in Western democracies.

The idea of ​​direct democracy also includes the militia system: the idea of ​​contributing to the protection of democracy. However, the will to serve has also suffered in Switzerland, even though 20,000 young Swiss men and women enter basic military training every year. My colleague Selina Berner reports on a study that analyzes the Polish approach to preparing the population for a potential war. You can find her article online or further down in this newsletter.

Today I also recommend the following from the «NZZ Pro» selection for your reading:

Even though the political noise sometimes causes headaches, civil debates still take place in our country. I experienced this firsthand as a panelist on Monday evening in Zurich. I discussed the issue with international law professor Urs Saxer and Stephan Rietiker, the president of Pro Switzerland – a staunch supporter of the neutrality initiative.

In fact, under Esther Girsberger's guidance, we had a civil discussion. Naturally, there were some rhetorical barbs, but they were based on a mutual interest in each other's arguments – and with respect for each other's experience and expertise. When the polemics subside, that's when things get really interesting. Then we actually have to engage with our political opponents.

Of course, I remain convinced that we must reject this initiative. But after Monday evening's conversation, I understand much better why someone would want to vote for enshrining neutrality in the Federal Constitution. That's called political culture: because grappling with the big questions ultimately strengthens the democratic core of Switzerland – as long as everyone participates.

Something else has occurred to me: If we engage with each other in discourse, then we also find common ground, the points on which we agree. Stephan Rietiker and I are convinced that we must tackle the modernization of the army now, even if there is hardly any time left. Because it is about protecting the delicate fabric of our democracy with military force.

This consensus also convinced the entire room.

Poland is one of those countries that lie exposed on NATO's eastern flank. The war in the neighboring country is part of everyday life here: Russian drones and military aircraft invade its airspace, targeted disinformation fuels political tensions, and the Kremlin doesn't even shy away from acts of sabotage against the railway network towards Ukraine.

Warsaw's answer to Russian aggression is large-scale rearmament. Measured by economic output, no other European country spends as much on its army as Poland: over four percent of its gross domestic product – more than the USA.

However, the rearmament drive is not limited to billions in loans for the army, as an investigation by the geopolitical and security think tank Swiss Institute for Global Affairs shows.

Poland understands security as a responsibility of society as a whole. The state and private sector are working together to strengthen the population's crisis awareness and individual resilience. For example, by next year the government aims to train around 400,000 citizens in basic defense and crisis response skills. Soldiers teach emergency preparedness and air raid procedures in schools. Young people can spend their holidays in basic military courses; since last year, a shooting course has been mandatory for high school students.

Civil society is also participating: NGOs educate seniors about hybrid threats and attempted fraud. Scouts are legally considered civil protection partners in a crisis. And the influential Catholic Church is developing an action plan for wartime with the Ministry of Interior and the Ministry of Defense.

However, the study states that for about ten years, Poland has had a military auxiliary force of trained civilians — a light infantry, anchored in the regions. It acts as a link between the people and the army.

In Switzerland, such measures probably sound absurd to many today. Marcel Hirsiger, an Eastern Europe expert and lecturer at the University of Applied Sciences Northwestern Switzerland, knows this. He is a study co-author and senior fellow at the Swiss Institute for Global Affairs. In Poland, there is a consensus about the Russian threat, while in Switzerland, this awareness is lacking – even though the neutral country has also fallen victim to cyberattacks and disinformation.

Switzerland has ideal conditions to make itself resilient as a whole society, Hirsiger says: «Bunkers and the militia principle are still firmly established in Switzerland today – Poland currently has to catch up on that.» Switzerland also has experience with civilian willingness to defend, he notes: During the Second World War, «spiritual national defense» shaped the neutral country. Today, most in politics, business, and society distance themselves from it; the concept is considered outdated. In any case, the small state considers itself a special case in relative security, the Eastern Europe expert observes. The study is intended to spark a discussion and show what a modern, comprehensive security architecture could look like, he adds.

In its first security policy strategy from the end of 2025, the Federal Council also speaks of «comprehensive security» as a goal for Switzerland. One measure to be taken is to strengthen the resilience of citizens, for example through increased information on the situation and emergency planning. However, the lead responsibility always lies with the federal departments, usually the Department of Defence. This is precisely where Hirsiger's criticism comes in: if security remains solely the responsibility of the state and the traditional security institutions, it will not reach many segments of society. Numerous companies, lulled into a false sense of security by the still-strong economy, believe they and their employees are «enormously vulnerable»—without even realizing it.

Poland shows that resilience only emerges from the interaction of the state, business, science, and civil society, Hirsiger says. This insight has matured despite political polarization. Defense Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz emphasized at the end of 2023 how central the civilian population would be in the event of war. As proof, he cited the example of Ukraine: Kyiv had not fallen in 2022 only because citizens offered determined resistance in the first weeks. According to Hirsiger, they maintained civilian logistics and ensured supplies.

But is Poland, as a NATO country in the East, really the right country for comparison with Switzerland? Hirsiger affirms this. While Switzerland does not have to fear Russian tanks, it does have to fear the Kremlin's hybrid warfare, Hirsiger says: Drones and cyberattacks do not care about national borders and reach Bern as well as Warsaw. «We are just as threatened as everyone else,» he says.

The new study proposes concrete steps: for example, an annual national «Resilience Day,» exercises in communities, crisis tests for business and authorities, and more cyber and media literacy in schools. The authors' conclusion: Poland serves as a source of inspiration, but the concept does not have to be copied one-to-one. Civil society, the militia system, and innovative strength were available in Switzerland and were also highly developed depending on the region, the authors conclude. They only needed to be strategically and proactively brought together.
