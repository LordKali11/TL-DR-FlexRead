Today's topics include new sanctions against Moscow and a budding Formula 1 driver from Zurich. But first, the commentary.

As a counterpoint to the all-too-prevalent faith in the state, even within the governing coalition, a voice of economic reason is urgently needed. This voice has been repeatedly and clearly heard from Economics Minister Katherina Reiche. Representatives of the business community therefore trust that the minister responsible for their affairs will take their concerns seriously, especially since this was less the case with Reiche's predecessor, Robert Habeck.

The minister thus plays an enormously important role. However, she jeopardizes it by repeatedly overstepping her bounds. Politics is fundamentally about compromises, and once these are negotiated, they should stand. The wealthy woman apparently only accepts this to a limited extent. This may be necessary in some cases. But anyone who behaves this way consistently risks being ousted from politics eventually.

A recent example of this is a letter sent by a state secretary from her ministry to a colleague in the Finance Ministry, headed by SPD leader Lars Klingbeil. In it, he criticizes above all the fact that the tax reform passed this Wednesday offers no relief. The main point of criticism is that the government is not reducing «bracket creep.» This phenomenon reduces the purchasing power of employees even when their wages rise in line with inflation.

In their coalition agreement, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and the Social Democratic Party (SPD) had agreed to relieve primarily small and medium incomes. There can be no talk of that now. That is why Reiche’s Ministry is right in substance.

The government's failure to provide relief, particularly for middle-income earners, is a serious oversight. Its attempt to conceal this failure is worthy of full criticism. However, when this criticism comes from within the government itself just one day before the vote, it is not an expression of integrity. It is a disruptive maneuver that creates nothing but unrest and reinforces the already existing doubts about the coalition's ability to function. The form and timing of this action therefore do more harm than good to the cause.

Back in April, the Economics Minister had already attacked her cabinet colleague Klingbeil so vehemently that Chancellor Merz reprimanded her. The fact that she is now launching another attack at such an inopportune moment raises the question of whether she simply lacks tact or whether she might even be deliberately provoking her own dismissal. Is she ultimately seeking to control the narrative surrounding a potential premature end to her term? After all, Reiche was also considered a candidate for departure when Merz reshuffled the cabinet in the summer.

Merz would hardly appoint another manager from industry as his successor. After Reiche's experience, the choice would likely fall on a reliable figure with extensive political experience, someone who integrates seamlessly into the coalition's discipline. And who doesn't disrupt the harmony even when it would be appropriate, not only in terms of policy but also timing. The liberal voice would fall silent. Katherina Reiche would then be missed. She would have only herself to blame.
