# Germany protests, and the Russian Finance Minister parleys in the U.S.: This is not how sanctions work

*Germany wants to hold Russia more accountable – and Donald Trump's administration is in the US talking with the Russian finance minister about cooperation in the international financial system. If sanctions are to be effective, they must be used more effectively and consistently.*

The signals do not align.

Kremlin ruler Vladimir Putin refuses to back down in the Ukraine war and is intensifying his provocations against European states. The German government has just concluded that the combat drone defused at Leipzig Airport most likely originated from Russia and its deployment points to the Russian secret services. Germany and its European allies want to show that they consider this unacceptable and are reacting – but not militarily. Berlin is expelling Russian diplomats and tightening sanctions. Former KGB spy Putin expresses outrage.

## Not just anyone

A few thousand kilometers to the west, Russian Finance Minister Anton Germanovich Siluanov is discussing risks in the international financial system and improving international cooperation at the G20 meeting of finance ministers and central bankers in Asheville, U.S. Siluanov is also meeting with U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who is concerned about financing the rising debt burden.

Siluanov is not just anyone. He has served continuously as Finance Minister and a member of Russia’s Security Council since 2011. When he sought to join a G20 meeting via video in April 2022, just over two months after the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the European and American representatives left the room in protest.

The U.S. had shortly before imposed personal sanctions against Siluanov, which are still in effect today. In its justification, the U.S. described Siluanov as a person who is part of the inner political leadership and central to financing Russia’s war against Ukraine. His assets are to be frozen, and American individuals and financial institutions are strictly prohibited from entering into direct or indirect business relationships with Siluanov.

Four years later, the Trump administration no longer seems to care much about that. Siluanov may not be a hardliner of the Russian regime, which is why the Europeans and Switzerland, interestingly, unlike the U.S., have not imposed personal sanctions against him. But he is undoubtedly a central figure in financing the Russian state.

## Sanctions only partially effective

The personal sanctions against leading figures of the regime were intended to make them oppose Putin’s war. The financial sanctions were designed to make it permanently impossible for Russia to finance its war by cutting the country off from the international financial system.

Sanctions are a tool that are meant to cause economic pain to deter the opponent and prevent a (further) military conflict. Unfortunately, experience with the Ukraine sanctions against Russia shows that they are less effective than hoped.

In Putin’s Russia, repression has grown so brutal that open resistance is barely an option. Making sanctions increasingly broad and thereby primarily punishing those Russians who are critical of Putin’s dictatorship and harbor sympathies for Europe probably even has a counterproductive effect.

More effective are sanctions that make the continuation and financing of the war more difficult. But the problem is that important parts of the world have not joined these efforts and they are only inadequately implemented. Moreover, their effect decreases with increasing duration because the affected economies decouple.

## Unmistakable signals needed

It would therefore be all the more important now to send unmistakable signals to the warlord in the Kremlin: The NATO countries, including the U.S., want an end to Russia’s war against independent Ukraine, which violates international law. And they collectively defend themselves against Russian provocations. To prevent a continuation and expansion of the war, they must drive up the price Putin pays for it as high as possible. Making it difficult to finance the war machine is among the most peaceful of all available means.

Europe’s maneuvering over deliveries of Russian liquefied natural gas is as much part of the problem and not the solution as Donald Trump’s constant advances toward his «friend» in the Kremlin. Demanding more support from Europeans in the Iran war and at the same time seeking deals with a Russian government that openly supports Iran seems absurd.

Bessent reportedly told Siluanov in a bilateral discussion that active cooperation would only be possible again after the end of the Ukraine war. That is correct. But the American government would do better to finally focus on a strict implementation of the sanctions aimed at financing this senseless war. Instead of discussing cooperation in the U.S., it would be necessary to take unmistakable action against the Russian shadow fleet.

Europe and the U.S. want peaceful international cooperation with Russia, just as with China – but only after Putin’s regime has agreed to a ceasefire and later peace with Ukraine. But this way, sanctions become a parody.
