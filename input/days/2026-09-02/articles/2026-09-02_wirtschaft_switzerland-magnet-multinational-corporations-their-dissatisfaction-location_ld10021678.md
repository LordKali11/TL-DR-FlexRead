# Switzerland is a magnet for multinational corporations: Their dissatisfaction with the location is growing

*A new report by McKinsey and Swiss-American Chamber of Commerce (Swiss Amcham) identifies the biggest dangers for Switzerland as a business location. The federal government's new chief economist explains why one point in particular worries him.*

Few myths persist as stubbornly as that of the boiling frog. Reportedly, a frog remains in water if it is heated slowly enough – until it is too late. Biologically, this is nonsense. Frogs do indeed jump out of the water if it is gradually warmed.

Nevertheless, the story has become a metaphor for creeping decline. And the metaphor is equally apt for Switzerland as a business location.

For decades, the country has succeeded like almost no other in attracting and retaining large, multinational corporations. According to the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (Seco), they employ over 1.5 million people, contribute over 40% to economic output, grow faster than domestic companies, and pay more than 50% of corporate taxes at the federal level. They are called Glencore, Nestlé, UBS, Roche, Novartis, IBM, Johnson & Johnson, or Alphabet. Some have been based here for over a hundred years, while others have only recently established themselves.

Switzerland and its multinational corporations are a success story. But how fleeting is the Helvetic economic miracle? A new study by McKinsey Switzerland and the Swiss-American Chamber of Commerce (Swiss Amcham) suggests that Switzerland is slowly losing ground in several key location factors.

The consulting firm and the Chamber of Commerce collected data on the relocation activity of 15,000 global corporations with at least 1 billion Swiss francs in revenue. They counted how many corporations established new operations or relocated existing headquarters to Switzerland and the most important European competitor locations – Ireland, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, and Great Britain (excluding departures). In addition, they surveyed more than sixty CEOs and executives of international companies in Switzerland on the development of important location factors.

Three shifts stand out – and give cause for concern.

## Development 1: Switzerland's share of new establishments: Stable, but shrinking in absolute numbers

At first glance, the situation appears unchanged: Switzerland managed to maintain its share of relocations and new establishments of large multinational corporations in the five countries mentioned above between 2020 and 2025. This share stands at about 20%, placing it third, after Ireland and the Netherlands.

But the pie has shrunk. Between 2014 and 2019, 179 companies still shifted their headquarters to Europe within the surveyed countries. Between 2020 and 2025, that figure dropped to 142. Proportionally, Switzerland is as successful as ever. Absolutely, the situation looks worse.

## Development 2: Research centers instead of headquarters

The type of establishments is also changing. Significantly fewer companies are relocating their global corporate headquarters to Switzerland, according to McKinsey. During the observation period, Switzerland's share in this area fell from 27 to 19%; for financial headquarters, it fell by a similar extent.

On the other hand, tech companies, in particular, are increasingly opening research centers in Switzerland. This is not necessarily a disadvantage. R&D departments create more economic added value than ordinary corporate headquarters, according to McKinsey. In an ecosystem of companies, startups, and other research departments, they generate added value that a simple corporate headquarters could not provide. The best example is Google, which works on products from YouTube to Google Maps with several thousand employees in Zurich. Nevertheless, the disappearance of new corporate headquarters is likely to become noticeable sooner or later.

## Development 3: Switzerland: More tech-focused, losing ground in pharma

When companies like Anthropic, Baidu, or OpenAI invest in Zurich, it is a source of pride for location promoters. Around 22% of all establishments in the technology sector between 2020 and 2025 were attributable to Switzerland. This is a significant increase of 7 percentage points.

However, the prominent names mask the true scale. «For many of these companies, Switzerland currently functions as a European satellite location,» says Michael Steinmann, head of McKinsey Switzerland. This means that hardly any of these companies are likely to become as large as Google in Zurich for the time being, he adds.

Precisely in a traditional Swiss flagship discipline, the trend is moving in the opposite direction: pharmaceuticals. Only one in three establishments was attributable to Switzerland, whereas between 2014 and 2019, it was almost half. Switzerland also recorded a decline in the industrial and consumer goods sectors, as McKinsey notes.

The figures show that the competitive balance among locations is shifting. This makes the question of how companies already operating here assess Switzerland all the more critical. In discussions with McKinsey and Swiss Amcham, the surveyed CEOs named the biggest dangers for Switzerland as a business location. Three of them stand out.

## Danger 1: The «Swiss Finish» and business paralysis

A full 70% of all CEOs complain about a growing regulatory burden, an increasingly formalistic application of rules, an ever more frequent «Swiss Finish,» and slow processes at the administrative level.

That company executives complain about regulation is not surprising. Just as trade unions criticize wages as too low, companies are annoyed by too many rules. Something else is remarkable: the federal government also sees the problem.

Ronald Indergand, the new chief economist at Seco, confirms in an interview with the NZZ that regulatory pressure and regulatory density are increasing. «Year after year, we note: the burden is steadily increasing. Switzerland is still somewhat better positioned than certain other countries. But we are not moving in the right direction.» He says there are over 36,268 obligations that companies in Switzerland must meet.

According to the McKinsey report, the annoyances range from the Swiss culture of objection in infrastructure projects to reporting obligations in the sustainability sector and lengthy price negotiations, which hinder and delay the introduction of new medicines.

There are also hurdles for skilled workers. Companies that want to recruit specialists in large numbers require considerable patience. For candidates from non-EU countries, unpredictable work permit procedures create additional uncertainty. As a result, many companies only concentrate the management of their research activities in Switzerland, the authors write. However, activities requiring more personnel often migrate abroad, they add.

## Danger 2: Dwindling tax advantage

For a long time, low corporate taxes were a trump card for Switzerland in competition among locations and a buffer against the strong franc. But this advantage is also dwindling. In January 2024, Switzerland was one of the first countries worldwide to introduce the OECD minimum tax. Large countries like the United States or China have yet to implement it to this day, despite initial commitments. In Switzerland, however, international companies with more than 750 million euros in revenue must pay at least 15% tax here.

Cantons like Zug have thus lost a distinguishing feature. «We implemented the OECD minimum tax in anticipatory obedience,» says Zug Finance Director Heinz Tännler. While he rejects its abolition, citing continuity, he argues that the new regulation harms Switzerland's attractiveness as a business location and creates significant bureaucracy.

Seco Chief Economist Indergand also confirms that the tax factor is losing relative importance for Switzerland. However, he is optimistic that Switzerland will defend its position through other location factors such as access to skilled workers or the availability of infrastructure.

McKinsey and Swiss Amcham emphasize that the rigorous implementation of the OECD minimum tax in Switzerland sometimes costs the affected corporations up to 2 percentage points of margin. 65% of the surveyed CEOs report increased cost pressure. It is all the more important, they say, that tax authorities make decisions quickly and create clarity, for example, through tax rulings.

## Danger 3: Scarcer, more expensive energy

In times of AI data centers and electric cars, stable access to affordable electricity is becoming an increasingly important competitive factor. For decades, Switzerland benefited from a low-CO₂ and affordably priced electricity supply from hydropower and nuclear power plants.

But this advantage is also threatened. The existing nuclear power plants Beznau, Gösgen, and Leibstadt are expected to be decommissioned in the coming decades. At the same time, the expansion potential of hydropower is limited.

By 2050, Switzerland is therefore likely to produce about 20 to 25% too little electricity to cover the greatly increased demand by then, and would have to import a large part of it, according to McKinsey. McKinsey describes the electricity gap as a strategic risk, especially since neighboring countries are increasingly prioritizing their own energy security. Electricity from solar and wind power is only a limited part of the solution due to Switzerland's geographical constraints, it adds.

«If energy is not reliably available at reasonable prices, sooner or later we will have a problem,» says Michael Steinmann, head of McKinsey Switzerland. He says China has understood this and sees investments in energy as a long-term competitive advantage for its industry and in artificial intelligence. «It is often underestimated in this country what a huge competitive advantage it was for Switzerland to have sustainable energy reliably available at reasonable prices,» he adds.

Back to the boiling frog: The McKinsey data do not show a decline in Switzerland as a business location. The water is not hot yet. But it is getting warmer.
