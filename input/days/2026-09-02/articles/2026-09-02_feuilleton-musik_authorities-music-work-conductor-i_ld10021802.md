It sounds like technical instructions: «Number seven again!» Or: «Number 197, five bars before the woodwinds enter!» Or: «Bar 47 again, strings enter.» In fact, it’s about music — specific passages from orchestral works that conductor Hugo Bollschweiler is rehearsing with the Aargau Youth Symphony Orchestra (JSAG).

Swiss filmmaker Georges Gachot is also present. He has placed the cameras right among the young musicians, so that viewers of his documentary film «Unisono. Of the Love for Music» find themselves, as it were, between instruments and music stands.

One listens to various passages from works by Richard Strauss, Antonín Dvořák, and Henryk Górecki. One also hears the conductor’s sometimes whimsical demands: «First violin, that passage again – but chopped! Like minced meat with pasta.»

## High motivation

The orchestra, composed of talented young people, music students, and young professionals, retreated to the Künstlerhaus Alte Kirche in Boswil in 2020 for a week of rehearsals (everyone still wore face masks due to Corona). Gachot captures the concentrated work in a reportage style. He shows the instrumentalists during sectional rehearsals, but also during daily relaxation exercises.

In addition, he conducts interviews with the musicians. The young people presented here could hardly be more well-behaved. Everyone seems highly motivated and ready to dedicate eight hours a day to music for a week.

The cello is a retreat for him, says the shy cellist, who sits somewhat uncertainly behind his instrument. Playing music promotes discipline, says a strict oboist. And the flutist finds that her better self emerges when making music. She says that in a flute solo she may sometimes be the orchestra diva for a few bars, but then she subordinates herself to the overall sound again.

Subordination to a common goal: This initially seems to be the aesthetic imperative during this rehearsal week. And one also wonders if this is the value system conveyed by classical music. In that respect, one would have liked to learn more about the social environment and cultural background of the orchestral members, who are as talented as they are disciplined.

However, Gachot’s film suddenly takes a surprising turn. Hugo Bollschweiler has invited jazz pianist and composer Iiro Rantala to Boswil. The Finnish maverick, as familiar with classical music as with jazz, rehearses some of his own pieces with the JSAG. He also wants to encourage the young instrumentalists to try improvisational self-experiments.

The ensemble freezes for a moment as if in shock. Suddenly, the focus is no longer supposed to be on a single work, but on the personal musicality of each individual, Rantala says. It is the tubist, then, who engages in a duo improvisation with Rantala — and makes a good impression.

## The conductor’s baton

Rantala, as his explanations in Gachot’s stimulating film later show, believes that improvisation can strengthen one’s artistic personality and sense of musical presence. He believes that the current generation should be allowed to incorporate influences from jazz, rock, and pop into their classical interpretations.

The young musicians do not seem entirely convinced. They praise Rantala for his openness and flexibility. They also see the importance of crossover in the musical future. But they are glad if they do not have to improvise at the final concert and can instead rely on their conductor’s baton.
