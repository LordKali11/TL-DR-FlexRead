# Trapped in dystopia: More than a month after the onslaught on Ceuta, the violence is escalating

*In Spain's North African exclave, refugees and residents alike are suffering. The opposition plans to use Ceuta's city holiday on Wednesday for nationwide protests against the government.*

Monday was the day of return. Few countries maintain a collective holiday period as steadfastly as Spain, where almost the entire country shuts down in August. Political activity was no exception; on Monday, that too returned. On television, many popular morning shows broadcast live from Ceuta as a central point of discussion.

Even more than a month after thousands of refugees entered the exclave and at least 150 migrants died, Ceuta dominates all debates in the kingdom. It was a place last visited by a Spanish king 19 years ago; a place that always had a rather exotic character; a place most Spaniards knew only from textbooks and found harder to reach than London, Zurich, or Istanbul. Since July 30, it seems to have become something like the country's soul and conscience.

Images of frustration and anger, of helplessness and exhaustion, wash across the sea to the mainland. Refugees and residents appear trapped in the same dystopia. Some live in miserable conditions, wander the streets, and still do not know where to go a month later. Others lament the loss of normality and are becoming increasingly violent. Last week, a tent camp was set on fire and Red Cross food distributions were blocked. Police and military, it seems, must protect everyone and everything from all quarters.

In some bakeries, bread is rationed. Sexual offenses have increased, and underage refugee girls are reportedly forced into prostitution in exchange for a single dinner. Parents fear for their children. Muslims often no longer feel like neighbors but rather like suspects. Regardless of religion, those who could left the city in August and traveled to family members or second homes on the mainland.

This Wednesday is now «Día de Ceuta.» The city holiday commemorates the year 1415, when João I of Portugal appointed Count Pedro de Menezes as governor on September 2, formally integrating Ceuta into his kingdom. In the 17th century, sovereignty passed to Spain, and there, the Federation of Municipalities and Provinces (FEMP) called for national solidarity rallies for Ceuta in all cities on Wednesday.

The FEMP is controlled by the conservative party, which also wants the demonstrations to be understood as a vote against the socialist government. Conservative party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo plans to participate in the demonstration in Madrid, in addition to a trip to Ceuta, and instructed all regional associations to do the same in their regions. The gesture costs nothing. However, in other regions of Spain, there is skepticism about what would truly help Ceuta – a distribution of the remaining refugees across the country.

## Government's underestimation of scale

A month after the influx, such a step in overcrowded Ceuta continues to fail due to the registration of migrants. On Friday, Spain requested support from the EU border agency Frontex and Europol for the triage of refugees and the processing of their applications. Brussels had already offered such assistance on several previous occasions, first at the end of July.

Government sources now privately admit that the extent of the crisis was underestimated even after its outbreak. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez has often proved to be an adept manager in the past, but in the Ceuta case, even allies do not give him high marks. Sánchez visited Ceuta on July 31 but has not returned to a city that, at times, the whole world was watching.

On Monday morning, he attempted to regain interpretive authority through a ninety-minute radio interview. During his vacation, his cabinet had not projected a favorable image. Key ministers Fernando Grande-Marlaska (Interior) and Margarita Robles (Defense) presented contradictory versions of the extent to which the intelligence service had been informed about the impending influx before July 30. Sánchez attempted to mediate on Monday, stating there had been information, but none about the extent. Russian and Israeli cyberactivists were responsible for this, he claimed, as they had spread the false news of an open border after the first arrivals. Both countries denied this involvement.

## Numbers and ministers at odds

Under the vague hope for Europe, up to 80,000 people arrived in the city, which barely has more inhabitants, at the end of July. Days later, the government spoke of 72,000 people, stating that only about 2,000 remained in the city. This was intended to emphasize how quickly and smoothly a large proportion of the migrants had been repatriated.

However, the numbers were clearly too optimistic. A larger number of minors and potentially asylum seekers were among the arrivals. Neither group may be expelled through an expedited procedure. The government had raised false hopes, a fatal political error. On Thursday, Sánchez will justify himself before Congress.

Meanwhile, Madrid speaks of 5,000 remaining refugees, while other sources cite a figure of 8,000 people. On Monday, distribution struggles broke out among migrants outside the reception centers over newly provided sleeping places. Police had to intervene with warning shots. Sánchez stated that 3,300 beds were currently available, with a target of 6,000. On Tuesday, the cabinet approved a new aid package of 168 million euros for Ceuta, which is expected to grow to 309 million by the end of the year.

The relationship with Morocco also remains a point of contention. The opposition criticizes the government's unwavering praise of cooperation with the neighboring state and its exoneration from responsibility for the border storm. Sánchez, for his part, announced his intention to arrange a visit by the King to Ceuta. Conservative governments had never dared to do this in the past. Morocco claims the exclave, and a visit by the Spanish head of state is considered a maximum provocation. Sánchez's announcement is apparently intended to show, against this backdrop, that someone like him is the last to lose courage.
