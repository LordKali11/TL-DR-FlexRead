Since last summer, more than 10,000 flags have appeared in Birmingham alone. These flags were not installed by public authorities on public buildings, nor were they brought out by people celebrating special occasions – such as royal jubilees, sporting events, or military ceremonies. Nor do they merely flutter on private properties.

Some flags in Birmingham were raised under the cover of darkness, but now they are also raised in broad daylight. They hang from streetlights, adorn traffic circles, bridges, and other public infrastructure. In the southwest of England's second-largest city, flags are attached with cable ties to almost every lamppost along entire streets.

## Islamist activities in schools

In addition to the Union Jack, the flag of the United Kingdom, the English flag with the red Saint George's Cross on a white background is often seen. The Saint George's Cross has also been spray-painted in the middle of some traffic circles.

What is happening in Birmingham? Not only are too many illegally installed flags fluttering in the wind. The city is marked by extreme political fragmentation, deep social tensions, and the aftermath of a severe financial crisis. To offset the deficit, funds for youth centers, libraries, and street lighting were cut. At the same time, municipal taxes were sharply increased. A garbage strike ongoing since 2025 is leading to overflowing bins and illegal waste disposal.

The city's «super diversity» also poses problems: more than half of the residents identify as ethnic minorities, while the proportion of ethnically white Britons is still around 43%. The mayor, Zaker Choudhry, is Muslim. The culture war between British nationalism and Islamic-conservative interests endangers social cohesion. In the summer of 2014, a scandal erupted when some schools faced accusations of having been infiltrated by Islamists. Not without reason, as subsequent investigations showed that there were «coordinated efforts to introduce an intolerant and aggressive Islamist ethos in some schools» in Birmingham.

## Far-right and anti-immigration tendencies

The widespread display of the national flag began in mid-July last year in Weoley Castle in Birmingham and spread to neighboring areas, from the West Midlands to the rest of England. The perpetrators are nationalist and anti-migration groups, such as the Weoley Warriors in Birmingham. Operation Raise the Colours is the name of another, supra-regional group that accepted a donation from the far-right organization Britain First, The Times reported.

On their crowdfunding page, the Weoley Warriors described themselves as «a group of proud English men with the common goal of showing Birmingham and the rest of the country how proud we are of our history, our freedoms, and our achievements.» They added that they want «to give hope to local communities that not all is lost and they are not alone.»

The Weoley Warriors stated on the BBC program «Midlands Today» that they merely wanted to brighten people's day and bring them joy and love.

However, the intensive flag display exacerbates a highly tense political and social climate. Furthermore, it is closely linked to current debates on migration. And it coincided with protests near accommodations for asylum seekers. Critics and anti-racism groups argue that the movement has far-right and anti-immigration tendencies and evokes a feeling of intimidation or unease among minorities.

Both the Saint George's flag and the Union Jack have been used as symbols by far-right political movements for many years. Critics consider the flag display not patriotic, but an excuse for xenophobia. Proponents, however, accuse the administrations of applying double standards, saying that Palestinian, Pakistani, or Somali flags are tolerated by authorities in public spaces.

## Up to £2500 fine for illegal flag hoisting

Former Prime Minister Keir Starmer tried to defuse the culture war by emphasizing that he was «a supporter of flags» and encouraged people to hoist them. His successor Andy Burnham, then-Mayor of Manchester, had tried to neutralize the issue with humor. The leader of Reform UK, Nigel Farage, is a proponent of flag displays. The Bishop of Leicester even suggested in The Times that churches should fly flags.

Legally, however, there are limits. English people are allowed to display flags on private property and on their houses. Authorities, local councils, and government buildings are also permitted to hoist the Union Jack or the Saint George's Cross.

But it is forbidden to attach flags to lampposts, bridges, or traffic circles without permission from the local highway authority or municipality. A violation can lead to fines of up to £2500. However, it is at the discretion of municipalities to decide the extent to which they will take action against the hoisting of unauthorized flags.

## Violence and arrests

The reactions of authorities across England vary. Several local authorities have applied for court injunctions or removed illegally placed flags on public property, arguing that they must ensure «safety and social cohesion.» These include municipalities such as Oxfordshire County Council and Southampton City Council.

All of this did not happen quietly. The dispute repeatedly led to confrontations in the streets between proponents and residents who removed the flags. This resulted in violence and arrests. City Council employees were threatened and verbally abused.

Birmingham City Council, led by a coalition of Liberal Democrats, Greens, and Independents, also classifies the unauthorized placement of flags as a «security risk» and an «impairment of public order.» The administration has removed hundreds of flags and applied to the High Court for an interim injunction against activist groups. The affected groups have announced that they will continue regardless.
