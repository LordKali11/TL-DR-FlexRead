# «I understand the concerns about Swiss, but sometimes they seem to me like phantom pains»

*Johannes Teyssen presided over the energy company Alpiq for five years; now he chairs the supervisory board of Lufthansa. He says in an interview that Swiss go-it-alone approaches are an illusion – whether in energy supply or aviation.*

**You took over as chairman of the Swiss power company Alpiq five years ago. Since May, you have also been leading the supervisory board of Lufthansa and are now stepping down from your post in Switzerland. Is this a difficult step for you?**

**Johannes Teyssen:** I do so with mixed feelings. I always greatly enjoyed the role at Alpiq. But the time commitment of my new role at Lufthansa no longer allows for such a mandate. But I will of course remain connected to Switzerland through Swiss.

**You just arrived from Germany on a Swiss flight. How well do you know Swiss, and what do you think of it?**

Swiss was the first Lufthansa unit I visited in my new role – although that was a coincidence. I fly with them regularly in my private life. At the beginning of the year, I traveled to São Paulo via Zurich. That was an excellent experience – but then so was the return flight with Lufthansa from Buenos Aires.

**At the time of the takeover more than 20 years ago, then-Lufthansa CEO Wolfgang Mayrhuber made a promise: Swiss would remain Swiss – even under the Lufthansa umbrella. Does this principle also apply to you as chairman of the group's supervisory board?**

Swiss is both: autonomous, but also part of a group. There is no other way. Or do you know of any other airline in Europe or North America with a good hundred aircraft that can survive independently? Flying is a business of scale. If we spend our time discussing how the various brands under the Lufthansa umbrella differ, we are on the wrong track. My most important task in my new position is a different one.

> **Experienced top manager** — Johannes Teyssen (67) has been Chairman of the Supervisory Board of Lufthansa since May. The lawyer and economist, who holds doctorates in both fields, also chaired the Swiss energy company Alpiq for the past five years – a position he will relinquish this autumn. From 2010 to 2021, he led the German energy giant E.On, which he fundamentally restructured.

**Which is?**

That Central Europe continues to have a major, high-performance airline in Lufthansa. We experienced during the financial and coronavirus crises how dangerous it is when international players suddenly fail. We must be able to operate core infrastructures ourselves – whether in the financial system, in energy, or in logistics. We must not make ourselves dependent on the Chinese, Americans or others.

**So the discussion about Swiss's autonomy is secondary for you?**

I myself sometimes find the service at Swiss to be a little more personal than at other airlines in the group. And I enjoy that. But for the future, there can only be cooperation, not an either-or. Everything else is nostalgia. The Lufthansa Group is a central part of Europe's transport infrastructure. We must defend its position like a mother her child. I want to help shape a company that all Swiss, Austrians, Belgians, Italians and Germans can look upon with a certain pride.

**Swiss is the Lufthansa Group's profit driver. The other passenger airlines like Austrian, Brussels Airlines or the core Lufthansa brand, on the other hand, barely make a profit or are even in the red. Surely that has to change.**

And how. The entire group must find its way back to a position of strength – in terms of profitability, quality and customer loyalty. The Lufthansa Group has clear weaknesses, but no one is denying that. In any case, I will tackle these weaknesses. We must now do everything we can, together with the employees and management, to ensure that things improve significantly.

**Are you already thinking about concrete measures?**

It's too early for that. The group is enormously complex. It has over 100,000 employees and is responsible for 3,000 flights a day. It has been around for 100 years for a reason. I am approaching my task with a great deal of respect.

**The concern remains that Swiss is being gradually disempowered from Frankfurt.**

I understand the concerns in Switzerland about Swiss. But sometimes they seem to me like phantom pains that one feels for years after an amputation – instead of focusing on how we can become better together in the future.

**After five years, you are leaving the electricity giant Alpiq this fall. When you joined the group in 2022, the company ran into trouble during the energy crisis – liquidity assistance from the federal government was on the table. Had you expected a quieter start?**

It was clear to me that Alpiq was struggling. But suddenly it was no longer about developing prospects, but about ensuring sheer survival. That was extremely challenging.

**Where does the group stand today?**

Alpiq has become a completely different company. It is more than solidly positioned, has an equity ratio of well over 50%, and has regained the full confidence of the financial markets. And it's no longer a fatal blow for us today if the Gösgen nuclear power plant is shut down for nine months, even if that is extremely annoying.

**Are you also using this financial leeway for new projects?**

We are in the process of implementing the largest investment program in the company's history. Our strategy is to react flexibly to electricity demand – for example, with pumped-storage and gas-fired power plants, and increasingly with large-scale batteries. That is paying off in full. The demand for these services in Europe is immense.

**These technologies serve to balance out short-term fluctuations in the European electricity system. In Switzerland, another concern is increasingly gaining ground: whether we will have enough electricity in the long term.**

The country's security of supply is and remains a central component of our strategy. But I have to say: Switzerland is absolutely right to have such concerns.

**Why?**

At least when it comes to major infrastructure, Switzerland has unfortunately lost some of its entrepreneurial drive. You make one ambitious plan after another – and then find that practically nothing can be implemented.

**Where, for example?**

With the 15 hydropower projects classified as important by the federal government, hardly anything has happened, with a few exceptions. Project developers, supporters and opponents meet at round tables, but as soon as these are over, everything is called into question again. But when it comes to electricity, Switzerland cannot simply rely on others. It should actually be rapidly expanding its capacity – instead, we are fighting today just to maintain what already exists.

**You're alluding to the Gösgen nuclear power plant, which is operated by Alpiq. How long do you think it will run?**

What I know, thanks to our experts, is this: It can be operated for significantly longer than 50 or 60 years.

**And will it be?**

That is an extremely difficult decision for us. We would first have to invest tens of millions over 10 years – without knowing how profitable the plant will be in 20 or 30 years, when even more cheap solar power is flooding the market.

**Is there a solution?**

The state and the operator must share the risk in this task of the century. In fact, this is how it is done in many countries. Behind the power utility EDF in France stands the state and thus the state's balance sheet. In England, Belgium or Eastern Europe, operators and governments enter into partnerships. What is clear is this: Alpiq cannot possibly shoulder such a task alone.

**But from a security of supply perspective, does it make sense to keep the Gösgen and Leibstadt nuclear power plants running longer?**

Not just from that perspective. It is simply the cheapest solution for the country and for the industry that depends on competitive electricity prices.

**The nuclear energy discussion is also currently revolving around the construction of new nuclear power plants. Is that a solution?**

We have to be careful not to have a phony debate here. Even if the voters lift the technology ban, there still won't be a single new power plant.

**Could Alpiq, for example, build the state-of-the-art small reactors that are being talked about so much right now?**

A country the size of Switzerland would be well advised not to jump on this bandwagon too early. The financial risks of the initial phase are simply too great for that. American companies and billionaires are currently investing huge sums of money in these technologies because they need electricity for their AI data centers. It is wise to let others bear these immense development costs for the time being and to wait and see what proves effective in practice.

**Or should Switzerland rely on established large-scale reactors, like the ones recently built in Finland or the U.S.?**

I don't see that happening. As I said: I wouldn't seek a pioneering role with new technologies – but I wouldn't rebuild the past either. We don't need another large-scale reactor. If anything, I would eventually build smaller nuclear power plants of a newer technology that can be controlled more flexibly and therefore fit better into a renewable energy system.

**When the sun isn't shining and the wind isn't blowing, Switzerland cannot close the gap in electricity production on its own. It is dependent on exchange with neighboring countries, especially in winter. How important is an electricity agreement with the EU for this?**

The discussion about the electricity agreement sometimes reminds me of the one about Swiss: Going it alone is an illusion – whether for Swiss or for the energy supply. Just as Swiss could not survive without the Lufthansa network, the Swiss electricity system cannot be operated in isolation. The country is already having the greatest difficulty expanding its own electricity infrastructure to meet future demand. How is going it alone supposed to work? It is much better to operate within a strong alliance.

**The negotiated electricity agreement with the EU is on the table. Are you satisfied with the result?**

The Swiss negotiators achieved much more than I would have thought possible. The agreement is a reasonable compromise.

**Are you saying that because Alpiq, as a European electricity trader, would benefit from an electricity agreement?**

For Alpiq, a situation without an agreement is a disadvantage, but not a decisive one – we can adapt. But for the country's prosperity, integration into the EU electricity market is the right solution. In the end, the Swiss population has always shown a very good instinct for what is economically sensible and necessary.

**What is your parting message for Switzerland?**

Hold on to your sense of community, your openness to the world and your pragmatism. But get back to implementing things more. Today, we are living too much off what our grandparents' generation created in terms of infrastructure expansion.
