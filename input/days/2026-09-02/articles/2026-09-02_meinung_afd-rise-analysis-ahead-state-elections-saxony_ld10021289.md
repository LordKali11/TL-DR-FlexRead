# The AfD on the Rise – An Analysis Ahead of the State Elections in Saxony-Anhalt, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and Berlin

*Declining CDU and SPD, rising AfD and The Left Party: Homogeneous coalition formation challenged*

The current level of excitement in politics, journalism and political science is immense – not least because of the expected successes of the AfD, which ranks clearly first nationwide among all opinion research institutes.

While the Christian Democratic Union/Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) received 28.6% in the federal election (less than expected) and the AfD 20.8% (more than expected), the ratio is almost reversed today. The losses of the CDU/CSU do not benefit its Social Democratic Party (SPD) coalition partner, and the gains of the AfD do not come at the expense of the other fringe force, The Left Party – quite the opposite. The SPD's share has shrunk by more than a quarter, while that of the post-communists has risen by more than a quarter. What seems paradoxical is that these parties from the «fringe» are finding more support than those from the «center.»

The CDU under its unpopular Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who broke central promises such as adhering to the debt brake after the federal election, is disappointing its supporters' expectations in every respect. Adhering to the «firewall» is becoming less effective even among its own clientele, as overlaps between the CDU/CSU and the AfD are discernible.

The coalition partner SPD sometimes sees itself as a brake, orienting itself toward the left-wing «base.» As a result, the working class, once the party's traditional clientele, is flocking to the AfD. When Lars Klingbeil, shortly before the September elections, views the initiation of a ban procedure against the AfD as «a democratic duty to protect our country,» this amounts to an admission of failure. The party knows what it wants to be against. But does it know what it stands for?

## Firewall: Strengthening the AfD

The rise of the marginalized AfD is less due to its own achievements, as it holds no political responsibility anywhere and can promise the moon in social networks with its «patriotic turn.» The firewall (what a martial term) does not weaken it but strengthens it. Affairs triggered by nepotism seem to roll off it, as do internal conflicts.

The Left Party also benefits from the federal government's poor image. In addition, there are three internal party developments: the anti-fascist furor not only against the AfD, for example from parliamentary group leader Heidi Reichinnek in the Bundestag (German parliament); the departure of the Wagenknecht faction, which has reduced internal conflicts; and the radicalization caused by the influx of many young members, for example in the assessment of the Middle East conflict.

The Greens have gained stature since the 2025 federal election. They are increasingly recognizing their mistake, namely, that they misjudged the realities of life for many voters and should by no means rely on alarmist climate protection as they did before. Furthermore, at the federal level, they are striving for an alliance with the CDU/CSU, not with the weakening SPD. This is now paying off, although less so in the east of the country.

In contrast, the FDP and Bündnis Sahra Wagenknecht (BSW) are not making any headway. Internal disputes paralyze any sense of optimism. The wing around the 74-year-old FDP chairman Wolfgang Kubicki is accused of right-wing tendencies and a lack of innovation. After the election in Saxony-Anhalt, the FDP is likely to lose its last ministerial office at the state level.

The elections in small Saxony-Anhalt are considered a turning point, a kind of paradigm shift – far beyond the region – due to the possible absolute majority of seats for the AfD: the politically polarized state is in turmoil. Unlike usual, the party has a smart top candidate in 35-year-old Ulrich Siegmund, who understands how to «sell» his concerns winningly. In contrast, the solid but by no means charismatic State Premier Sven Schulze (CDU, since January 2026) falls short. Without an AfD majority, the CDU could form a minority government with all other factions, tolerated by The Left Party. Although the CDU maintains that it adheres to the incompatibility resolution of 2018, it is now only on paper with regard to The Left Party.

With parliamentary representation of the BSW, which rebels against the firewall, neither the AfD nor its competitors would, under certain circumstances, have a majority of seats. Schulze would initially remain acting state premier, before new elections would probably become inevitable.

The crucial question is: Can the AfD really have an interest in a sole government given the internal and external obstacles? And is a heterogeneous «against the right» government led by the CDU desirable?

## Good Chances for Manuela Schwesig

In Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, the AfD with Leif-Erik Holm is ahead of the SPD, but not by such a margin that it would have a real chance of power. In this respect, much speaks for a continuation of Manuela Schwesig's state premiership; she enjoys great popularity and received a sensational 39.6% for the SPD in 2021. However, it is likely that the numbers will not be sufficient for a continuation of the alliance with The Left Party. If the Greens were to re-enter the state parliament, a red-red-green coalition might be forged. Otherwise, the SPD would have to form a minority government with the CDU, tolerated by The Left Party. The era of two-party coalitions based on majorities (black-yellow, black-red, red-red, red-black) is probably over now in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. A single-digit CDU result would correspond to its worst in a federal state since 1951.

In once-divided Berlin, with almost four million inhabitants, larger than Saxony-Anhalt and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania combined, fragmentation is particularly severe. Four parties (CDU with Stefan Evers, the Greens with Werner Graf, The Left Party with Elif Eralp, the AfD with Kristin Brinker) are vying for first place. The SPD with Steffen Krach follows behind. For arithmetic reasons alone, only a three-party alliance is an option. Whether the SPD and the Greens will coalesce with the CDU or The Left Party depends on imponderables: Should the CDU be the strongest force, the two parties could enter into an alliance with the post-communists. If, on the other hand, The Left Party were ahead, they might prefer an alliance with the CDU.

## One-Sided Media

What particularly bothers me as an extremism researcher is that while extremist positions circulate within the AfD, these do not define its overall image. Nevertheless, some media generally describe it – probably with deterrent intent – as «extremist» and thus suppress the fact that the domestic intelligence agency in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and Berlin classifies the party neither as «proven right-wing extremist» nor as a «suspected case of right-wing extremism.» Leif-Erik Holm and Kristin Brinker are not «radicals.» In contrast, the AfD in Saxony-Anhalt is considered «proven right-wing extremist,» with the domestic intelligence agency functioning as part of the Interior Ministry.

In Berlin, for example, by no means in Saxony-Anhalt, the local radical Left Party is more at odds with the principles of the democratic constitutional state than the AfD, not only because of its «expropriation fantasies» but also because of anti-Semitic tendencies. What is irritating is that although the former domestic intelligence agency president Hans-Georg Maassen, who was dismissed in 2018, has expressed his interest in the office of Saxony-Anhalt's Interior Minister under an AfD state premier, the AfD remains tight-lipped. Do they not trust him?

After the September elections, Bärbel Bas and Lars Klingbeil are likely to give up (or have to give up) the SPD leadership. However, such reshuffles are of little use if they are not accompanied by a substantive change, for example, a commitment against state paternalism.

What unites the CDU and SPD in the states is that they each distance themselves from the federal government they jointly support. The drain on these parties is immense: the number of their members is decreasing year after year. At the end of 2025, the CDU had 356,769 members (end of 1995: 657,643), and the SPD had 348,451 (end of 1995: 817,650). This corresponds to a halving. 2027 will see another «super election year» with five state elections in the old federal states. Which party will then manage a necessary change of course and by what means?
