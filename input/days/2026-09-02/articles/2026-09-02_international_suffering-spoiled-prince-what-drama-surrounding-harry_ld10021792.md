Prince Harry and his wife Meghan Markle have returned to England after six years in the U.S. This homecoming triggers strong emotions in the British public. For days, both right-wing and left-wing media have been full of news and analyses on the topic, with the abundance of contributions contrasting with the lack of verified information. Beyond the fact that they are back, little is known.

Why does the banal fact of a move stir up the British so much? The excitement reveals a lot about the complex and contradictory relationship with the monarchy.

## Super-privileged as victims

Prince Harry and Meghan have always polarized. Some see them as representatives of a young, modern generation who rebel against rigid court conventions and fight for more freedom and self-determination – much like Harry's mother Diana, who died in 1997. These admirers share the couple's outrage that some royals allegedly met the dark-skinned Meghan with racist rejection. And they understand Harry's anger about the sensationalist tabloid media, which reportedly made the couple's life a living hell. Both were apparently important reasons for their emigration or even flight to California.

Others, however, suspect hypocrisy. From their perspective, Harry and Meghan are narcissists who constantly crave attention and at the same time complain that they are not left in peace. Hardly had they escaped the British paparazzi when they spread their private lives in a book («Spare») and a Netflix series («Harry & Meghan») and lambasted the Royal Court on Oprah Winfrey. A retreat into private life looks different. Many also find it offensive that while they stepped down as working members of the royal family, they still value their titles as Duke and Duchess of Sussex.

For average earners, the constant complaints about their suffering also seem self-pitying: hyper-privileged individuals staging themselves as victims. Harry's legal battle over the payment of his bodyguards also caused bewilderment. Why should he not personally pay for his security if he has already renounced the Royal Court? At the same time, there was an air of self-importance. Was he really as endangered as he claimed? Why did he constantly push into the limelight and then lament that he was exposed and dependent on permanent protection?

Even more ridicule is poured on Meghan, who portrays herself as a lifestyle consultant with banal mindfulness wisdom in the series «With Love, Meghan» and with her product line As Ever. Naturally, the couple immediately interprets the rejection of Meghan as a smear campaign.

## Royals: exemplary and scandalous

At the same time, the British public's obsessive preoccupation with Harry and Meghan casts a harsh light on the people's contradictory relationship with the monarchy. On the one hand, the royals are supposed to represent British ideals, so that people can see themselves reflected in them as in a flattering mirror. The Queen embodied this role exemplarily. In all her nobility, she appeared modest, diligent, selfless, discreet, polite and kind – just as the British themselves like to see themselves.

On the other hand, the public wants stories and scandals. After all, the royals are also part of the entertainment industry. Taxpayers want something for their money, and the tabloid media would long be bankrupt if all royals were as decent and boring as Queen Elizabeth. People are outraged when Harry steps out of line – for example, when he appeared at a party in a Nazi costume – but at the same time, they wish for such excitement. The royal family must also live out the decadence, immorality and excess that good British citizens deny themselves.

The monarchy is supposed to be shrouded in an aura of mystery and myth. But the people also crave intimate revelations, just like with any celebrity. These are all the more appealing the more the royal family isolates itself. Not only the – actually conservative – tabloid media, which serve up dirt daily in the name of decency, but also the royals themselves, for whom nothing would be more devastating than to sink into oblivion, thrive on this tension and taboo-breaking. All of this is paradoxical and hypocritical, but it is well known that no one has ever died of contradictions.

## Reenactment of the Diana drama

The excitement surrounding Harry and Meghan is only understandable against the backdrop of the Diana story. For the tragicomedy surrounding Meghan seems like a tired remake of the Lady Di drama. In both cases, it concerns a young woman who runs up against the centuries-old walls of the Royal Court and encounters cold rejection. The only difference is that Diana won the hearts of the British – which did not amuse the Queen. However, according to surveys, two-thirds of Britons find Meghan annoying. The fact that Harry, when he speaks of Meghan, constantly invokes his mother's fate does not make it better.

For some, the permanent comparison smells of a PR strategy to present Meghan as a kind of reincarnation of the idolized Diana. Perhaps it is indeed a reenactment, but in a psychological sense. It is obvious that Meghan's personality in many ways recalls Diana. It is conceivable that Harry, who lost his mother at the age of twelve – when she was pursued by paparazzi and her car crashed into a concrete pillar in the tunnel – not only relives this trauma again and again, but also reenacts it repeatedly.

Again, Harry has the impression that his wife is being pursued by journalists and driven to madness, with the Court lacking any understanding for the precarious situation. He repeatedly says that he does not want history to repeat itself. This time, he tries to protect Meghan – by breaking away from the Court, moving to the U.S., his lawsuits against the press, and the demand for personal protection.

All of this is understandable, but at the same time, it creates the impression of a show – not necessarily in the sense of conscious manipulation, but in the sense of the inauthentic and paranoid. Moreover, because Harry's books, series, and interviews are not just about the truth but also about the commercialization of his story, there is a suspicion that he inflates every minor problem into a Shakespearean drama.

The Royal Court does not need such egomania at the moment. After the revelations about Andrew in connection with convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, the image of the British monarchy is already damaged. The risk is high that the self-centered Harry and his self-absorbed wife will further harm the royals' reputation.

Britons are fully confronted with rising living costs, economic stagnation, domestic political instability, and the question of whether the new Prime Minister Andy Burnham can turn things around. They lack the patience for the sufferings of a spoiled prince who persistently puts himself in the spotlight and at the same time complains that his privacy is not respected.
