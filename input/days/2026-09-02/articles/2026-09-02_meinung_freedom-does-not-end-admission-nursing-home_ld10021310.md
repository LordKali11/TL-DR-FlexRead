# Freedom Does Not End with Admission to a Nursing Home: Assisted Dying Possible There

*It is legitimate for private nursing homes to refuse assisted suicide. However, they must still tolerate it in the case of their fellow human beings.*

More and more people are choosing to end their lives voluntarily. Since the turn of the millennium, the number of assisted suicides in Switzerland has increased tenfold. These are primarily elderly people who – in the face of incurable diseases, chronic suffering, or the loss of fundamental physical abilities – wish to determine for themselves when and how their journey ends.

Most of those who decide on this step want to carry it out in their own homes. But not everyone can do so.

Many elderly people in need of care spend their last months or years in a nursing or care home. And there, on assisted dying, there is no consensus in Switzerland.

In some cantons, all nursing and care homes must tolerate the assisted suicide of a resident; in others, this rule applies only to homes with a state performance mandate. In many places, there is no explicit rule at all; the institutions decide for themselves.

Now, the Zurich Cantonal Parliament wants to create a uniform and clear rule, at least within its territory. All homes, it says, must tolerate assisted suicide. It is a good principle: No one is forced to provide assisted dying against their will or actively support it. At the same time, no one may interfere with the freedom of those affected to die with self-determination in their own homes.

## Freedom and self-determination for the dying

There is resistance to the new rule from conservative circles. The Swiss People's Party (SVP), Federal Democratic Union (EDU), and Evangelical People's Party (EVP) have launched a referendum; therefore, on September 27, the Zurich electorate will vote on the issue. A popular initiative by assisted dying organizations, whose demands went even further, was subsequently withdrawn.

Christian-oriented private homes in the canton, in particular, still have great concerns. They see the obligation to tolerate assisted dying as a violation of their freedom of belief and economic freedom. For staff and residents, they say, the mere knowledge of an assisted suicide by a neighbor is an unacceptable imposition.

These concerns are legitimate. Two important fundamental rights clash in this vote. And yet, the liberal answer is clear in the end: One may reject assisted dying. But one must tolerate it for fellow residents.

The principle behind this, according to the philosopher John Stuart Mill, is: The freedom of the individual may only be restricted if its exercise causes harm to someone else. However, merely causing offense is not harm that justifies intervention.

## Changing one's mind until the last moment

This is also the case here. The religious feelings and economic freedom of the institutions must be respected. Whenever possible, those affected should ask themselves before entering a home how they would like to end their lives one day.

But death and dying are unpredictable. Especially with something so intimate, one must be allowed to change one's mind until the very last moment. After all, freedom does not end with admission to a nursing home.

Not many will be directly affected by the new rule: 29 homes in the canton, accounting for about 10% of the beds. Even after a yes vote, these institutions do not have to fear a flood of cases. Most elderly people will continue to choose a home that aligns with their values.

The extension of the duty of tolerance to hospitals is also no reason for a no vote. Assisted suicides are extremely rare there and are likely to remain an exception, given the widespread desire to die in one's own home.

Finally, the arguments of individual moralists who brand assisted suicide as a selfish act and therefore, they argue, do not want to tolerate it are utterly absurd. The Swiss assisted dying regulation has proven its worth over the years – it is a pragmatic solution that takes into account the freedom of the individual and at the same time ensures that only those whose wish to die is firm take this path.

To accept this reality in daily nursing home life as well, and not to banish it to anonymous premises: That is not a question of individual morality. It is a question of dignity.

## Good palliative care: At least as important

Finally, it remains important that the debate about a dignified death is not reduced to the question of assisted dying. A different case is much more common: that of a seriously ill elderly person who does not wish for invasive life-sustaining measures, but rather a transition as pain-free as possible – and who is nevertheless taken to the hospital and kept alive there by all means.

The answer to this is: good advance care planning, clear living wills, and the promotion of specialized palliative care, which Canton Zurich has increasingly pursued for several years.

Especially in the canton's nursing and care homes, there are still gaps here. Closing them is at least as important – if not more important – than a yes vote on September 27.
