# Solar power: Misunderstood and over-subsidized?

*Photovoltaic systems are first subsidized, then declared mandatory under the Energy Act, and from 2027, owners will pay for the right to feed electricity into the grid. It is not surprising that doubts about the Energy Strategy are emerging.*

Photovoltaic systems are the fastest-growing source of electricity in Switzerland, not only throughout the year but also during the winter half. The systems newly installed on rooftops in 2024 alone produce approximately 400 GWh of winter electricity annually. This roughly corresponds to the amount of energy that Elcom has defined on average as a hydropower reserve for the winter.

Regardless of whether a winter is characterized by dark doldrums or prolonged periods of snowfall, these systems can contribute to supply security by ensuring that reservoirs have an additional energy reserve of approximately 400 GWh available in spring. The fact that this may differ in practice is due to energy trading. Nevertheless, the sun remains our most reliable energy source in the annual balance.

## Flawed thinking on both sides

Solar power thus has the potential to make a significant contribution to Switzerland's electricity supply even in winter. With a share of more than 15% of annual electricity demand, photovoltaics have advanced to third place among the most important electricity sources, after hydro and nuclear power, within just a few years. With more than 10 GW of installed capacity, Swiss photovoltaic systems already form the country's most powerful power plant, largely operated as a single unit by the sun.

The installed photovoltaic capacity is now about three times greater than that of all Swiss nuclear power plants combined. Most of the approximately 400,000 systems feed their electricity into the grid without capacity control. For several hours, current solar power production already exceeds Switzerland's total electricity demand.

These are not good conditions for the quadrupling of photovoltaic capacity, as envisaged by the Energy Strategy. Solar power is no longer the untouchable beacon of hope it once was, which could enjoy priority feed-in and remain exempt from all grid and system stability requirements. Various larger distribution grid operators and the Association of Swiss Electricity Companies (VSE) report that rooftop photovoltaics are significantly driving grid expansion. They point to high investments that would be necessary to integrate the amount of solar power planned in the Energy Strategy into the grid.

In doing so, they make the same mistake as the uncompromising supporters of photovoltaics, who demand high feed-in tariffs and suspect an attack on renewable energies in every grid integration measure. Both sides overlook the fact that photovoltaics have now reached a level of maturity where a future without adapting the rules of the game would inevitably lead to system problems. Whether the distribution grids are expanded for this purpose or not plays only a minor role from the perspective of the overall system.

A look at the electrical capacities illustrates the dimension of the challenge: the currently installed 10 GW of photovoltaic capacity is expected to grow to more than 40 GW in the medium term. And this in an electricity system whose maximum demand barely exceeds 10 GW. With approximately 3.5 GW of pumping capacity, pumped-storage power plants change the picture only marginally. Exporting these surpluses abroad is also not a realistic solution, because the sun shines in our neighboring countries at the same time as it does here, and they too are increasingly relying on photovoltaics.

## Control at the feed-in point

The only sensible way to handle future solar power capacities is to control them at the feed-in point. It is not about dictating to owners how they should use their solar power, but about avoiding unnecessary infrastructure for rarely needed peak capacities. According to a study by the Bern University of Applied Sciences and Swissgrid, with 30 GW of photovoltaic capacity, a grid connection of 40% of the system's capacity already provides almost the entire economic benefit of feed-in.

In certain circles, this finding triggers a different reaction: they say we already have enough photovoltaics and could simply solve the problem by stopping expansion. That is true insofar as the side effects of a medication also disappear when it is discontinued. But do we therefore forgo treatment?

Photovoltaics is and remains the best horse we have in the stable for the next decades of the energy transition. Solar power is manageable if it is correctly integrated into the system. This is also shown in Swissgrid's recently published White Paper. We therefore need both framework conditions that promote the further expansion of photovoltaic systems and measures that limit uncontrolled grid feed-in. Passing on market price signals to system operators, as planned from 2027, is an important step in this direction.

This also resolves the contradiction mentioned at the outset: building more systems, but not being allowed to feed in at all times, is part of the reality of a functioning electricity system with a high proportion of solar power. It is the task of politicians, authorities, and associations to ensure that photovoltaic systems continue to be built in accordance with the popular mandate and that they simultaneously support rather than destabilize our electricity system.
