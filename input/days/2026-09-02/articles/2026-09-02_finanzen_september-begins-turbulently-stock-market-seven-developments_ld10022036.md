American government bonds are considered the safest form of investment worldwide. Yet Treasuries are experiencing a sell-off not seen in a long time – even though the world currently appears quite uncertain. Can investors no longer rely on anything in financial markets?

At the very least, investors must prepare for a turbulent September. Stock markets are gripped by several problems for which there is no quick solution. An overview of the most significant difficulties and interconnections.

## 1. Investors turning away from the U.S.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) e0dee9722d20b8c0e6f2d50690327eb2]

Most striking is the price decline in the government bond market, especially for U.S. bonds. Mirroring the falling prices, yields are climbing. American government bonds with a 30-year maturity yield just under 5.3%. This is the highest value in almost 20 years. 10-year bonds are trading at a three-year high.

Behind the sell-off are concerns about the increasing debt burden of the world's largest economy. Due to doubts about the sustainability of liabilities, investors are demanding higher risk premiums, especially for longer maturities.

These concerns were reinforced rather than mitigated by the U.S. Treasury's announcement to buy back its own government bonds on the market. While this would increase demand for the bonds and thus their prices, it would only combat the symptom – not the cause of doubts about America's fiscal policy.

## 2. Rest of the world caught in the maelstrom

Investors could dismiss the unrest as an American problem, had it not already spread to other countries. In Japan, 10-year government bonds are yielding more than 3% for the first time since 1996. 30-year bonds are yielding more than ever before.

This also has repercussions for the U.S. bond market, where Japanese investors are traditionally heavily involved. Foreign investment becomes less attractive for them when yields also rise at home. Furthermore, the weak yen makes the purchase of American government bonds more expensive for them, which additionally burdens demand for these bonds and thus their prices.

Government bond prices are also falling in Europe. The phenomenon is pronounced in France, but German 10- and 30-year bonds also yielded at their highest levels in 15 years. Many Eurozone countries themselves have to shoulder large mountains of debt. Added to this is the international signaling effect of the much-watched Treasuries: Bank of America expects that American risk premiums are an important driver for the sell-off in Europe and will remain so in the short term.

## 3. Rising prices and rising interest rates

Another reason for the price losses in the bond markets in the U.S., Japan, and Europe is inflation. Inflation in the Eurozone climbed to more than 3% in August. This increases the chances that the European Central Bank (ECB) will raise key interest rates next week. The main reason was higher energy costs, driven by turbulence in the crude oil and natural gas markets due to the Iran war.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b8410dd5ca0403bd650b88c41c810bf2]

The same applies to the U.S. central bank, the Fed, which will decide on interest rate levels in the middle of the month. Chairman Kevin Warsh named inflation as his biggest concern just a few days ago. Rising interest rates make existing bonds less attractive compared to new bonds, and inflation erodes coupons.

## 4. Iran war, expensive fuel

On Wednesday night, the U.S. military carried out new strikes against targets in Iran, while Iran shelled U.S. targets in Jordan and Iraq. The fighting escalated on Sunday, after a month of relative calm. The Tehran regime announced that it had prepared «unforgettable lessons» for America.

Subsequently, the price of oil has risen and now stands at around $94 a barrel for benchmark Brent crude. In early July, when peace between the U.S. and Iran seemed within reach, it traded at $70. Crude oil exports from the Gulf region remain restricted because no stable and secure regulation for the passage of tankers through the Strait of Hormuz has yet been found.

At the same time, refineries in the Persian Gulf, as well as in Russia, are not operating at full capacity. Russia was an important exporter of fuels, particularly diesel, but its production there is suffering from Ukrainian attacks. The investment bank Goldman Sachs maintains that the supply situation for refined fuels has developed worse than for crude oil. Analysts expect higher price premiums until 2027.

## 5. Trade dispute spreading

As if fuel costs were not burdening the global economy enough, trade barriers also threaten to continue growing. A spotlight was cast on this by the meeting of the G-20 finance ministers, a forum of the largest economies. The gathering in the U.S. ended on Tuesday without a joint final declaration. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said only 19 of the 20 participants agreed on a communiqué. The communiqué criticizes countries with excessive trade surpluses that rely heavily on exports for their economic growth.

This wording was directed against China, which therefore did not agree to the declaration. Bessent called on other countries to protect themselves from Chinese goods that were seeking new buyers on world markets due to U.S. tariffs. This increases the risk that the trade war initiated by President Trump will find protectionist imitators. This could also increase import prices and thus inflation.

## **6. AI investments competing with government bonds**

Price increases due to energy costs, as well as tariffs and trade barriers, also burden the outlook for companies and thus the stock markets. Many stock indices stopped their upward trend in August. Only one sector seemed immune to concerns for a long time: the U.S. tech sector, driven by billions in investments in AI and data centers.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 5caeb2cde3decbe8e2ab753b769a5fd7]

Although some investors are becoming more skeptical here too, the tech companies' demand for capital remains unbroken. The latest news: American media report that AI developer Anthropic closed a $35 billion data center construction deal this week.

Such investments bring a new problem for the bond market: tech companies are increasingly financing them via longer-term bonds. These bonds compete with government bonds for investor capital. UBS notes that if AI investments remain high, this could exert further pressure on government bond prices. The higher bond yields, UBS states, reflect not only fiscal concerns but also the private sector's financing needs.

## 7. Partners Group: Alternative investments not a sure thing

Providers of unlisted investments are also struggling with the tense market sentiment. The best example: Partners Group. The shares of the Zug-based private equity firm lost more than 7% in value on Tuesday after the company warned of lower earnings. The first-half figures disappointed expectations, especially the outlook for revenues from so-called performance fees.

These fees are due, among others, when the financial group sells holdings. However, some of these sales could be postponed until 2027. Although management still spoke of a reasonably intact sales environment – the doubts were enough to scare investors away.
