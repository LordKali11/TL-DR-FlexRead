# Women who fight still provoke – even now: Einsiedeln hosts first international tournament for female wrestlers

*The Ladies Wrestling Tournament Switzerland is making Swiss sports history. And a new book dispels the myth that fighting is masculine.*

In the second match, her favorite move, the *Päckli* (package hold), works. Wrestler Delia Gisler wraps one arm around her Norwegian opponent's neck, while with the other she pulls up her knee. The opponent cannot break free. Gisler throws her to the ground and presses until the opponent's shoulder blades are both on the mat. A pinfall victory for Gisler.

«I am usually physically superior,» she says, explaining that a *Päckli* requires arm strength. She had already tried it in the first match of the tournament. It didn't work. So she did the leg screw: lying on the ground, she grabbed the outstretched legs of her Austrian opponent and twisted them several times around her own body. Gisler received two points for each rotation. With a 10-0 score, the match was over; Gisler won by technical superiority.

Women who fight still provoke – even now. Female wrestlers are a minority in Swiss wrestling. There are 27 clubs in Switzerland, and about half of them also have girls and women wrestling. Most only have a few individuals.

In training and in championship matches, women usually wrestle against men. They grab each other, embrace and lie on each other's bodies. The wrestling singlet covers the torso and thighs; but to prevent it from being grabbed, it is little more than a wisp of nothing. Female wrestlers are used to physical contact, and also to having to listen to sexist remarks.

At the Ladies Wrestling Tournament in Einsiedeln, the first international tournament for female wrestlers in Switzerland, the women fight among themselves. Women's wrestling is becoming more professional and more visible, thanks to individual fighters who no longer want to be just sparring partners.

## Battle braids and a hole in the stomach

For Gisler, 17, from the Schattdorf wrestling club, a national squad athlete and a second-year apprentice in advertising technology, the tournament day begins with hunger. For a week, she has been watching her weight, eating almost no carbohydrates, no sugar and drinking only water. The evening before, she had to «make weight» in training, meaning sweating out fluids. One can lose up to a kilogram that way, she says, if one has time. She went to bed already dehydrated so that she would stay under 61 kilograms at the weigh-in before the tournament. The scale reads 60.9 kilograms, a perfect landing; she starts in the Under-17, 61-kilogram category. Finally, she can drink and eat a ham sandwich.

For the Under-17 World Championship in Baku at the beginning of August, she had started lowering her weight to 57 kilograms two months before the tournament. If she hadn't managed it, she would have had to wrestle against opponents 4 kilograms heavier; Gisler finished the World Championship in tenth place. Family members in the Brüel sports hall in Einsiedeln say that during tournament week, wrestlers are nervous, hungry and have a short fuse, so one should hardly speak to them.

Soon the tournament begins. One wrestler runs laps in a hoodie before stepping onto the scale again, others warm up with strength exercises and headstands to prepare their muscles, joints and especially their necks for the strain. The experienced ones demonstrate the exercises, and the youngest, 6- and 7-year-old girls, imitate them. And on the long benches next to the three wrestling mats, battle braids, tight to the head, are braided. Gisler's battle braids were braided by her sister, a dancer.

## Sexual harassment via mobile phone

That an international tournament exclusively for women is taking place in Einsiedeln is thanks to pioneers. Eveline Lötscher organized the tournament alongside a full-time job; she was also previously on the national squad and now wrestles for the Einsiedeln wrestling club. «I want to inspire more women and girls for this male-dominated sport,» she says. Once a month, with the Women's Only Wrestling Training in Zurich and soon also in Bern, she creates a protected space for female wrestlers, where technique, strength, self-confidence and mutual support are central.

Lötscher's predecessor in the fight for greater visibility in women's wrestling is Nadine Pietschmann. For fifteen years, she was a top wrestler, participated in twelve World and twelve European Championships, and was one of the first women to complete the elite sports military service. Since 2024, the 41-year-old has been the president of Swiss Wrestling, the Swiss Amateur Wrestling Association, making her one of the few women to head a Swiss sports federation.

«Today we are writing Swiss wrestling history,» Pietschmann says into the microphone at the opening of the tournament. «Fight hard, fight fair, and foster friendships beyond club boundaries.»

Just ten years ago, it would have been hard to imagine a woman leading the national association, Pietschmann says. Wrestling is the basic form of combat sports and one of the first Olympic sports; since the 2004 Athens Games, wrestling has also been Olympic for women. But in Switzerland, women were not allowed to wrestle in championship matches until 2024 and consequently played hardly any role for the clubs, she adds. «During my active time, women's wrestling was often ridiculed,» Pietschmann says. Later, there were also resistances against its development, she notes. Although female wrestlers still do not enjoy the same status as their male colleagues everywhere today, the doors of the clubs are generally open to them.

In conversations with young women, Pietschmann still hears about inappropriate remarks and messages via social networks. She herself had to listen to sexist remarks directly. Today, young women say they receive unsolicited pictures of all sorts of things after training from male club colleagues. «As an association, we are challenged here to raise awareness across genders and to set clear boundaries regarding criminally relevant assaults,» Pietschmann adds.

## The inferiority of women: A social construct

Delia Gisler, the 17-year-old wrestler from Schattdorf, was 5 years old when she explained to her parents that she wanted to wrestle like her father and godfather. At first, her parents were not thrilled, saying wrestling was «a rough sport.» Her mother wanted Gisler to wear ear protection to avoid the typical deformation of the auricle from blows and crushes, the deformation into a wrestler's or cauliflower ear. «I didn't wear it for long; it bothered me too much,» Gisler says. Her ears are still perfectly fine.

Today, Gisler trains six times a week, on and off the mat. And her godfather, Michael Jauch, is also her athletic trainer. «When Delia wanted to wrestle twelve years ago, we thought the sport had no future for women,» he says at the tournament in Einsiedeln, while his 6-year-old daughter is just running into the ring to wrestle in an Under-11 match. «For my daughter, getting started is much easier,» he adds. Now he is happy that she also wrestles. After the match, he high-fives her, saying, «Schön gsi» (Well done). «Daddy, the other one won,» says the 6-year-old and hops away to play with her friends. Victory or defeat still holds little meaning for her.

Regardless of how advanced equality may be, the belief that women are naturally passive and men aggressive is firmly entrenched, writes German journalist and author Andrea Böhm in her new book «Fighting like a Woman. The History of Women Who Fight Back.» In it, she visits female fighters on different continents and travels back to past centuries. To the suffragettes who, in smart clothes and lavish hats, fought for the right to vote in Great Britain at the beginning of the 20th century. Or to the schoolgirls in a slum in today's Nairobi who assault their self-defense course trainers with punches and kicks.

Böhm concludes that the inferiority of women is a social construct and prevents them from defending themselves. In the book, she quotes from the essay «Throwing like a Girl» by American political philosopher Iris Marion Young from 1980. In it, Young reacted to earlier statements by medical professionals, who claimed it was not possible for women to occupy lateral space, which they believed was evident in women's inability to throw. Young asserted that girls are taught from a young age to make themselves small, not to provoke, to be attractive to men, and thus never learn to take up space and protect themselves.

## Wrestling as a school of life

In Einsiedeln, a spectator wears a T-shirt with the imprint «Free Hugs»; indeed, wrestling seems like aggressive cuddling or roughhousing according to rules – a school of life. Young women cry from pain and disappointment and yet shake hands with the referees, opponents and opposing coaches after a lost match. Observers say that women are more agile and fight more precisely, even more technically. Men, on the other hand, are stronger and fight more athletically, these observers add.

Gisler enters her last match for first place as she does any other. «You make the fight,» her coach says to her with a serious face. Gisler slaps her legs, her cheeks and runs into the ring. Greets the referee, greets her opponent, a German.
