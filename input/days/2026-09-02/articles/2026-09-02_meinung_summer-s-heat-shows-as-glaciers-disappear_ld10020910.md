# This summer's heat shows: As glaciers disappear, more dams needed

*The Alps are considered a refuge for day-trippers and tourists. However, due to glacial melt and water shortages during heatwaves, they could gain far greater importance – provided their inhabitants take action.*

**Matthias Venetz, Bern** · Meinung · 2026-09-02 · 1209 words · [ld.10020910](https://www.nzz.ch/meinung/dieser-hitzesommer-zeigt-weil-die-gletscher-verschwinden-muessen-wir-mehr-staudaemme-bauen-ld.10020910)

The first sign of trouble appeared when Zermatt mountain guide Harry Lauber published an extraordinary photo of the Matterhorn early this summer. It showed torrents and waterfalls pouring down the Matterhorn’s north face. At first, people considered Lauber’s photo to be the product of artificial intelligence, but then the 78-year-old denied it, saying he had taken the picture from his house. He also said he had only seen such waterfalls once before. That was in 2022, during the last heatwave summer.

A few weeks later – in the middle of peak season – Zermatt mountain guides urgently advised against tours on the Matterhorn. The heat made the exposed mountain unpredictable, they said. Later in the summer, Zermatt mountain railways had to close the summer ski area on the Theodul Glacier to tourists. For the first time in fifty years. Enthusiastic skiers initially hoped they could simply switch to Saas-Fee, in the neighboring valley. But within a few days, the glacier there was also closed. Meltwater pools had formed in many hollows. The ski area looked like a water park.

These images can be ignored, suppressed, or dismissed as mere episodes of a heatwave summer. Alternatively, one can ask what all this means in the long term. Climate change is forcing new priorities on mountain regions.

Glacier melt is no longer merely a problem for tourism, but one for the entire country. Meltwater from the high Alps feeds drinking water pipes in many places. Industry and, above all, agriculture depend on sufficient water being available even in midsummer. Swiss glaciers are systemically critical in every respect.

In the future, the profitability of summer ski areas on Valais glaciers or the danger of tours on the exposed Matterhorn will no longer be the crucial questions. Politics and society must ask how meltwater will be used and, above all, stored in the future.

## Romanticized alpine landscape

The mountains must become a strategic area. Even if the images of melting glaciers are painful, it is primarily because the Alps have been a projection surface since the days of the Old Confederacy. They are considered the origin of the nation, their topography a symbol of Switzerland. Good confederate virtues such as modesty and love of freedom are rooted in the barrenness of the alpine soils.

Even in the age of the service society, these narratives persist. The Alps are romanticized as a redoubt of an original Switzerland and marketed accordingly.

A hundred years ago, Charles-Ferdinand Ramuz described Switzerland on the eve of the apocalypse in «Sturz in die Sonne» (Fall into the Sun). At the end of the story, people flee to the «third dimension,» into the mountains. Up there, the refugees hoped, they would find refuge, like in a second Noah's Ark.

The apocalypse is not imminent, but this summer, too, many day-trippers and tourists fled the heat to the mountains. Swiss cable cars recorded almost 30% more entries on average over five years. But this boom is deceptive.

Scientific studies show that the Alps are not spared from the effects of climate change, as Ramuz's refugees believed. On the contrary. The refuge he describes is crumbling.

The temperature increase in Switzerland compared to the pre-industrial era is twice as high as the global average. This development is more pronounced in the Alps. Winters are getting warmer, summers hotter, and glaciers smaller. The number of mudslides and rockfalls is increasing. According to a study by the National Centre for Climate Services, 50% to 90% of the glaciers are likely to have disappeared by the middle of the century. Mountain regions must prepare for this – not just for the next season.

## Dams as artificial glaciers

Again and again, Switzerland is referred to as the water tower of Europe; the Alpine glaciers are still its largest water reservoir. But when the glaciers are gone, these natural reservoirs will be lost. Alternatives are needed, and this can become an opportunity for mountain regions.

The dams in the Alps have so far been the backbone of Swiss electricity production. In the future, they will also gain importance in other areas: for the water supply of the entire country.

Ideas about what this water management could look like have existed for a long time. Researchers from ETH Zurich and the Swiss Federal Institute for Forest, Snow and Landscape Research (WSL) launched a «provocative» thought experiment – as they themselves said – ten years ago in a study: Could dams replace the shrinking glaciers?

The researchers explained that two-thirds of the missing water volume from the Alps could be compensated for by dams and artificial reservoirs in summer. At least theoretically.

In practice, everything is more complicated. The expansion of hydropower has been blocked for years – even though a round table of environmental organizations, electricity producers, and politicians has agreed on a whole list of projects. The Grande Dixence is the last major dam to be inaugurated in Switzerland. That was in the 1960s.

In response to this stagnation, Albert Rösti's Department of the Environment is looking for new projects. Alpiq proposes in this context to raise the dam wall of the Grande Dixence in Valais. This is the right approach; cantons and municipalities must now look for suitable locations for new dams or expansion projects for existing facilities. But that alone is not enough.

Already in May, the Swiss Water Association submitted a paper to the federal government, demanding national water management. According to the water suppliers, the federal government – and not, as is currently the case, the municipalities and cantons – should take the lead in the future. Jürg Grossen, president of the Green Liberal Party, recently declared that the Federal Council's plans for water management were «not far-reaching enough.» The Greens have also been demanding a shift of competencies in favor of the federal government for years.

The mountain cantons are thus threatened with a massive loss of sovereignty. In the coming years, ownership of numerous power plants will revert to the cantons. Many cantons hope for a windfall and believe themselves to be in a promising position. But this is deceptive. The pressure on them will increase.

In the upcoming discussions on water management, a rethinking is needed, first in the mountain regions and then throughout the country. Until now, dams have been considered cumbersome infrastructure for electricity production. This must change, because dams will be used multifunctionally in the future: for energy production, agriculture, drinking water supply, and flood protection. It is necessary to weigh the interests of landscape and nature conservation against the necessity of a secure water supply. After this summer, supply security must no longer merely mean that electricity supply is ensured. The term must also be applied to water supply in the future.

Below the Aletsch Glacier, the largest water reservoir in the Alps, lies the Gebidem reservoir. During the heatwave summer of 2022, operators constantly had to release water over the dam wall because the meltwater volumes were too large, but the storage capacities were far too small. The river below the wall is actually a small stream. In 2022, however, 120 cubic meters per second drained in phases, which corresponds approximately to the flow of the Aare in Bern. Except that this outflow is in a narrow gorge. This summer, the overflow at the Gebidem dam was even greater, according to Alpiq.

Already in the heatwave summer of 2022, a project titled «Oberaletsch» existed to reduce this waste. Meanwhile, the project has been resized. It is now called «Oberaletsch Klein.»

The fact that this and other projects are not progressing shows that the upcoming discussions must no longer only be about how many terawatt-hours a project yields. Instead, it must be about how much water is wasted each heatwave summer if it is not built.
