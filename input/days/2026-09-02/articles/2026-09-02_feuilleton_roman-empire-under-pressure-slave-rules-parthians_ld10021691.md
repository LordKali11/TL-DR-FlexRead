# The Roman Empire is under pressure, a slave rules the Parthians, and in Palestine Jesus preaches about the end of time: Around the year zero, the world began to falter

*History told through stories: In his new book, Moritz Aisslinger outlines the panorama of a time when Europe's foundations were laid.*

The greatest historical upheavals are often those you don't feel, even when you are right in the thick of them. You only notice them when they are over. The turning point at the beginning of our calendar, for example. What actually happened then? Not much. Nothing happened in the year zero. Because there never was a year zero. A lot happened in the years around it. But hardly anything that would have indicated an epochal shift. Contemporaries did not have the impression of living in a special time.

In Rome, Augustus had secured his sole rule and tried, as desperately as he was unsuccessfully, to regulate his succession. In Jerusalem, King Herod had one family member after another murdered, fearing he would be overthrown. In China, a nobleman seized power in a coup, became emperor, and tried to establish an ideal state. And in Palestine, a young man named Jesus traveled through the land, announcing the end of times.

Five centuries later, Dionysius Exiguus, a monk from the Black Sea region, invented a new calendar that began with the birth of Jesus Christ. He calculated his birth date from the temporal information in the Gospels and the Acts of the Apostles in the New Testament. Not quite correctly; he was a few years off. Jesus was probably born not in 1 AD, but between 7 and 4 BC. But the new calendar caught on. It was common in Europe from the 8th century on, and gradually the entire Christian world adopted it.

For Christians, the birth of Jesus Christ marked the beginning of a new era, even though the year one, with which Dionysius began the new era, was entirely unremarkable. In the decades around the birth of Jesus, however, the world fundamentally changed. Not only in the Roman Empire, which encompassed the entire Mediterranean region, but also in distant parts of the world: in Africa, the Middle East, India, Iran, and China. Moritz Aisslinger shows this in his new book.

## A gift for the king

Aisslinger is a historian and journalist. In a grand tableau, he describes developments that unfolded simultaneously in different parts of the world during the same period. The book In the Year Zero. Rome, Egypt, and a Prophet in Palestine revolves around the decades between 30 BC and 30 AD. From Rome to Jerusalem, from Africa to China, Aisslinger's gaze wanders, and he tells stories. Of rulers, upheavals, wars. Of intrigues, journeys to unknown lands, and natural disasters. Of new ideas conquering the world. Of people who lose everything, and others who find a new life.

Like Musa, who came to the court of the Parthian Empire in present-day Iran around 20 BC. A young woman, not yet twenty. A slave in the entourage of Emperor Augustus, who traveled to the Parthian king to sign a peace treaty. Decades earlier, the Parthians had wiped out three legions on the plain of Carrhae in present-day eastern Turkey. A disgrace for Rome. The adversaries had killed and captured thousands of Roman soldiers and captured the troops' standards: the legionary eagle and the vexilla (military banners).

The reputation of the invincible Roman army had suffered. Augustus had not succeeded in defeating the Parthians. And he could not afford to have them as an enemy in the long run: the Silk Road ran right through their sphere of influence. Caravans of merchants transported silk, spices, and precious stones from China and India across the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia to the West. To Rome, too.

Therefore, Augustus decided to negotiate. To create favorable conditions, he brought gifts for the Parthian king Phraates IV: gold and silver vessels, precious fabrics. And slave women like Musa. For the court's harem. Musa's origin was unknown, and at the time, nobody cared. Slaves were property like horses, furniture, or jewelry. They were disposed of and given away at will. One can only guess what feelings Musa had as she entered Phraates' palace.

## Pepper from India, silk from China

Augustus' charm offensive was successful; the treaty was concluded. The standards were returned, and the living prisoners of war were exchanged. Many prisoners, however, did not want this at all, having built new lives in Parthia. Some reportedly threatened suicide if they were forced to return to Rome.

The Parthian problem was solved. But the Roman Empire was under pressure from various sides. To the north were the Germanic tribes, who remained untamed. In 9 AD, they inflicted a severe defeat on the troops of General Varus in the Teutoburg Forest. In present-day Sudan, the Queen of Kush fiercely resisted the Roman troops. In Jerusalem, serious unrest over the succession erupted after the death of King Herod.

And Rome always had to react. With comparatively modest military means. For an area of about three and a half million square kilometers from the Euphrates to the Atlantic and from the North Sea to the Sahara, where about sixty million people lived, only twenty-eight legions and auxiliary troops were available. Just over three hundred thousand soldiers. That was barely enough to secure the hotspots.

But the world extended far beyond the Roman Empire. Trade relations with the Far East had existed for a long time. Indian pepper and silk from China were common luxury goods in Rome, Athens, or Alexandria. And in China, there were astonishing developments. The same year Varus was overrun by the Germanic tribes, a nobleman in the Chinese capital Chang’an overthrew a centuries-old imperial dynasty, seized power himself, and undertook a tremendous socio-political experiment.

## The socialist emperor

The man was named Wang Mang, had risen through the ranks to the inner circle of the court, and had become an indispensable advisor to the imperial family. When Emperor Ping died childless in 6 AD, Wang orchestrated the appointment of a nearly two-year-old child as crown prince – and himself as his advisor. Three years later, he ended the masquerade, deposed the child emperor, and granted himself absolute governing power.

As soon as he took power, Wang announced unprecedented things. Slavery would be abolished, he said, calling it inhumane. Land ownership, he declared, would be collectivized because it was unjust. Wang had all the land belonging to noble families expropriated and distributed among the peasants. He founded an imperial bank that granted loans. At state-fixed interest rates, to put an end to usurers.

Prices for everyday goods were determined by government officials. An income tax was created. Artisans, merchants, hunters, and livestock breeders had to surrender ten percent of their income. A great experiment, a radical reform. A socialism by imperial decree. However, it was short-lived. After fourteen years, Wang Mang was overthrown. His reforms were reversed. He himself had already revised parts of these reforms to avert economic chaos.

A few years after Wang Mang was killed in a peasant uprising, Jesus of Nazareth began to wander through Palestine: a Jew who promised to heal the sick and predicted a new world order. He called God his father and gathered followers around him. Jesus was not the only wandering preacher at the time. But he was the only one condemned to death by the Roman governor and who became the founder of a religion that would spread throughout the world.

## The slave woman from the West

Moritz Aisslinger tells the story of a time when the world began to falter – gently but lastingly. He presents the grand narrative in small stories. From loosely assembled mosaic pieces emerges a picture of a time when the foundations for Europe's next thousand years were laid: The Christian Church became the dominant institution of Western culture, and the organizational structures of the Roman Empire remained decisive for Europe's development, even long after the empire had collapsed.

Incidentally, Musa, the slave woman from Augustus' entourage, made a career in the Parthian Empire. Ten years after she had been presented at court as a gift, she was queen. She bore Phraates IV a son and urged him to send his older sons to Rome to make her son the heir to the throne. A few years later, she married her own son Phraatakes and ruled the empire alongside him. Phraates had been poisoned. Probably by Musa.
