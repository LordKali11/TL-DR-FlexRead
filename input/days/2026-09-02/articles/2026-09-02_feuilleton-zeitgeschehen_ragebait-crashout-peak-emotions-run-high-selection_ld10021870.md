The German words «Schere» (scissors) and «gute Käse» (literally «good cheese») were among the top 10 candidates for Youth Word 2026. They have now been eliminated. They are probably too little known to make the shortlist. Nevertheless, it doesn’t hurt to know their meaning if you want to understand young people at all.

If something is «gute Käse,» it means «really good,» «stable,» «I’m celebrating it.» It’s therefore a compliment: «Sabrina Carpenter’s song is really gute Käse.»

The word «Schere» comes from the gaming scene and means something like «my mistake.» It’s also used to apologize in everyday life: «I’m running late. Schere!»

## Youth out of control

Three English terms have prevailed. «Ragebait» is in the running for first place. «Ragebait» («rage» for anger, wrath, and «bait» for lure) stands for online content designed to trigger users, which offers greater reach: people click, comment, and share – because they get annoyed. Young people also use it to comment on the behavior of others they find stupid or provocative.

Interestingly, a second contender also points to an emotional state of emergency, as if people were particularly exposed to their own moods in the age of social media. In a «Crashout,» someone completely loses their composure and freaks out. «If my phone dies soon, I’m going Crashout.» The third contender for Youth Word is «Peak»: one experiences something as a highlight, as the best of the best: «The World Cup final was peak.»

When the Langenscheidt publishing house launched the competition in 2008, German words were still more common. At that time, «Gammelfleischparty» (spoiled meat party) took first place. It referred to parties for people over 30. In second place was «Bildschirmbräune» (screen tan), also not a very nice term for pale faces staring at computers. The word in third place sounded cozy: «unterhopft sein» (being under-hopped). That’s someone who fancies a beer.

## Speaking Denglish

The influence of English on everyday language is also noticeable in past winning Youth Words that even older people now know and use: «lost,» «Yolo,» «cringe,» «wild» (pronounced in English), «smash» (finding someone attractive), «goofy.» For «goofy,» there would be a nice old German word that hints at what is meant: tollpatschig (clumsy).

That English is becoming a second native language for young people aligns with observations of how effortlessly young people today merge German and English into one language when they talk to each other. In a single statement, they alternate English and German words and phrases that follow their own logic.

Last year, the wild combination «das crazy» was crowned Youth Word, which could reconcile cultural pessimists because it shows how language changes and how creatively it is used: «Das crazy» is a phrase used to signal that they don’t feel like answering or are speechless. «I have 1,000 new followers – das crazy.» In addition to English, the shortening and omission of words also reflects a trend.

## Canceled words

This year, too, young people between eleven and twenty years old could submit their favorite word. The winner will be announced in October at the Frankfurt Book Fair. However, the process has been criticized not for the first time. For example, the jury arbitrarily intervenes if it dislikes a word it deems discriminatory or sexist.

It did allow «hartzen» in 2009, which means «to be unemployed,» «to hang around.» Critics promptly contended that this implied unemployed people were lazy and exploiting the state. In 2015, it removed the word «Alpha-Kevin» preventively from the vote. Its meaning, «the dumbest of all,» could have offended real Kevins.

This year also saw an exclusion. The word «Mehrzweckeier» (multi-purpose eggs), chosen by many participants for the Top 10, was not admitted for the vote. Langenscheidt merely explained that the term was based on a «campaign» and stemmed «from a personal insult.»

## In defense of Friedrich Merz

The media soon provided a more detailed explanation. «Mehrzweckeier» is probably a variation of the saying «Merz, leck Eier» (Merz, lick eggs), which a student had written on a poster at a demonstration against the reintroduction of military conscription.

The police confiscated the 18-year-old’s poster and initiated investigations on suspicion of defamation of Chancellor Friedrich Merz. The altered word creation «Mehrzweckeier» only emerged when the incident went viral.

Perhaps the selection of the canceled term as Youth Word would have resulted in ragebait, led to a crashout for some, while others would have found it peak.
