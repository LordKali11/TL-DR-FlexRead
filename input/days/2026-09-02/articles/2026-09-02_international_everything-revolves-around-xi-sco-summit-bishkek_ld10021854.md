# Everything revolves around Xi: SCO summit in Bishkek reveals the growing influence of China's president

*At regional summit in Kyrgyzstan, China presented itself as a source of stability in a turbulent world, remained unimpressed by U.S. pressure to reduce support for Iran and Russia*

It is rare for Bishkek to be in the international spotlight. Kyrgyzstan's capital usually lies outside global events. But on Monday and Tuesday, the most powerful leaders of the non-Western world gathered in the city at the foot of the Alatau Mountains for the summit of the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO). Central to the event was Chinese President Xi Jinping, who used the forum to portray his country as a reliable partner and a source of stability in a multipolar world.

Also present were Russian President Vladimir Putin, Iranian President Masud Pezeshkian, and Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The meeting was held under the motto «Together for Sustainable Peace, Development, and Prosperity.» However, several SCO members remain far from peace. The Iran war is in its sixth month, and peace efforts in the war in Ukraine have largely come to a standstill.

In Bishkek, Iranian President Pezeshkian said that if the U.S. adhered again to the framework agreement agreed upon in June, Iran would also immediately comply. He insisted his country remained open to negotiations. However, no talks were currently underway. On Sunday, the U.S. attacked Iranian positions in the Persian Gulf for the first time in a month, after which Iran shelled American military bases in Jordan and two tankers in the Strait of Hormuz.

## China resists Trump's pressure

Modi said at a meeting with Putin on the sidelines of the summit: «We must move from an endless war to an end of the war.» But the summit did not point a way to peace in Iran or Ukraine. On the contrary, the meeting showed why Donald Trump found it so difficult to find a way out of the Iran war. After failing to force Iran to its knees with months of airstrikes, he was now relying primarily on economic pressure.

To effectively isolate Iran, Trump needed the cooperation of China, which was the most important buyer of Iranian crude oil. But Beijing showed no willingness to stop imports. Trump was set to meet Xi shortly and was therefore keen to avoid an open confrontation. China also did not want to bow to U.S. pressure regarding Russia. In Bishkek, Xi said at a meeting with Putin that their countries' relations would only grow stronger.

## India's record Russian oil imports

In the Ukraine war, Trump was also trying to increase economic pressure to bring Russia to the negotiating table. A year ago, he imposed punitive tariffs of 25% on Indian exports to the U.S. The goal was to force India to stop its crude oil imports from Russia. Indian refineries temporarily reduced their imports, but imports rose again after the start of the Iran war.

Since oil deliveries from the Persian Gulf had largely come to a standstill due to the blockade of Hormuz, India felt compelled to procure more oil from other sources. In July, Russian imports to India reached a record share of 50%. Meanwhile, Indian refineries were also exporting gasoline to Russia, which was struggling with shortages at gas stations due to Ukrainian drone attacks on its own refineries.

At the summit, Putin called on the other SCO members to establish their own development bank. Putin said the bank should operate independently of the Western-dominated financial system, which he argued was being misused by the West as a means of pressure. The West largely excluded Russia and Iran from the international banking and payment system. SCO members such as China, Russia and Iran have long sought to replace the dollar as the global reserve currency to break the U.S.'s dominance.

## Rivalries hinder closer cooperation

In addition to the 10 SCO members, 15 dialogue partners also participated in the Bishkek summit. Afghanistan and Mongolia were present as observers. The summit marked the 25th anniversary of the organization, which was founded in 2001 by China, Russia, and the former Central Asian Soviet republics of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan. Initially, it focused primarily on economic cooperation and the settlement of border disputes. Later, security issues and the fight against terrorism were added.

In 2017, the SCO was expanded to include India and Pakistan, Iran joined in 2023, and Belarus has been a member since 2024. Unlike other Asian regional organizations, whose activities have come to a standstill due to the conflicting interests of their members, the SCO has maintained its relevance. However, the organization is primarily a forum for dialogue. All too often, the rivalry among members prevents deeper cooperation.

The SCO includes India and Pakistan, two deeply hostile countries. China and India also have an unresolved border conflict in the Himalayas, which last escalated bloodily in 2020. Although they de-escalated the conflict in 2024, many disputes remain open. India and China are important trading partners, but the relationship is unbalanced, as the Indian economy is much more dependent on Chinese imports than vice versa.

## China's dominance also a concern

India is not alone in this dependence – the other SCO members are also economically, technologically, and in some cases militarily dependent on China. Even Russia increasingly appears as a junior partner. Many leaders of SCO member states value the summits as an opportunity to meet Chinese President and Communist Party leader Xi. Deepening trade relations and expanding transport routes are high priorities for them.

However, the Central Asian states' hope of becoming a hub between Asia and Europe has not yet been fulfilled. In addition to unresolved border disputes in the region, this is due not least to the fact that the wars in Afghanistan and the sanctions against Iran have prevented the expansion of road and rail networks and the construction of oil and gas pipelines through these countries. The Iran war now further complicates the expansion of trade routes.

Against this backdrop, China's importance as a trading partner has grown even further. However, Beijing's power also causes concern among other states. They are keen to preserve their strategic autonomy. For example, India is sticking to its partnership with Washington despite the tariff conflict. The U.S. also remains an important partner for the Central Asian republics. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio is expected to tour the region shortly. In the great power competition, even the SCO states prefer not to take sides.
