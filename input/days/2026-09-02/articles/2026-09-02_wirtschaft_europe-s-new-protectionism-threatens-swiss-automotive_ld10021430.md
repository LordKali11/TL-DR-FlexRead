# Europe's new protectionism threatens Swiss automotive suppliers – will China be the one to benefit?

*The EU wants to strengthen its key industries with new laws. Now, the industry association Swissmem warns that if the EU adopts strict rules of origin, it will jeopardize Swiss suppliers' access to the European market.*

The European Union no longer tolerates industrial competition distorted by Chinese subsidies. In early March, the EU Commission presented a draft for the Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA). The broad framework law aims to strengthen domestic economic sectors like steel and aluminum production or green technologies and secure industrial manufacturing capacities in Europe.

The automotive industry is a key focus. The goal is to protect it from growing competition from Chinese imports. To this end, the draft law relies on origin requirements for public procurement and certain state funding programs. For electric vehicles supported under such programs, strict conditions would apply: they must be assembled in the EU, and at least 70% of the value of components – excluding the battery – must be of European origin.

Negotiations on the law are underway in Brussels. However, it is becoming clear: depending on the outcome, the Swiss automotive supply industry could become collateral damage of the planned regulation. It remains unclear whether Swiss companies will ultimately be recognized as European suppliers at the end of the political process.

## Major concerns at Swissmem

«We are very concerned about the EU's Industrial Accelerator Act,» Stefan Brupbacher, director of the industrial association Swissmem, confirms. «From our perspective, this development is extremely dangerous for our automotive suppliers and the entire industrial base.»

Brupbacher says the central question is how the EU will precisely define the origin requirements in the final legal text. Opinions on this, however, appear to diverge widely. He adds that a conflict between protectionists and proponents of open markets is underway within the EU.

France, he notes, notably advocates a strict definition, pushing to limit the requirement to suppliers from the EU. This approach is based on a calculation. A strict regulation is intended to give domestic industry an advantage, while competitors from the United Kingdom or Switzerland would be at a disadvantage.

German industrial circles, for example, demand that the requirement for European origin must necessarily include Switzerland and other partner countries, he explains. They fear a significant increase in vehicle prices if supply chains are primarily limited to the EU internal market.

## Struggling suppliers

The nervousness among Swiss suppliers on this issue is not without reason. The companies are already under immense pressure. A study by the consulting firm Berylls shows that revenues for surveyed Swiss suppliers fell by an average of 13.4% in 2025.

In comparison, for the world's 100 largest suppliers, the decline during the same period was only 2.2%. Global corporations were able to stabilize their business through growth in software and electronics. Swiss companies, however, are heavily dependent on the European automotive industry, particularly on German car manufacturers, to whom they primarily supply classic precision parts. The crisis in this core market therefore hits them with full force.

The industry association Swissmem's main concern is for smaller businesses. Stefan Brupbacher says larger corporations could relocate production to the EU if necessary. But about half of Swiss suppliers are small companies that lack these alternatives. «They depend on access to the EU market,» he says.

Aargau-based component manufacturer Jehle shares the industry association's concerns. The company generates between 35% and 40% of its revenue from the automotive industry, supplying, for example, safety-critical steering components. Its most important sales market in the automotive segment is the EU.

A strict new origin requirement «would be anything but helpful for us,» Robert Reimann, CEO, says. The same applies to the entire industry. «Increased protectionism in the EU would hit the Swiss supplier industry hard,» he says – especially companies that are entirely dependent on the automotive sector.

Reimann argues that the consequence of such a tightening would by no means be that car manufacturers would buy more in the EU, as apparently hoped in France. Instead, he says, manufacturers would take a different approach: for all vehicles they build for the global market outside Europe, they would specifically look for cheaper alternatives – for example, from suppliers in Asia.

The entire European automotive industry would suffer the damage, Reimann says. «Supply chains are also innovation partnerships,» he explains. Swiss suppliers put a lot of effort into developing their products further, which then benefits car manufacturers, he adds. «If the EU isolates itself, the entire European automotive industry loses competitiveness,» the Jehle CEO warns, adding, «China would ultimately be the one to benefit.»

## Underestimated problem

Stefan Brupbacher, director of Swissmem, believes the problem is still greatly underestimated in Switzerland. The reason: Many believe Switzerland is on the safe side because it has a free trade agreement with the EU. Such countries are generally included in the draft law. However, he warns, this is «a dangerous misjudgment.»

The warning stems from the EU's dealings with other free trade partners. China, he says, is currently investing billions to build manufacturing capacities in countries like Morocco or Turkey to circumvent EU tariffs. The EU, he adds, will not watch this development indefinitely and will introduce stricter rules to prevent this circumvention.

The free trade agreement, Brupbacher explains, protects against tariffs, but not against such tightening – with the risk that, in addition to Chinese companies, Swiss suppliers will also be affected.

The State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (Seco) is closely following discussions about the Industrial Accelerator Act, a spokesman says. EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen had already commented on this law as part of the Bilateral III package, the spokesman notes. In doing so, she stated that «there is no interest in not including Switzerland,» the spokesman adds. Bern, however, is not content with this declaration of intent from Brussels. Switzerland, he says, is actively working with EU institutions and member states to ensure that «domestic companies do not suffer disadvantages.»

Jehle CEO Robert Reimann says one thing is clear: «Not only do we want to keep our supply partnerships, but also our customers in the EU.» Therefore, he says, Switzerland must be included in any requirement regarding the origin of components. However, what would be best for the competitiveness of the entire EU, Reimann contends, would be something else entirely: if Brussels did not introduce such a requirement at all.
