# «Lex UBS»: Committee decision is a step in the right direction, but not yet a liberation

*The Council of States' Economic Affairs Committee rejects the Federal Council's proposal for the big bank's capitalization. However, its own response to the Federal Council's plans also shows shortcomings.*

On Monday, the atmosphere in the Federal Palace was tense as the Council of States' Economic Affairs Committee debated future capital requirements for UBS. Finance Minister Karin Keller-Sutter left the meeting room without a word, and the Council of States members present followed suit.

Eventually, it was evening before Council of States member Erich Ettlin, president of the Economic Affairs Committee (WAK-S), appeared before the media. He spoke of a «compromise solution» that would protect the financial centre and taxpayers equally, Ettlin said.

The question of appropriate capitalization for UBS has been hotly debated for months. The committee had twice scheduled the matter for discussion and then postponed it. Now, the majority of members support a proposal that allows the big bank to back half of its foreign holdings with core capital (CET1) and half with AT1 bonds.

## Objections taken seriously by Council of States members

The decision by the Council of States members is a defeat for the Federal Council. Finance Minister Keller-Sutter wanted UBS to fully back its foreign holdings with core capital.

Economic associations and the big bank had been vehemently opposing these plans for months. They feared that this would impair UBS's competitiveness and, among other things, could lead to higher credit costs in Switzerland.

The fact that a majority of the Council of States committee is now taking the objections from the business community seriously and has rejected the Federal Council's overly strict proposal is a step in the right direction. Overly strict capital requirements for UBS ultimately harm the entire economy. Companies' concerns about higher credit costs are justified.

## Disciplining UBS with bonds

In regulating systemically important banks, the Council of States members want to shift the focus away from core capital and toward AT1 bonds. The proportion of core capital required to back foreign subsidiaries will only be slightly increased from the current 45% to 50%. Instead, AT1 bonds will play a more important role. These are financial instruments that can either be written down or converted into equity capital in the event of a crisis.

However, to ensure these are not deployed only when it is already too late for a bank, the committee wants to make changes to the bonds. This is positive. For example, the committee proposes that AT1 bonds be activated if UBS's equity capital falls below a higher threshold than is currently customary.

If this scenario occurs at the big bank, the AT1 bonds primarily have a disciplining effect on UBS and its management. It may then no longer pay coupons, distribute dividends, or carry out share buybacks. In addition, bonuses would be reduced.

If UBS fails to meet the capital requirements again within six months, the next stage is mandatory. The big bank must carry out a capital increase, or it must make an offer to its AT1 creditors to exchange or convert the securities. For this, it must create so-called «contingency capital» even before a crisis.

## What do AT1 bonds with a «Swiss Finish» offer?

That the Council of States is taking a step toward UBS is to be welcomed. However, the revamped AT1 instruments bring new problems. In their current form, AT1 bonds are widely accepted internationally, and there is a liquid market for these financial instruments. Especially in a crisis, this can be additionally helpful.

If the AT1 bonds are indeed modified as the Economic Affairs Committee wants them to be, this type of financial instrument will only exist in Switzerland. Whether international investors will understand how the bonds actually work remains to be seen. The same applies to whether there is any demand at all for AT1 bonds with a «Swiss Finish.»

Otherwise, there is a risk that the Council of States members will create a new financial instrument that nobody wants and that is so expensive for UBS that ultimately there is hardly any difference from core capital. That cannot be the purpose.

Next, the committee's proposal will be debated in the Council of States during the autumn session. It could also get heated here. At least, this is suggested by the Economic Affairs Committee's votes on the individual variants. These were sometimes very close. Not only left-wing, but also center-right Council of States members were in favor of stricter capital rules for UBS. Proponents of a 50:50 rule for the big bank therefore still have a lot of work to do.
