# «If a young chef is hyped up by Gault-Millau, starts with 15 points and charges 300 Swiss francs for the menu, people get skeptical,» says top chef Didi Bruna

*Many chefs are only interested in self-realization, according to the top Zurich restaurateur. Now he is retiring.*

****

A few minutes' walk from Central, on the inconspicuous Stampfenbachstrasse, lies a top address for gourmets: «Didi's Frieden,» a restaurant awarded 15 Gault-Millau points. Didi Bruna has been a fixture of Zurich's gastronomy scene for decades – besides his cuisine, he became known for his handwritten menus and the small chocolate drawings on his dessert plates.

In a few weeks, Bruna will be retiring, and he has decided to close his restaurant.

**You have been a restaurateur in Zurich for over 40 years. Now you are calling it quits – why?**

I have to close. The property where «Didi's Frieden» is located is being renovated. There will be a restaurant again, but with conditions that no longer work for me and my head chef, Markus Furtner.

**What conditions?**

The owners are breaking through the wall in the dining room and are planning for 100 seats. That's far too many for us. We currently have 50 seats inside and 50 outside, which is optimal.

**But isn't more space in the restaurant a good thing?**

Not for us. We cook with fresh ingredients, and the kitchen is already very small for that. After the renovation, it will stay the same size, but the storage rooms will be smaller because they want to add more apartments. Whoever cooks here in the future will be relying on chain catering or installing a pizza oven. It won't work for our style of cuisine. The owners actually wanted to start four years ago, but I was able to negotiate with them to wait until my retirement. Now, it's over on Sept. 30; four days later I turn 65. For me, that works out. Even if we are very busy right now. Everyone wants to come visit us one last time. Someone is constantly wandering into my kitchen to say goodbye. I can't get anything done anymore.

**In recent years, many gourmet restaurants have had to close: «Elmira» in Zurich, «Skins» in Lenzburg, «Trübli» in Winterthur. Is fine dining in crisis?**

It's quite astonishing: «Elmira» always had good reviews, and suddenly the restaurant is gone. But with fine dining, that's how it is: If the restaurant isn't in the right location, if you don't have the right personality, or if the concept isn't right, it gets difficult. Unfortunately, it's not enough to just be a great cook. On top of that, the value for the price has to be right. Even 20 years ago, young chefs were hyped up by the critics, then you'd go eat there and think, yes, that was special. But also expensive. A year later, the restaurant was closed.

**So what is a good concept?**

Offering what the guests want. Sure, with big names like Andreas Caminada and his peers, the guests travel from all over. But he also had to establish himself first. When a young chef is hyped up by Gault-Millau, starts with 15 points and charges 300 francs for the menu, people get skeptical. Only a few guests are prepared to spend that much money. Many cooks simply don't take the guests' wishes into account. They only want to do what they think is good.

**So self-fulfillment in gastronomy doesn't work?**

It's difficult. What's the use of plating with tweezers if the guest doesn't leave full but has spent a lot of money? That way, no one leaves the restaurant satisfied. And then they just don't come back.

**How important are Gault-Millau points to you? Your restaurant has also been recognized, with 15 points.**

Yes, and they are a nice recognition. But at the same time, guests then arrive with expectations. Everything has to be perfect. That's why I don't have any signs or points on the door today. I want people to come to us without any preconceptions. I'm shaped by an experience I had as head chef at the Neumarkt restaurant.

**What happened back then?**

We had a huge garden for 250 guests. We couldn't have cooked for so many guests without semi-prepared products. A critic came by; I knew him well. Of course we gave him the best table, and I spontaneously cooked him a six-course meal that wasn't on the menu like that. After that, we had 14 points. I thought, this can't be true – for this kind of simple eatery. If a normal guest comes in now, there's no way they can make sense of that rating.

**Many chefs report feeling great pressure because they are afraid of making a mistake when the critics visit.**

I have friends in Michelin-starred gastronomy. I witnessed some of them having burnouts. The whole ratings business has also destroyed livelihoods. That's why I just cook to my own taste. I also never did any paid advertising. For me, the most important thing is that the guest leaves satisfied and tells others that they had a good meal.

**There are said to be chefs who look for a patron – so they can do exactly what they want.**

I know a few such patrons. And I can tell you: They also want to see black ink at some point.

**In many high-point and Michelin-starred restaurants, guests have to order at least a seven-course meal, but would prefer to eat less.**

It's true: Many people these days prefer to order individual dishes from the menu. It was particularly bad during the pandemic: A couple would sit there with two plates of ravioli, drink tap water and discuss their relationship problems until closing time. Maybe they would order a verbena tea as well. We didn't make any money from that. So we thought of something new.

**Which was?**

In the evening, a three-course meal is the minimum, but people can put together the dishes from the menu. With an amuse-bouche, that costs 85 francs. And if someone doesn't feel like dessert, we serve them a glass of sparkling wine to start or a digestif as an alternative. We are happy when people take their time to eat. Unfortunately, that is increasingly rare. It has to be quick, especially at lunchtime. People rush in at 12 o'clock, and an hour later they're gone again.

**Was it different in the past?**

Oh, yes. In the 1980s, I was the head chef at the «Blaue Ente» near Tiefenbrunnen station. At every table, the ad men were drinking a bottle of Gaja, Margaux or Château Mouton-Rothschild even at lunchtime, and eating a multi-course meal that went on late into the afternoon. Those were the golden days. Then the recession came in 1992, and everyone switched to tap water. The banks suddenly cut back on expenses.

**So the opulence has been over for a long time.**

I wouldn't say that in such general terms. We tend to have older guests, who perhaps deliberately behave differently from what the mainstream dictates. Like on the evening of Jan. 8, we had just reopened after the New Year's holidays. The first two tables didn't drink a drop of alcohol. I was already thinking: Uh-oh... Dry January is striking! But then suddenly every table was ordering red wine, white wine – really going for it! It seemed like an act of defiance to me.

**You once said the «Kronenhalle» was a role model for you. Why?**

In 1983, I joined the «Kronenhalle» as chef entremétier (station chef for vegetables, soups and starches), back when Ruedi Weber was the head chef. At that time, the legendary landlady Hulda Zumsteg was still alive; I even saw her a few times. I was immediately fascinated by how people in that establishment treated each other. I have never seen a restaurant where the cooks or waiters stayed for 30 or 40 years. Back then I told myself: If I open my own place, I don't want to close it down right away either. Of course, one can argue about the cuisine at the «Kronenhalle.» But certain dishes still work to this day. I go there once a year. I like that consistency.

**But you don't always have the same dishes on the menu, do you?**

No. We change the menu every six weeks. I love new things. I could come up with different menus every week. But guests often don't appreciate that. That's why I'm cautious and sleep on a menu three times before deciding. I had to put cordon bleu on the menu at my restaurant to please the owners. I did, and to my chagrin it became the best-selling main course. Today I have it on the menu in winter; I can't get rid of it.

**In the fine-dining segment, chefs like Andreas Caminada of Schloss Schauenstein or Peter Knogl at «Les Trois Rois» are treated like stars. Is that good or bad for your industry?**

The image of chefs has improved; in the past, people automatically thought we were all hot-tempered. But actually, we chefs are rather introverted. When I opened my first restaurant on Asylstrasse in Zurich in 1998, I realized: Now you have to play the boss and show yourself to the guests. That was unusual for me. Today I find it wonderful. I get to know so many people I wouldn't meet in another life. Members of the cantonal government and City Council come to my restaurant. City President Raphael Golta once said in an interview that «Didi's Frieden» was his favorite restaurant. I was very pleased about that.

**Let's talk about food. What do you like to order in a restaurant?**

I love hearty cuisine. Sausage with potato salad – wonderful. I'm a meat eater, much to the chagrin of my partner, who is less fond of it. But we can work things out. Sometimes I also go to a top chef and let myself be pampered.

**And at home?**

At home, I like it simple. Pasta and salad, that's all it takes. When I have guests, I often prepare the food at the restaurant. Like recently, I seared a rack of veal here in the kitchen and finished it in the oven at home.

**Many people get nervous when they host guests at home. What simple dish can you use to impress visitors?**

A good meal starts with the right preparation. You should go shopping early and then do a mise en place; that's the be-all and end-all. Otherwise things get stressful and the guests have to wait. Anything you can slide into the oven is a good bet. Then just fry up some spätzle in a pan on the side, and you're done.

**Which dish do amateur cooks make the most mistakes with?**

With risotto – it always sounds so simple, but it really requires a delicate touch. The rice can quickly get mushy. You have to have made it at least a thousand times before you can do it with your eyes closed.

**You now have only a few weeks left at «Frieden.» Is there a dish you absolutely want to present to your guests one last time as a farewell?**

We recently changed the menu, and that will stay until the end. Our head chef Markus Furtner's Wiener Schnitzel is on it. It's the best in all of Zurich, but nobody has noticed yet (laughs).

**What's next for you?**

A friend of ours found a new place for Markus Furtner in Uster. He's opening there at the beginning of November. I'm helping him with the wine list, because I still have so much stuff in stock. I'll help him out a bit at the new place. When a new place opens, it's always hectic. You need all hands on deck then.

**You're not taking a vacation?**

Yes, I am. In January, I want to go away with my partner for a month, maybe to South Africa. I haven't been away for that long since «Didi's Frieden» opened. And when I did take vacations, I would check my phone at least once a day to see if there was anything wrong with the restaurant. I'm looking forward to doing nothing for a while. Although, the last time that happened, it didn't agree with me at all.

**What happened?**

When I closed «Didi's Bistro» in 2001 after three years, I fell into a deep hole. I completely underestimated what it means to no longer have a purpose. Luckily, after two months, Michel Péclard called me, whom I didn't know at the time. He asked me if I wanted to help out as head chef at the «Münsterhof.» I really liked it there. I ended up staying for half a year, much longer than originally planned. But now it's really over. Goodbye, door closed, that's it.
