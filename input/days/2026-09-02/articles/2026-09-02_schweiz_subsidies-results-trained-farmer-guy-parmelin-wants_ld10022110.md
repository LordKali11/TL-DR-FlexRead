# Subsidies for results: Trained farmer Guy Parmelin wants farmers to think like entrepreneurs in the future

*Agricultural Policy 2030: Economic affairs minister seeks new mindset for agriculture to address climate change*

Federal President Guy Parmelin has had many jobs. As a young apprentice, he toiled for a year on a farm near Lake Murten. Later, he became a vintner and pressed grapes. For seven years now, he has been the Minister of Economic Affairs. And as he lectures on future agricultural policy this Wednesday afternoon, he sounds as if he has a new job: business coach.

Parmelin promises farmers «more entrepreneurial freedom» and «less bureaucracy.» But he also demands more responsibility from them. He explains what opportunities new technologies would bring, but hopes for «more efficiency» and «increased productivity» from them. His goal is to promote sustainable production and thereby generate more profitability – not only for farmers, but for the entire agricultural production chain.

The basis for the business case Parmelin outlines this Wednesday afternoon is Agricultural Policy 2030 plus, which the Federal Council adopted and sent for consultation a few hours earlier. Specifically, the Federal Council wants to reform the system of direct payments for Swiss farmers. In the future, they should no longer receive their funds if they meticulously follow federal regulations, but if they achieve predefined goals. The results in the fields and on the arable land should be decisive.

Today, Swiss agriculture is a highly subsidized industry. Parmelin’s new agricultural policy will do little to change that. In addition, it faces a multitude of problems that have been exacerbated this hot summer. Parmelin says, however, that the Federal Council’s reaction to this is «not a break» with previous policy or even a «revolution.» Primarily, the Minister of Economic Affairs is prescribing a new, market-oriented mindset for the agricultural and food industry: «Farmers must see themselves as entrepreneurs.»

Initial reactions, from the left to Parmelin’s own party, the Swiss People's Party (SVP), have been critical – and for a variety of reasons.

## Hot summer exacerbates situation

In recent weeks, hardly a day passed without new reports of problems for Swiss farmers. Due to the drought, the army had to fly water to the Alps in the canton of Fribourg. In the meadows of the lowlands, grass barely grew. This resulted in a fodder shortage that forced farmers to slaughter their animals.

In addition to the consequences of the hot summer, many other problems have plagued agriculture for years. Dairy farmers complain about milk prices that are too low, while buyers complain about full warehouses and an anachronistic overproduction. Environmental organizations and conservationists criticize the pollution of soils and waters. They therefore demand stricter regulations, which, however, lead to more bureaucracy and frustrate farmers.

With the realignment of direct payments – «the result in the field is decisive, not the path to it» – he hopes for a reduction in bureaucracy. Instead of documenting every work step in forms, farmers should upload their data to the new «agridata» platform. From there, it should be possible to exchange data easily among farmers, labels like Bio Suisse, and the federal government.

Furthermore, the Federal Council wants to improve the position of farmers in the markets: target prices of industry organizations should be published in the future, and their calculation also disclosed. At the same time, the Federal Council wants to continue supporting the milk price, as parliament has already demanded. It also wants to increasingly promote plant-based products for human consumption.

To enable farmers to adapt to climate change, the Federal Council plans advisory services. These services should inform them about modern cultivation methods, insect protection measures, and new technologies. With target agreements for retailers, the Federal Council wants to further boost sustainable food production. In its communiqué, the Federal Council writes that this would also enable all actors in the value chain, including consumers, to take on their responsibilities even more strongly.

Apparently, the population’s shopping list is also part of Parmelin’s new agricultural policy.

## Small farmers against economic affairs minister

But: Are these measures sufficient after the past summer? Parmelin reacts slightly annoyed to the question and says: «For twenty years, I repeat, for twenty years, politics has been taking measures to adapt to climate change.» Investments are being made in soil fertility, in resistant varieties, etc. In the so-called «Climate Strategy Agriculture and Food,» for example, the Federal Council lists over forty corresponding measures. A little later, Parmelin explained that he did not rule out that the Federal Council would still «make improvements» on «certain points» in this area.

This is exactly what environmental organizations and the left will demand from him. WWF, Pro Natura, Greenpeace and Birdlife Switzerland wrote a little later in a joint communiqué that the Federal Council’s reaction to climate change currently focuses on short-term relief. «Hay imports from Canada may be a temporary solution, but they are hardly a sustainable model for the future.» The associations therefore want to demand «fundamental reforms» in the consultation process.

While the Social Democrats (SP) praised Parmelin’s approach of extending responsibility for agricultural policy to the food industry and wholesale trade, they complained that the measures did not go far enough. The Swiss People's Party (SVP), Parmelin’s own party, however, criticized precisely this point and wrote of an «eco-bureaucracy.»

However, the Small Farmers' Association criticized the Federal Council’s plan most clearly. In a communiqué, it states that the Federal Council is «actively promoting farm closures.» The reason for this: In the valley floor and the so-called «hill zone,» the Federal Council is increasing the requirements for receiving direct payments. Mountain farmers are exempt from this tightening. According to the Federal Office for Agriculture, more than 2,000 small and micro-enterprises would thereby lose their entitlement to direct payments. This corresponds to about one in ten farms, the Small Farmers' Association says.

However, the Federal Council is granting small farmers a transition period. Parmelin said that the transition would «certainly not be easy,» but would not happen overnight. However, he said, small farmers were being clearly signaled that they now had to find solutions, utilize synergies, and, for example, enter into cooperations within a village.

These somewhat cumbersome formulations can actually be summarized in one word in the language of a business coach: structural change.
