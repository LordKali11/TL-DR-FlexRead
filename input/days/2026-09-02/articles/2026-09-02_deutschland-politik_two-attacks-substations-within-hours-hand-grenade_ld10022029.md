# Two attacks on substations within hours, hand grenade at train station: Germany’s critical infrastructure is vulnerable

*The incidents in Brandenburg and North Rhine-Westphalia illustrate how difficult it is to secure critical infrastructure comprehensively. Attacks on it are likely to increase in the future.*

Within a few hours, unknown perpetrators carried out two suspected attacks on the German power grid. In Brandenburg, saboteurs deliberately caused short circuits on high-voltage lines at the Turnow-Preilack substation in the south of the state. A short time later, a short circuit also occurred at a substation near Bergheim, west of Cologne.

Police assumed a «deliberate act.» Investigators later found six launch devices. The devices were likely designed to guide a wire over the power lines using pyrotechnic objects, the Chief Criminal Director said.

North Rhine-Westphalia's Interior Minister, Herbert Reul, previously announced that an investigation into sabotage against the state was underway. Anyone who «goes after that» is attacking Germany, he said.

An incident at Augsburg’s main train station on Wednesday morning also raised questions. An initially unknown object exploded there – reportedly a hand grenade, according to the Bild newspaper. Several people sustained minor injuries. The explosive incident in Augsburg was likely not a targeted attack on infrastructure, initial media reports indicated. Investigators were examining a possible link to organized crime.

A connection between the three cases was not yet apparent, although the launch devices in Brandenburg and North Rhine-Westphalia were reportedly almost identical. Together, however, the three cases highlight a weakness: critical infrastructure is difficult to protect comprehensively against sabotage.

## 321 suspected sabotage cases

The investigations in Brandenburg were the most advanced. At Turnow-Preilack, unknown individuals had prepared several homemade projectiles. The perpetrators likely intended to use them to introduce conductive material into high-voltage lines, triggering a short circuit. This succeeded in two instances.

One line failed briefly, and a 500-megawatt block of the Jänschwalde power plant was temporarily shut down. However, the population's power supply remained stable. By Tuesday evening, investigators had found further explosive devices. Brandenburg's Interior Minister Jan Redmann spoke of a systematic approach.

Investigators were also examining the case on suspicion of forming a terrorist organization. In addition, police were investigating disruption of public services, causing an explosive detonation, and destruction of essential equipment.

German security authorities had long been concerned about an increasing number of suspicious incidents involving attacks on critical infrastructure. According to an internal situation report by the Federal Criminal Police Office, 321 suspected sabotage cases were recorded nationwide last year. These included incidents at railway facilities, power lines, substations, radio masts, and German armed forces facilities. Not all of them were later confirmed as sabotage.

At the same time, the security situation had changed. The Office for the Protection of the Constitution had long warned that Russia also recruited individuals for acts of sabotage who did not belong to an intelligence service and were deployed for individual, comparatively simple tasks. Such so-called disposable agents can scout facilities or carry out acts of sabotage. This was part of the general threat situation but not an indication that Moscow was behind the recent incidents.

## Threat from Russia and left-wing extremists

However, the drone attack in Leipzig and the German government's realization that Russia was behind it showed that sabotage attempts and attacks in Germany were likely to increase in the future.

Similarly, Germany repeatedly experienced politically motivated attacks on power and rail infrastructure from left-wing extremist circles. In early January, an arson attack on power facilities in southwest Berlin caused a power outage lasting several days. In June, around 30,000 people were temporarily without power after a fire at a substation in Reutlingen.

Unlike, for example, Soviet infrastructure, German infrastructure is predominantly designed for peacetime and therefore cannot be protected comprehensively. Power lines, railway lines, or pipelines stretch over thousands of kilometers – seamless monitoring would be extremely costly. And so, protection must be particularly high where a failure would have particularly severe consequences. At the same time, the infrastructure must be designed so that damage is limited even after a successful attack.

## Act on the protection of critical infrastructure

The incident at the Turnow-Preilack substation showed that this could succeed: although the perpetrators managed to trigger two short circuits, there was no major power outage because the German transmission grid was able to absorb the short-term failure.

Nevertheless, improved protection of critical infrastructure in Germany would become necessary. According to experts, the Critical Infrastructure Umbrella Act, which only came into force in March, was not yet sufficient to meet the demands. Among other things, it provided for risk analyses and minimum requirements for the physical protection of critical infrastructure.

It also required operators of these infrastructures to implement resilience measures such as stronger site protection, emergency teams, or provisions for maintaining supply. The Act covered particularly important facilities that supplied more than 500,000 people. Critics demanded that this limit be reduced to 150,000. They also contended that operators' willingness to spend additional funds on the security of their facilities would only be present if requirements were legally stipulated.

Furthermore, the practical implementation of the new Act was still largely pending. The Federal Office for Civil Protection and Disaster Assistance pointed out that several legal ordinances and concrete guidelines were still being developed. For example, it had not yet been conclusively regulated which facilities fell under the Act.
