An armada of blue-collar workers in yellow helmets cycles along the village street on rickety bicycles. Their destination: the gate of the Hongrun Petrochemical oil refinery. Here, in Weifang, eastern China, they are working on expanding the plant, say several construction workers returning from their lunch break.

«It should be finished by April next year,» says a man in a grey work uniform. His sleeve bears the name CNCEC, a state-owned construction company specializing in the chemical industry. Business is apparently good, at least so far.

Hongrun, a former subsidiary of the state-owned company Sinochem, now operating independently, is one of those small Chinese refineries that energy experts at the American analysis firm Energy Intelligence suspect specialize in Venezuelan oil. The company has invested in facilities for processing heavy crude oil with high residues – ideal for the high-sulfur oil from Venezuela. Locally, no one can or will say where the oil processed here comes from.

A request for comment from the company went unanswered. Sinochem, which still holds a 13 percent stake in Hongrun, stated that it is committed to compliant business practices and «strictly adheres to the laws and regulations of the countries in which the company operates.»

Here in the eastern Chinese province of Shandong, one of the country's most important refining centers, the plan is to process not only sanctioned Venezuelan oil, but also oil from Iran and Russia. This oil is also subject to American sanctions – and therefore much cheaper. However, the US intervention in Venezuela and a possible intervention in Iran jeopardize this business.

While three large state-owned companies dominate the Chinese oil market, they leave the sensitive, sanctioned crude oil business to small, private refineries, jokingly referred to as «teapots» by commodity traders. But how independent are these «teapots» really? And why does the Chinese government allow them to operate with impunity?

## High discounts due to American sanctions

Until the US military attack on Venezuela and the abduction of its leader Nicolás Maduro, China was the main buyer of oil from the Latin American country. Last year, the People's Republic received approximately 395,000 barrels of oil per day from Venezuela, according to estimates by industry experts at the Kpler analysis firm. One barrel of crude oil contains approximately 159 liters.

Since the US imposed sanctions on oil trade with Venezuela in 2019, many countries have avoided deliveries. As a result, the oil was offered at steep discounts. Now, the US government intends to manage Venezuela's oil reserves to its own advantage and sell them at market prices. How this will affect China's supply contracts remains unclear.

Although China's oil market is dominated by the three large state-owned companies China National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC), China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC), and Sinopec, around two-thirds of imports from Venezuela went to small, independent, mostly privately owned refineries. To avoid repercussions from American sanctions, the state-owned companies largely leave this sensitive business to private competitors. According to industry experts, these private companies account for up to one-fifth of China's refining capacity.

## «Teapots» most affected by the US intervention in Venezuela

The «Teapots» are therefore most affected by the potential impact of American influence in Venezuela, writes Michal Meidan, director of the British research firm Oxford Institute for Energy Studies, on the LinkedIn platform.

Even if Venezuela continues to supply oil to China, the previously high discounts are likely to disappear with US approval. This would make the oil less attractive for companies, which typically operate on slim profit margins. However, private refineries are known for their bargain hunting. They could switch to other sanctioned crude oils, such as those from Iran, Meidan suggests.

However, the recent unrest in Iran and a possible US intervention jeopardize this alternative. This is because China is also the most important buyer of Iranian oil.

## Driving 1000 kilometers for cheap oil

The fitting name «Teapot» becomes clear on this sunny January day in Weifang: next to the spherical metal tanks used to store the oil, white smoke rises into the deep blue winter sky – like from a teapot. A network of pipes stretches between the tanks and chimneys.

Heavy tanker trucks thunder down the village road towards the north entrance of the Hongrun refinery. «I come here because it's 55 cents cheaper per liter,» says a driver at the Hongrun gas station right next to the factory gate.

He delivers the oil to the central Chinese province of Hubei, about 1000 kilometers away. He drives the route every two to three days. The journey takes 24 hours.

The owner of a small kiosk on the village street emphasizes how important the factory is for the local economy. «The workers buy cigarettes, water, and alcohol here,» she says. However, they tend to buy cheaper brands. «Life is hard, business is bad everywhere.»

The teapots are also suffering from lower demand from the domestic industry. When asked about the impact of world politics, the kiosk owner just laughs. That's much too far away, she says; ordinary people don't concern themselves with such things. «We're focused on doing our jobs and earning a living,» she says.

## Important center for oil processing

Shandong Province is one of China's most important oil refining centers and a significant petrochemical cluster. This has historical reasons: In the northeast of the province lies the Shengli oil field, the second largest in the People's Republic. The oil is sulfurous, although not as much as Venezuelan oil. The oil field is operated by the state-owned company Sinopec.

Independent refineries developed in parallel with the development of Shengli in the 1960s. To overcome resistance from local governments, the central government allowed them to capture oil leaking from pipelines and build refineries to process it, according to a 2017 analysis by the Center for Global Energy Policy (CGEP) at Columbia University.

Beijing's attempts in the 1990s and 2000s to expand its control over the oil market, reduce overcapacity, and drive the «teapots» out of business failed. This was because local governments protected the companies, which are important employers and taxpayers in the region.

In 2015, the central government changed its policy and, for the first time in a while, allocated quotas for imported crude oil to independent refineries. This was intended to increase the pressure for reform on the sluggish state-owned companies. Experts at Columbia University describe a «love-hate» relationship between the large state-owned companies and the «teapots.» On the one hand, both sides see each other as competitors. On the other hand, the private companies also sell their refined products to the state-owned enterprises.

## Sanctions over processing Iranian oil

Venezuelan leader Maduro visited Shandong several times, most recently in 2023 during a state visit. He said at the time that he hoped for «direct cooperation with local state-owned enterprises and private companies in Shandong,» as these were «very complementary» to his country's industry.

The «teapots» play an important role not only for Venezuela. Last year, the US government imposed sanctions on four independent Chinese refineries. These refineries are alleged to have purchased and processed hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of Iranian crude oil, including oil from shipments sent by sanctioned vessels, the so-called «shadow fleet.» Three of the companies are based in Shandong: Shouguang Luqing Petrochemical, Jincheng Petrochemical, and Shengxing Chemical.

A Shengxing plant is located just under a 30-minute drive from the Hongrun refinery. The plant grounds are secured with a semi-transparent barrier that allows a glimpse inside. It doesn't appear as if they have anything to hide. Tanker trucks are parked in front of the entrance, waiting their turn.

## Port operators also subject to sanctions

In 2025, the US also imposed sanctions on three port operators at the Dongying port. The US Treasury Department accuses them of having received more than one million barrels of Iranian oil. Neither Shengxing Chemical nor the port operators responded to requests for comment.

The journey from the refineries in Weifang to the Dongying port in the Bohai Bay takes about an hour and a half along the well-maintained, four-lane highway. Tanker trucks travel in both directions. To the left and right of the road, numerous red nodding donkey pumps, typical of oil production, move slowly up and down.

Access to the port itself is blocked by a checkpoint. «Build an iron coast, defend the nation with a great wall, ensure healthy economic development,» reads a nearby sign. Oil rigs and a cargo ship are visible at sea. Oil storage tanks stretch along the coast as far as the eye can see.

## Important transshipment point for the shadow fleet

As a result of American sanctions, the number of so-called «dark calls» at the port of Dongying has risen sharply, wrote experts from the shipping logistics publication «Lloyd's List» last year. These calls involve ships switching off their GPS tracking systems before entering the port. The increase in these blackout actions suggests that Dongying is a «growing transshipment point for sanctioned oil.»

The US's access to Venezuelan oil is unlikely to have an immediate impact on China – partly because the country has already secured reserves. According to industry experts at Kpler, almost 82 million barrels of Venezuelan oil are currently stored on tankers in the waters off China and Malaysia. A quarter of this oil originates from Venezuela.

Furthermore, at least a dozen sanctioned tankers carrying Venezuelan crude oil have left the country since the beginning of the year – despite the US blockade in place since mid-December. This was reported by the Reuters news agency, citing documents and information from industry sources.

Most of the ships in this shadow fleet are sailing the high seas without flags or safety documents. About half of them are supertankers, typically transporting Venezuelan oil to China.

It is unclear whether the US would have permitted these shipments. At a press conference following the American intervention in Venezuela, US President Donald Trump emphasized that the US also wanted to sell the oil to existing customers. However, he did not mention China by name.

## Pushing back China's influence

While many Western companies withdrew from Venezuela as a result of American sanctions, Chinese oil companies remained active in the South American country. This was viewed critically by the US government. One of the reasons for the military intervention in Venezuela was to limit investments by rivals in the country's oil industry, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in an interview with the American broadcaster NBC.

China's biggest concern after the US intervention is therefore less a question of energy security and more one of geopolitical consequences, writes energy expert Meidan. Should contracts with Chinese companies be terminated and these companies forced out of the country, it would be «a massive loss of face for China.»
