# In Europe and the US, calm prevails despite enormous energy prices; in Asia, Eastern Europe and Africa, however, alarm bells are already ringing

*The oil and gas shortage is directly affecting many countries – and concerns about food security are rising. Some governments are taking countermeasures.*

The earthquake in the Persian Gulf has led to economic turmoil worldwide. The main cause is the drastic increase in oil and natural gas prices. These commodities are both the lifeblood and the lifeblood of many economies.

However, the high prices and the looming shortage are currently not primarily affecting the countries of Western Europe, the USA or China, but above all states in Eastern Europe, Asia and Africa.

Until recently, roughly one-fifth of global oil and gas was transported by ship through the now largely impassable Strait of Hormuz between Iran and the United Arab Emirates. A large portion of this oil went to India, South Korea, Japan, and other Asian countries, in addition to China. However, the first effects of the energy crisis are already being felt in Eastern Europe and Africa.

The consequences and countermeasures vary: Japan has released part of its strategic oil reserves, small businesses in India are running out of gas, fuel prices are being capped in Croatia, and concerns about food supply problems are growing in Africa, as NZZ correspondents report.

## The energy crisis is hitting Southeast Asian countries hard: India and Pakistan are taking austerity measures

In India, restaurants are running out of cooking gas, in Pakistan there are fears of payment problems due to increased import costs, and in Bangladesh the government has closed universities to save electricity. The energy crisis is hitting South Asia hard. The region is economically intertwined with the Arab petrostates. Millions of migrant workers in the Gulf come from India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh. Above all, India and its neighbors are highly dependent on oil and natural gas imports from the region.

India imports nearly 90 percent of its crude oil, and up to 50 percent of these imports must pass through the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran has closed due to the war. While only 60 percent of India's liquefied natural gas (LNG), which it primarily uses for cooking, is imported, 85 percent of these imports must navigate the strait between Iran and Oman. It was cooking gas that first experienced supply shortages.

Media outlets across India are reporting that restaurants are reducing their menus, street food vendors are halting operations and canteens are closing. The reason is a shortage of gas cylinders. Furthermore, the price of cooking gas has risen dramatically. Desperate restaurateurs are trying to buy on the black market, but cylinders are in short supply there as well.

Gas sales in India are state-regulated. Even under normal circumstances, supply is tight. Restaurateurs are now calling on the government to take action against price gougers. Restaurant associations are advising their members to soak rice, lentils, and other pulses before cooking to reduce cooking time. Unlike Europe, India has hardly any underground storage capacity. There are also shortages of natural gas for auto-rickshaws, which have been running on it for environmental reasons for several years. And there are also looming problems with fertilizers, which are important for agriculture and are often produced from gas.

Warnings have already been issued that the energy crisis could jeopardize food supplies. In Bangladesh, the most crucial time for rice planting is beginning. In the country of 176 million people, the energy situation is even more dire than in India. At the start of the war, Bangladesh's oil reserves were sufficient for only two weeks.

To reduce electricity demand for air conditioning and gasoline consumption, the government sent students on vacation. It mobilized the army to protect gas stations and oil depots.

The situation in Pakistan is hardly better. The country of 250 million people on the Indus River has been dependent on aid from the International Monetary Fund for years. The rise in import prices is now straining the state budget. The government is considering a multi-billion-dollar emergency fund to cushion consumers against further price shocks. While India has so far managed to keep gasoline prices at the pump stable, they have already risen sharply in its western rival.

This puts the government in Islamabad, which maintained good relations with Trump, in a delicate position. As has been seen repeatedly in Iran, rising gasoline prices can easily trigger unrest.

India has at least been granted a reprieve. Following a telephone call between Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, two Indian gas tankers were allowed to pass through the Strait of Hormuz, escorted by Indian warships.

## America's allies Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan are feeling their dependence on imports from the Gulf

The three East Asian allies of the U.S. – Japan, South Korea and Taiwan – are extremely vulnerable to disruptions in the energy market. These countries share a risky mix: They are not only important manufacturers of high-tech products for global supply chains, but they also have to meet almost all of their energy needs through imports. And energy is vital for the survival of their industries.

The severity of energy market disruptions depends on the extent to which individual countries rely on oil and gas imports from the Gulf region. Japan is most vulnerable in the long term with regard to oil. The country purchases over 90% of its oil from the crisis region. A small portion of Saudi oil is exported via the Red Sea, but 75% to 80% must pass through the Strait of Hormuz.

To prepare for short-term disruptions, Japan has long built up reserves of more than 240 days. This buys time, but the longer the oil supply is cut off, the more critical the situation becomes. South Korea and Taiwan, on the other hand, obtain more oil from other sources: South Korea is still 67% dependent on the Gulf region, while Taiwan is only 36% dependent. The chip manufacturing powerhouse off the coast of China has drastically shifted its oil purchases from the Gulf states to the U.S. over the past 10 years.

Japan and South Korea have reacted most strongly to the crisis. Tokyo has already released a month's worth of its oil reserves for the domestic market to curb the rapid rise in oil prices. South Korea's leadership has introduced a cap on gasoline prices and is closely monitoring oil refineries to ensure they do not exploit the crisis for unjustified price increases.

The situation is different with gas. While the three countries are among the world's largest importers of liquefied natural gas (LNG), with Japan and South Korea ranking second and third behind China, their sources of supply are more diversified.

South Korea and Taiwan are the most dependent on LNG from the Gulf region, each at around 30%. In Japan, by contrast, only 11% of LNG comes from the Gulf states. Japan has also hedged its position with two additional measures: A large portion of deliveries was secured via long-term contracts, making the country less dependent on the spot market.

The three countries have considered the consequences of gas shortages beyond electricity and gas supplies. All three have built up huge reserves of fertilizer to prevent agricultural collapse. Japan can supply its farmers with fertilizer for 100 days, South Korea for 80 days, and Taiwan for 70 days.

The crucial question is to what extent the three countries can reduce their gas consumption through increased coal-fired or nuclear power generation. Taiwan is in the worst position: The government shut down its last nuclear power plant last year. South Korea could increase its nuclear power generation by 5% to 10% by postponing planned maintenance work.

Japan currently has 18 reactors that the country could theoretically restart. However, little is possible in the short term. Tokyo could benefit, though, from the fact that the power company Tepco restarted one of its own reactors in February for the first time since the Fukushima nuclear disaster 15 years ago.

## In Eastern Europe, countries are reacting with price caps, and in Ukraine, agriculture is feeling the effects of high energy prices

The price shock following Russia's major attack on Ukraine hit Eastern Europe particularly hard in 2022. While the current air war in the Gulf is not comparable to the Russian invasion, and energy prices have not yet risen as sharply as they did then, the experience nevertheless shapes our view of the present situation.

Some countries have introduced significantly stricter measures than Western European countries like Germany, France, and Italy. Croatia, for example, introduced a government-imposed price cap on fuel last Tuesday. According to the conservative Prime Minister Andrei Plenkovic, this cap is 17 cents below the market price for diesel. The Croatian government has repeatedly intervened in energy market pricing, both during the COVID-19 pandemic and after the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Now, this measure is being reactivated.

The Hungarian government also imposed price caps last week. In addition, taxes on fuel are being reduced. The regulation also stipulates that only vehicles registered in Hungary are eligible for the reduced prices. These measures come against the backdrop of parliamentary elections in four weeks. Energy security is a key campaign issue, especially since Prime Minister Viktor Orbán escalated the dispute with Ukraine over a damaged pipeline.

In Poland, the national-conservative opposition is demanding a reduction in the value-added tax (VAT) on fuels from 23% to 8% and submitted a corresponding draft law. The liberal government of Donald Tusk has not yet responded, but stated that it would revisit the issue should the situation worsen.

In Romania, too, there is a debate about taxes. Although the country is the most important oil producer in Southeast Europe, it has the highest fuel prices in the region due to its heavy tax burden. For the time being, the government plans to increase production capacity. Serbia has imposed an export ban on crude oil and its derivatives. However, exports declined sharply last year due to American sanctions. Price controls have been in place in Serbia since 2022.

Even in countries that have not yet taken drastic measures, there is considerable concern. In Ukraine, the crucial agricultural sector is feeling the effects of high energy prices most acutely. Fuel accounts for 10% to 15% of production costs, depending on the type of grain. Added to this are increased transport costs and logistical challenges. The Middle East is one of the main markets for Ukrainian agricultural products. However, Kyiv's greatest concern is not the increased costs themselves, but rather the additional revenue they generate for Russia. High energy prices are funneling billions of dollars into the Kremlin's war chest.

## East Africa fears disastrous consequences for fuel and food supplies in the event of a prolonged war

In East Africa, around 80% of gasoline and kerosene comes from the Gulf States, according to figures from the consulting firm S&P Global. The region is therefore threatened with a fuel crisis. Two main reasons could lead to a situation where no fuel reaches countries like Kenya soon. Firstly, the Strait of Hormuz, the sea route, is blocked. Secondly, the infrastructure in the Gulf States is damaged.

One of the large oil refineries in Abu Dhabi, from which countries like Kenya and Uganda had sourced gasoline, was hit in the war and had to be shut down. Other refineries have also temporarily halted production for safety reasons. Experts are warning of an impending fuel shortage from the end of the month. «If the war continues until mid-April, we will face disastrous consequences,» says Edgar Odari, head of Econews Africa. Reserves are already being brought to the market, he added.

So far, gasoline prices in Kenya have remained stable – and with them, the basic cost of living. But as soon as gasoline prices rise, food prices will skyrocket because they are often transported long distances by truck to the capital, explains Laura Muniafu, an independent consultant in the oil sector. Around 75% of Kenya's imported crude oil goes to the transport sector. «The war shows our dependence.»

The situation is similar in other countries in the region. Ethiopian President Abiy Ahmed called on the population on Monday morning to conserve and ration gasoline. «Even though we are not involved in the war, we are paying part of the price,» Muniafu continued. If the war drags on, oil becomes scarce, and prices continue to rise, countries like Kenya will be left behind, Odari believes. The remaining oil will then go to the highest bidder.

Of the 54 countries on the African continent, 48 import more oil than they export. The major exception is Nigeria. After Libya, it possesses the largest oil reserves on the continent. And since the beginning of 2024, the country has also been able to process crude oil into gasoline. Billionaire entrepreneur Aliko Dangote financed a refinery that is now one of the largest in the world and could potentially meet the entire fuel needs of West Africa. Nigeria is slowly becoming independent of the international oil market.

Nevertheless, gasoline prices in Nigeria have risen several times since the outbreak of the war in Iran, most recently over the weekend. In the medium term, Nigeria could benefit in the coming weeks if oil prices on the world market rise due to shortages.
