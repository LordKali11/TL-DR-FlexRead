# From Trump's tariffs to wage protection: Helene Budliger is Parmelin's woman for the delicate missions

*The State Secretary for Economic Affairs had a difficult start. But with her unconventional approach and perseverance, she has come a long way.*

**Tobias Gafafer (text), Annick Ramp (images)** · Schweiz · 2025-03-10 · 1850 words · [ld.1873921](https://www.nzz.ch/schweiz/von-trumps-zoellen-bis-zum-lohnschutz-helene-budliger-ist-parmelins-frau-fuer-die-heiklen-missionen-ld.1873921)

Helene Budliger Artieda enjoys long-distance hiking in her free time. She and her husband have already hiked from Porrentruy to Chiasso, from Rome to Apulia, and from Rorschach to Spain. «I like having to push myself,» she says. «Walking around a lake or up a mountain isn't my thing.» The State Secretary finds it easiest to unwind on strenuous long-distance hikes. «After ten kilometers, something usually starts to hurt.»

Professionally, the 59-year-old operates similarly. «I'm someone who speaks up when things get difficult,» she says. The director of the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) holds a prominent position: she is responsible for numerous, sometimes explosive, dossiers – from foreign trade and sanctions to arms exports and the labor market. When she took up her post, she was tasked with one of the most challenging jobs in Bern. She leads the domestic political negotiations on the accompanying measures to the free movement of persons. The goal is to maintain wage protection at its current level, even though Switzerland had to make concessions in the negotiations with the EU.

Budliger encountered a deadlock. The relationship between SECO and the unions was strained – so strained that the unions temporarily boycotted the talks. The social partners aired their dispute publicly. But in recent months, she has achieved something remarkable. In February, she was able to announce, together with Federal Councillor Guy Parmelin (SVP), that the social partners and the cantons had agreed on a catalog of primarily technical measures to safeguard wage protection. These measures are intended to make it easier to extend existing collective bargaining agreements that are generally binding.

The agreement is fragile. But Budliger is on the right track. She is highly committed, having personally attended thirty out of fifty meetings with the social partners. She wanted to know how wage protection works. She took the time to meet with the joint committees responsible for its implementation. This is appreciated – especially by the former critics of SECO.

«She was the first one who wanted to understand what was going on,» says Daniel Lampart, chief economist of the Swiss Federation of Trade Unions (SGB). Budliger and her chief official, Valérie Berger, who is responsible for the dossier, were a positive surprise in every respect. The SECO director has no airs and an unorthodox manner. While other state secretaries would summon the SGB leadership, she simply dropped by the union's office.

Budliger also showed up when the Swiss Federation of Trade Unions (SGB) finalized its position at a delegates' meeting in January. She arrived late and took a seat at the back of the hall. SGB President Pierre-Yves Maillard greeted her warmly, first from the podium, then with a handshake and kind words. The relationship between the unions and the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) hadn't seemed this good in a long time. Budliger's predecessor, Marie-Gabrielle Ineichen-Fleisch, was a skilled trade diplomat. She was less interested in the minutiae of the accompanying measures and never really connected with the unions – perhaps she never even tried.

## Crisis manager in Thailand

Budliger says she deliberately applied for the position of State Secretary to be closer to domestic politics. Parmelin's mandate was clear: «He wanted the relationship between the social partners and SECO to improve again.» Moreover, after seven years as Switzerland's ambassador to Thailand and South Africa, she had experienced the international stage extensively. «I met with ministers, kings, and presidents.»

Budliger spent three years in Bangkok, a post considered demanding due to the large number of Swiss expats. «I loved it,» she says. Because of the coronavirus pandemic, she experienced little of «normal» Thailand. She was almost constantly occupied with crisis management. Initially, the task was to bring thousands of tourists back to Switzerland on commercial flights and later on repatriation flights.

Then Thailand closed its borders. «We had cases of pensioners who were visiting Switzerland and couldn't return, even though they lived in Thailand.» For those who had stayed behind, Budliger and her team tried to organize vaccinations. The average age of the Swiss community is high, and many vulnerable people with pre-existing conditions are among them. After Bangkok, Budliger knew it was resilient.

Abroad, she learned that dialogue and genuine interest in the subject matter can take you far. She also applied the «Budliger method» to discussions about wage protection. «I told myself, I'll just give it a try.» She knows how important body language and atmosphere are. «That gets you further than poring over theoretical books.» Budliger sees herself as a facilitator but doesn't hesitate to contribute her own ideas.

«You can tell she's an experienced negotiator,» says Lampart. Budliger has mastered the whole repertoire, from praising to applying pressure. «She tests what works best.» Budliger is direct, but not hurtful, says Roland Müller, the director of the employers' association. «She does that very well.»

Budliger also addresses uncomfortable issues. When it became clear that the EU would not grant Switzerland an exemption regarding expense regulations, she announced a national solution to the media in February. «If a company comes to Switzerland, it must comply with Swiss legislation. Period.» And when the Federal Council announced the conclusion of negotiations with the EU in December 2024, Budliger appeared more composed than others. She admitted that there had been some setbacks regarding wage protection. Explicitly, and in front of rolling cameras, she admitted what the government didn't want to say. Why can she afford to do that?

«Look, I’m turning sixty this year,» says Budliger. «I’ve always been the way I am, and I’m not going to change.» They knew that when they hired her. If a security deposit is only required in cases of repeated violations of wage protection, that’s a step backward. She merely observed this, she didn’t pass judgment. That’s why compensatory measures are needed. The Federal Council, too, has always pursued the goal of maintaining the current level of wage protection. She had to remind the social partners that everyone wanted the same thing.

Budliger's plain speaking was well received by the unions. Nevertheless, she is not pandering. In February, the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) announced that Jérôme Cosandey would be taking over the leadership of the important Directorate for Labour. The Swiss Federation of Trade Unions (SGB) reacted angrily, calling it a provocation. Cosandey, as a leading figure at the liberal think tank Avenir Suisse, had advocated free-market radical positions such as the abolition of wage protection.

Budliger says she chose the best candidate. Cosandey had a reputation, like all the other shortlisted candidates. She hints, in her own way, that filling the position wasn't easy. «The Romansh-speaking woman, who is intimately familiar with the labor market and wage protection and whom everyone praises, unfortunately didn't apply.»

## From secretary to state secretary

Budliger's style is as unconventional as her résumé. She graduated from business school in Zurich, started as a secretary at the Federal Department of Foreign Affairs (FDFA), moved to the consular service – and worked her way up to State Secretary. She attributes this success in part to the Swiss education system. «In many countries, you don't become State Secretary unless you've graduated from an elite university.» Her academic background is modest – which drew criticism upon her appointment.

Budliger says she had opportunities and seized them. Her career was only possible thanks to the support of her husband, whom she met while working at the embassy in Peru. Budliger also owes a great deal to several superiors who recognized her strengths. The then Foreign Minister Micheline Calmy-Rey (SP) appointed her head of the Directorate of Resources, the first woman to hold the position, at the age of 43.

She was responsible for a billion-dollar budget, staff, and real estate. It was also unprecedented that she came from the consular service and had never led a mission abroad. She didn't shy away from making difficult decisions and adapting the organization, which caused unrest among the staff.

## The dispute with Miller

Budliger succeeded in breaking the ice with the social partners. However, in diplomacy, too much directness can also be detrimental. The State Secretary took up her post just a few months after the Russian invasion of Ukraine. The section responsible for sanctions was understaffed and hopelessly overwhelmed. SECO had 8,000 unanswered emails from companies requesting information.

In an interview with the NZZ , Scott Miller, the American ambassador to Bern, personally attacked Budliger. He expressed his hope that she would continue to examine the matter with the utmost care. «However, some of her comments worry me somewhat because she questions the effectiveness of sanctions.»

Miller was referring to a podcast from French-speaking Switzerland in which Budliger had indeed expressed criticism of the effectiveness of sanctions. She cited her experiences in sanctioned Cuba, her second posting as an assistant at the Federal Department of Foreign Affairs (FDFA). She said she had the impression that the sanctions had actually contributed to prolonging the Castro regime. Budliger stands by her statements to this day. «I wanted to show what the reality is,» she said. She insisted that this did not mean she wasn't doing her job.

Miller has since left Bern. Donald Trump has other priorities. His trade disputes with numerous countries are causing economic unrest. Switzerland has «a very good narrative,» says Budliger. «For years, we've been doing what President Trump wants.» She points to the high level of investment by Swiss companies in the US. This creates good jobs for Americans and pays high wages. Despite the recent security and geopolitical turmoil, she still considers a free trade agreement desirable. Switzerland must explore whether this is possible. Perhaps it will have to be content with reducing trade barriers in one sector, as has been achieved in the pharmaceutical industry.

## Budliger's recipe for success

Budliger's track record in free trade is already impressive after two and a half years. In 2024, she succeeded in negotiating a trade agreement between India and the EFTA states. It is the biggest success since the free trade agreement with China. Budliger is doing what a good leader does: she attributes the achievements to her well-established team.

But business representatives say the agreement with India wouldn't exist without Budliger. She seized the opportunity when the then-ambassador to Delhi, Ralf Heckner, called her in Thailand – before she even started at SECO. Heckner recognized early on that India was opening up and that a window of opportunity existed. Budliger met the Indian Minister of Trade at the G20 summit in Bali, where she was representing Parmelin. The first conversation was difficult.

She traveled to Delhi seven times. «After a meeting, you have to be prepared to get back on a plane three weeks later,» she says. She succeeded in building a relationship with the minister. That is considered crucial in India. When Budliger recently visited Delhi with a Swiss delegation, the Indian minister devoted an entire afternoon to them, despite the parliamentary session.

Budliger has a knack for dealing with people, whether they are ministers from India, CEOs of large and small companies, or trade unionists. Her strengths will be in high demand in the coming weeks. By the end of the month, she and the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) aim to finalize the concrete measures to safeguard wage protection. Then it will become clear whether the agreement reached by the social partners holds. Budliger also plans to travel to the USA soon. Neither she nor Parmelin have been there since the new administration took office. The next step is something Switzerland did exceptionally well under Trump's first administration, and something Budliger also enjoys: building contacts – step by step.
