# India is buying lots of crude oil from Russia. Who really benefits?

*Indian oil refineries are financing Putin's war against Ukraine – and certain Western sanctions have an absurd, and dangerous, side effect.*

**Erika Burri** · NZZ in English · 2025-01-28 · 1759 words · [ld.1867852](https://www.nzz.ch/english/india-is-buying-lots-of-crude-oil-from-russia-who-benefits-ld.1867852)

> Summary
> -  India is helping turn Russian crude oil into diesel and gasoline for the global market.
> - Despite Western sanctions, Indian refineries are benefiting from Russia's reduced oil prices and Reliance Industries is among the major beneficiaries due to cheap Russian oil.
> - While Russia earns about $10 billion a month from oil exports to India, the Indian population does not directly benefit from the crude oil deal.

India is helping ensure that Russian oil continues to be processed and reaches the world market in the form of diesel and petrol. The oil is transported on rusty, poorly maintained tankers. Every nautical mile they travel is a risk to the environment.

But what does India gain from this shady business, in which one of the richest men in the world is also heavily involved? Who on the subcontinent benefits? And does the Indian population also benefit from this crude oil deal?

Since the end of February 2022, Russian crude oil is no longer allowed to be exported to Europe by sea. This is the result of the sanctions that were imposed following Russia's attack on Ukraine almost three years ago. The idea was to prevent Russian head of state Vladimir Putin from financing his campaign against Ukraine through crude oil exports.

It is now clear that little or no Russian crude oil is coming to Europe. However, heating oil, diesel, aviation fuel and other petrochemical products made from Russian oil do indeed find their way into the EU countries – and most likely also into Switzerland.

India is an important hub for this trade. Shortly after sanctions were introduced, the share of Russian crude oil imports to India increased. In hindsight, it was a significant jump.

While Russian oil accounted for less than 2% of Indian oil imports before the attack, a few months later it made up around 40% of imports. Since then, oil imports have stabilized at a high level.

## Indian refineries benefit from price reductions

Indian refineries initially benefited from Russia's price reductions on its oil due to Western import bans. From the end of 2022, however, Indian refinery operators took advantage of another sanction.

This second sanction was a compromise: Western countries decided on an upper limit of $60 per barrel for Russian crude oil on the world market. This was supposed to reduce Russian income. Western politicians also wanted to use the measure to prevent price surges and shortages on the crude oil market.

This would have had an impact on gasoline prices and, as a result, would have driven up inflation – a tricky issue for politicians because high inflation can cause instability and, as in the US case, can also help decide elections.

The way this second sanction was set up, only oil tankers that sell their oil for less than $60 per barrel would qualify for insurance. The West believed it could enforce this price because 90% of shipping insurers are based in the West. Without insurance, it is assumed that no shipping company would dare to send its tankers out to sea.

But the West made its calculations without reckoning on India – or Russia. Moscow bought old tankers, which are now transporting crude oil to Indian ports in order to circumvent the sanctions.

## Russia has high revenues thanks to India

Thanks in part to India, Russia earns around $10 billion a month from crude oil exports, according to estimates by the Kyiv School of Economics, which cites current trade data and data from the International Energy Agency. That's roughly Putin's monthly budget for his war against Ukraine in 2025.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 9f8267c303898ae5bcb59486c5143fd6]

Russian oil is transported from the Baltic Sea ports of Primorsk and Ust-Luga and the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk on ships that should have been decommissioned long ago. They are sailing under the flags of dubious states and without insurance to cover accidents and damage. They call at various ports in India, but they often unload their cargo in the Gulf of Kutch in the northwestern state of Gujarat. This is not a coincidence.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 161cd517db048a316b465e1c4621e477]

It is not known exactly how many ships belong to the Russian shadow fleet. Estimates range from 400 to 1,400. They all cross the Baltic Sea or the Black Sea, then the Mediterranean, the Suez Canal, the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean.

Many of these tankers even circumnavigate the entire Indian subcontinent and then head north towards Malaysia and China. Like India, China is also buying record quantities of Russian crude oil and is considered Russia's main customer. In contrast to India, however, China was already a major importer before February 2022. China also mainly uses Russian oil itself, instead of «whitewashing» it for re-export into the European market, as India does.

## China also benefits from low prices

China uses cheap Russian oil to meet domestic demand and thus manages to keep fuel prices low. The state controls the oil market and subsidizes diesel and petrol. As a result, prices in mid-January were around $1.10 per liter of petrol, below the global average of $1.15. Petrol is cheaper in China than in India, where a liter costs around $1.17 – and that's with a per capita income that is just 20% of China's. China has capped its own refinery capacities.

India, on the other hand, has expanded its refinery capacities due to cheap Russian crude. The country has to cover 85% of its crude oil requirements with imports. However, with 22 refineries – 18 of which belong to the state – India has many times more refined product capacity than it needs for its own requirements. Petrol, diesel and other petrochemical products have been an important export sector for years.

Petroleum products manufactured in India account for around a fifth of India's total exports. Reliance Industries, the Indian conglomerate that operates the twin refineries in Jamnagar in the Gulf of Kutch, has even foregone maintenance work to produce more petrol and diesel for the global market in the past three years. Satellite images show how construction is continuing in the vicinity of the refinery.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 161cd517db048a316b465e1c46231b0f]

Together, the two Reliance refineries constitute the largest refinery operation in the world. Reliance Industries is also the largest private company in India. Its boss and main shareholder is Mukesh Ambani; he has an estimated fortune of $100 billion. In 2019, his eldest son celebrated a three-day wedding in St. Moritz, which is said to have cost $110 million. Reliance earns its money primarily from petrochemicals and oil production.

India has a total refinery capacity of around 5 million barrels per day, soon to be 6 million. The giant Jamnagar refinery will probably soon account for almost a third of this capacity.

Reliance Industries, and thus the Ambani family, is therefore one of the major beneficiaries of cheap Russian oil reaching India in a questionable manner. Furthermore, every tanker in this shadow fleet is basically a ticking time bomb. Experts believe it is only a matter of time before one of the ships causes an environmental disaster.

## Tankers directly affected by sanctions

The latest sanctions from the US target tankers from this Russian shadow fleet. In mid-January, 183 ships were added to the sanctions list. This means that they will no longer be allowed to call at ports and be serviced or refueled on their way to Asia.

The US is less concerned with environmental protection than with trying to minimize Russia's profit from the oil trade, after the moderate success of previous sanctions. Indian refineries have announced that, going forward, they will no longer work with ships affected by sanctions. However, thanks to a transition period, tankers that have already been booked are allowed to unload their cargo in India.

In the meantime, trade relations between Russia and Reliance have become more official: In mid-December, it was announced that the Russian oil company Rosneft and the Indian megarefinery in Jamnagar had signed a trade agreement.

Starting on Jan. 1, 2025, 500,000 barrels of oil have been shipped daily from Russia to India, which is processed in the Jamnagar refineries on the Gulf of Kutch. A 10-year contract was signed, with an option to extend. It is regarded as the largest energy agreement between the two countries.

## Narendra Modi praises the oil deal

In fact, the second largest refinery in India, Vadinar, not far from the Jamnagar twin refineries, is half-owned by the Russian oil company Rosneft. Russian crude oil was already being processed there before Russia's attack on Ukraine. This area is also being expanded in order to increase capacity.

State refineries, such as the Mangalore Refinery can also process Russia's sulfurous oil and have considerable capacities. The Indian energy specialist Probal Ghosh says the private and state refineries are among the big winners in the Russian-Indian oil business. According to some estimates, all Indian refineries together bought almost half the crude oil transported by the Russian shadow fleet last year.

Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi traveled to Russia in November. The raw materials contract was certainly a central point of discussion at Modi's meeting with Putin. During his visit, Modi praised the new line of business. Thanks to Russian support, India is able to supply its large population with energy, while other countries are facing severe crises in the energy market.

## The Indian population does not benefit directly

Modi argued that Indo-Russian oil trade had helped stabilize the turbulent global energy market. The world must accept this. Modi, who professes neutrality in the Russian war on Ukraine, is covering up the fact that India is financing Russia – and thus supporting its war of aggression.

Energy expert Ghosh notes that the Modi government is not willing to pass on the lower purchase prices of crude oil to the wider Indian economy. According to Ghosh, state-owned oil companies have been able to restructure financially after difficult years, thanks to higher profits. In addition, the state is skimming off more taxes thanks to the oil processors' flourishing business. It is money that the state urgently needs.

However, according to Ghosh, ordinary Indians do not benefit directly from the Russian-Indian oil trade. This is occasionally criticized in India. Otherwise, the country has established itself as a major beneficiary of the sanctions. This is because India needs oil for its growth, and in large quantities.
