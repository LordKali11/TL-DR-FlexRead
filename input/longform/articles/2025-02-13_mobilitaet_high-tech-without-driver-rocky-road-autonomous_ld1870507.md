# High-tech without a driver: The rocky road of autonomous buses in public transport

*Public transport companies want to densify their route networks and serve outlying areas with shuttle buses accessible via app. These vehicles are intended to be autonomous and driverless, as staff is scarce and expensive. However, the future of local public transport is delayed.*

Since 2017, the pioneering project has attracted experts and camera crews from all over the world to Bad Birnbach in Lower Bavaria. In the spa town, and later extending to the train station located outside the town, the first public bus service using autonomous minibuses operated.

Small, seven-seater electric vehicles, occasionally derided as «golf carts with a roof,» navigated a nearly three-kilometer course without a driver. However, the operator, the «safety driver» required by current legislation, was always on board – in case the software and sensors necessitated emergency human intervention during the leisurely drive at a maximum speed of 17 km/h.

Autonomous traffic currently operates worldwide only with special permits and supervisory personnel. The so-called robotaxis of Google subsidiary Waymo are actually driving without drivers in San Francisco; further test operations are also underway in China.

In Bad Birnbach, the future has come to an end, at least for now. The autonomous shuttle buses were taken out of service at the end of the year. The French vehicle manufacturer Easymile wants to withdraw from the expensive passenger transport business.

Without further technical support from the supplier, the project partners, from Deutsche Bahn to municipal institutions, Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München and the renowned Fraunhofer researchers, did not feel confident enough to continue operations in Bad Birnbach.

Money had also become scarce, and further funding is uncertain. After all, the driverless bus service cost six-figure sums annually. What happens next is unclear. For now, there will be a return to traditional bus service with drivers.

This demise is not an isolated case. A report by the international research and development service provider IDTechEx notes that the number of robo-shuttle developers worldwide has halved in recent years – to just twelve players. High costs, empty public coffers – and the remaining risk of whether and when driverless bus driving will actually be safe on public roads are dampening enthusiasm.

For example, automotive supplier ZF Friedrichshafen withdrew. «The high upfront investments are no longer justified, as the market is developing more slowly than expected,» ZF stated. Competitor Schaeffler is also no longer operating scheduled shuttle services. In 2023, it had announced a cooperation with the Dutch bus manufacturer VDL.

Germany's Federal Ministry of Transport, by contrast, refers in its strategy paper «The Future Drives Autonomously» to statements from experts. They reportedly expected «savings of 30% to 40% in scheduled service» once the vehicles and their components reached market maturity.

## Actual route buses are soon to drive autonomously

Not everyone is giving up. A new beginning with the next generation of vehicles is starting in Burgdorf, in the Hanover region. The «Albus» project aims to put three 50-seater vehicles on the road. These are intended to navigate city traffic at speeds of up to 40 km/h, initially with a driver and later autonomously. These are not boxy cabin scooters like the first prototypes, but rather typical electric city buses from the Turkish automaker Karsan, upgraded with American high-tech for autonomous driving. The first vehicles of this type are already operating in Norway, also with accompanying personnel.

In the canton of Zurich, Swiss Federal Railways (SBB) is trying to improve connections between rural areas and the S-Bahn (suburban rail network). This year, a pilot project is set to begin in the quiet Furttal valley , encompassing the municipalities of Otelfingen, Boppelsen, Hüttikon, and Dänikon. A computer-controlled on-demand bus system will offer a taxi-like service in these villages, with fares based on public transport prices, transporting passengers from their doorstep to the nearest station on the S6 line between Zurich and Baden. However, instead of a bus, a Japanese mid-range car, equipped with an electric drive and autonomous driving technology – without a safety driver – will be dispatched. The technology is supplied by the Chinese company Weride from Guangzhou.

The metropolis of Hamburg is thinking on a different scale. For years, the city has been testing the «Alike» project to significantly increase the market share of public transport, the so-called modal split, in order to reduce traffic congestion. As spokesperson Julia Lindemann succinctly puts it: «Every car that remains parked and unused counts.»

One idea here is to make access to the bus and train networks easier by providing feeder services. The ambitious goal: No Hamburg resident should have to walk more than five minutes to the nearest public transport option. Customers booking on demand should be able to board or alight at several thousand shuttle bus stops distributed throughout the city.

In a three-year trial phase, funded by the Federal Ministry of Transport with a double-digit million-euro sum, the «autonomous on-demand ridepooling» service will start with up to 20 vehicles in an operating area of 37 square kilometers.

Ridepooling means the computer-controlled bundling of ride requests and is thus a modern form of shared taxi. With each booking, passengers are to be picked up for a shared ride thanks to computer assistance: The project partners, above all the Hamburg public transport company Hochbahn, hope this will encourage a significant number of customers to switch from cars to buses and trains. Will it work?

Experts like Professor Barbara Lenz, long-time head of the Institute of Transport Research at the German Aerospace Center (DLR), give pause for thought: «Not everyone who uses ridepooling ultimately ends up using traditional public transport. A pilot project in Berlin showed that only about half of the passengers actually used the shuttle as a feeder service, while the other half used the comfortable transport option like a taxi to reach their destination, even though it was more expensive than a ticket for bus and train.»

## Hamburg bets on autonomous minibuses

The service is intended to operate at Level 4 autonomy in the future. This means truly driverless operation, with monitoring from a central control room. The Hamburg shuttle service is divided into two parts. One partner is the Volkswagen Group, which, through its technology subsidiary Moia, will initially deploy up to ten electric ID.Buzz vans.

The second operator is the Hamburg public transport company Hochbahn itself, using vehicles from the Paderborn-based company Holon, a subsidiary of the automotive supplier Benteler. Holon is also the name of the second vehicle type for the test phase in Hamburg, praised by its manufacturer as an «autonomous mover» with global market potential. The battery-electric vehicle offers space for 15 passengers, is barrier-free and is designed to travel at speeds of up to 60 km/h in city traffic.

Ultimately, the goal is to deploy several thousand vehicles in Hamburg. Barbara Lenz sees the fundamental challenge for transport companies as simply obtaining autonomous vehicles for everyday public transport operations: «Anyone who doesn't mention large numbers won't impress the automotive industry.»

Jan Brandstetter, an expert on autonomous bus operation at the Association of German Transport Companies (VDV), confirms: «The German bus industry has some catching up to do. The major manufacturers of our city buses have too little interest in developing autonomous vehicles for the transport sector. We must be careful that we don't lose our engineering spirit in this field in Germany.»

Indeed, the market – lasers, lidar, sensors and software – is firmly in American and Chinese hands. «Here in Germany, the same manufacturer's technology is always tested in pilot projects. There's no competition between the systems,» observes Lenz. Given the driver shortage, she says, automation of public transport would be desirable. Without it, denser schedules and extensions into nighttime hours and weekends will continue to be delayed.

Hardly any major city in Germany has not jumped on the autonomous bus bandwagon, and the same is true in Switzerland, for example in Sion, Freiburg, Zug and Schaffhausen. There are numerous projects with futuristic-sounding names – almost always Anglicisms. But all of them are still only in the experimental phase and sometimes feature imaginative ideas. For example, «platooning»: A conventional bus leads the way, and driverless vehicles follow behind via a kind of virtual drawbar to offer passengers more space on the route.

Trials are also underway off public roads, on university campuses and around the Charité hospital grounds in Berlin. Zurich Airport is now planning something similar. It intends to establish an internal staff shuttle service using a nine-seater «robobus,» also employing Chinese Weride technology.

The era of pilot projects using small, slow vehicles, such as those in Bad Birnbach, the village of Keitum on the holiday island of Sylt, or the town of Monheim am Rhein between Düsseldorf and Cologne, appears to be drawing to a close. Enthusiasm is waning everywhere.

This is not only due to the withdrawal of manufacturer Easymile and the high costs of the systems, but also to the realization that «we need a level of quality for the shuttles that is not yet universally available,» says VDV representative Brandstetter. «For example, the vehicles must already be capable of operating at Level 4,» he demands. Sensible operating and business models for autonomous driving are an opportunity to maintain adequate mobility in aging societies despite the shortage of drivers, he added. However, this will not come without a price: «The question will be how much this is worth to us and what we can afford.»

No one knows exactly when buses will actually be operating without drivers. Optimists repeatedly express their views, such as the head of the German Federal Motor Transport Authority, who boldly claimed in a newspaper interview: «By 2026, or at the latest 2027, self-driving robobuses will be operating in the first cities in Germany.»

The ADAC, which is generally sympathetic to automobiles, remains cautious: «The technology to be developed by car manufacturers, as well as the legal situation on the part of the legislature, were and are apparently more complex than previously thought.»
