# Trump tariff shock threatens to tarnish shipowners' golden era

*Too many ships for too little cargo? MSC, Maersk and other giants of the world's oceans are facing exactly this nightmare scenario. Their recent successes were already on shaky ground before the United States' sweeping new tariffs.*

**Benjamin Triebe, Rotterdam** · NZZ in English · 2025-04-10 · 2476 words · [ld.1879272](https://www.nzz.ch/english/us-tariffs-add-to-mounting-worries-for-shipping-sector-ld.1879272)

> Summary
> -  After years of strong profits, the container shipping industry faces troubles after building more ships than are needed to handle current freight volumes. 
> - A collapse in world trade due to U.S. tariffs and other countries' countermeasures would exacerbate the industry's overcapacity problems.
> - Some shipping companies are moving to a new hub-and-spoke operating model that is designed to speed overall delivery times.

There was no shortage of good wishes at Adrian's christening. Dozens of invited guests offered their blessings from the speakers' rostrum. «May you bring prosperity and pride to all!» «May your travels be peaceful and successful!» «May you make all those who travel with you happy and provide them with safety!»

That's a long list. But the Adrian Maersk is also a large container ship, which – on that bright spring morning in the port of Rotterdam – had a bottle of Bollinger Special Cuvée broken across its bow.

The freighter can carry more than 16,000 containers filled with T-shirts, car parts, computers, medicines or anything else that needs to cross the world's oceans. Lined up end to end, these many containers would stretch a distance of 100 kilometers. However, they can be stacked on a ship just 350 meters long. This is the only way to make globalized world trade possible.

## Fleets growing faster than freight volumes

The Adrian can use the good wishes. AP Möller-Maersk, the world's second-largest shipping company, is sending the newest member of its fleet on an uncertain voyage. Never before have so many container ships been traversing the world's oceans, and their numbers are still increasing. Their cargo holds need to be filled. Just how well the industry will be able to manage this in the years to come is a question clouded by uncertainty.

Nobody knows what the future holds for global trade. Since last week, the biggest and most pressing concern has been Donald Trump. The extensive tariffs that the US president wants to use to protect the American economy could trigger a chain reaction. The merchant shipping sector may soon find itself thrown into the path of a storm.

Four years ago, when Maersk commissioned the Adrian's construction, the world was a different place. The COVID-19 pandemic was reshaping the global economy. People were staying at home but spending their money online. A flood of orders bought shipping companies' bottom lines. Freight prices shot up, and supply chains snarled. Shipowners' profits boomed, and they ordered new container ships en masse.

Since that time, the shipyards have been busy processing the orders. Many ships have now been completed. Transport capacity on the world's oceans grew by 11% last year, according to estimates by Alix Partners, a management consultancy specializing in logistics. A further 6% is likely to be added in 2025.

This means that shipping capacity is growing faster than the volumes of freight being transported on the ocean. Alix forecasts that sea freight volumes will increase by only 2% to 3% this year. Zürcher Kantonalbank, Switzerland's biggest regional bank, is predicting essentially flat growth for the year. However, forecasts in the current environment are very difficult. One thing seems clear: «The Trump tariffs will reshape international trade and transportation,» says Philip Damas, a supply chain expert at logistics consultant Drewry.

## Switzerland-based MSC has overtaken Maersk

A new fear is now rippling across the world's oceans - the fear of overcapacity. If there are more ships at sea than can be filled with goods, there is a risk that freight prices and shipowners' profits will collapse. Excessively ambitious ship-construction orders and subsequent economic hangovers are a regular pattern in the industry. But this time the distance of the drop appears to be particularly great.

None would be as potentially significantly affected as the world's largest shipping company, the Switzerland-based Mediterranean Shipping Company, branded as MSC. The tight-lipped privately held Geneva-based company has aggressively expanded its fleet in recent years, both through new orders and the purchase of used ships. As a result, MSC displaced Denmark's Maersk from the industry's top spot in 2022. According to Alix, MSC's transport capacity grew by 12% in 2024.

*Container ships are getting bigger, but they still need to be filled. An MSC freighter in the port of Rotterdam.* (Jochen Tack / Imago)

Maersk's executives are currently acting with a demonstrative optimism. In Rotterdam, Executive Vice President Karsten Kildahl assured journalists that the industry has sufficient means to react to overcapacity. For instance, shipowners could scrap old ships, which has been a rare practice in recent years. Scrapping old vessels reduces capacity and costs. New ships like the Adrian are more efficient.

Another hope: Kildahl noted that in Trump's first term in office, during the United States' trade conflict with China, trade shifted rather than declining overall. For example, trade within the Asian region increased, and orders submitted by European buyers of goods filled the gaps left by the Americans, he said.

Alix Partners' Fabian Engels therefore sees a great opportunity for Europe today – especially if the continent's policymakers react with their own punitive tariffs against the United States. «That would improve the conditions for Asian exports to Europe,» he says. This is because Asian exporters could be forced to seek new markets, reducing their prices in order to gain a foothold, he notes.

Not everyone shares this optimism. During Trump's first term, trading patterns shifted, but overall volumes continued to grow, confirms the Drewry consultancy's Damas. However, the tariff rates imposed by the US were nowhere near as high or comprehensive as those being imposed or proposed today. «A tariff-triggered recession in the United States or in other countries could cause volumes to decline,» Damas says. «That would be new.»

## Militants protecting shipowners' profits

At the moment, shipowners' profits are still getting some support – from armed militants. In late 2023, the Houthi rebels in Yemen began firing missiles and drones at freighters passing through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. This narrow passage connects the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden, and is one of the most important shipping routes in the world. Anyone using the Suez Canal has to travel through it.

But for more than a year now, this route has been too dangerous for the vast majority of freighters. Many ships are instead sailing around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa's southern tip. This can take as much as two weeks longer. Without the loss of the Suez Canal, today's overcapacity on the seas would already be much more problematic for shipowners. However, the longer routes mean that more ships than before are currently required to transport the same amount of freight to its destination.

Peter Heijkoop, the captain of the Adrian, is very happy with the detour. He was serving on the bridge of a freighter in 2015 when pirates tried to board his ship off the coast of Somalia. The attackers did not succeed, but Heijkoop today says it was the worst thing that had ever happened to him at sea. «You want to get home to your wife and children in one piece,» he says. «The detour around the Cape of Good Hope is safer.»

It is also more lucrative for his employer. Freight prices have skyrocketed following the Houthi attacks. Although the gains have not been as high as during the pandemic, they have been enough to significantly improve shipowners' balance sheets. In February 2024, Maersk forecast an operating loss (before interest and taxes) of up to $5 billion for the year. In fact, the company's operating profit for 2024 turned out to be $6.5 billion – the third-best result in the organization's history. Only the pandemic years were more profitable.

## MSC and Maersk have fallen out

The sector's profit potential for 2025 depends in part on whether and when the Suez Canal can be used again. The business is facing vast amounts of uncertainty. Even before the Trump tariffs, Maersk's forecasts for the year ranged from the breakeven point to a profit of $3 billion. MSC does not disclose business figures or forecasts. According to reports, the industry leader has considerable cash reserves on hand. If necessary, it could hold out in a price war as a means of strengthening its competitive position.

MSC need not have any scruples in this regard, as its ties to Maersk were cut a few months ago. Many large shipping companies join forces in alliances that function like airline code-sharing agreements. On some routes, for example, they market their ships jointly in order to utilize their capacity more efficiently. MSC and Maersk were linked for a decade in the 2M Alliance.

However, Maersk recently pulled out of this partnership, and since February has instead been working with Germany's Hapag-Lloyd in an alliance called the Gemini Cooperation. The reason: The Danes have developed a new operating model that MSC did not want to go along with. «We are now doing things quite differently than is usual within the merchant shipping sector,» says Lars Mikael Jensen, head of hubs and collaboration for Maersk subsidiary APM Terminals.

Traditionally, container ships sail a route that involves calling at a number of ports, for example from Asia to Europe. But in the Gemini alliance, they are instead traveling back and forth between a few large ports. Smaller ships bring the cargo from its points of origin to these larger ports, or transport it from there to its ultimate destination ports for the last leg of the journey. The model is similar to the way airlines use short-hop regional flights to bring passengers to the bigger hubs that serve as the end points for intercontinental flights.

## Restoring punctuality to a goal

Maersk took years to develop the new model. The associated planning effort has been huge, because much more freight has to be unloaded and reloaded than before. But the company believes the system will make deliveries much more punctual. If each ship calls at fewer ports, there are fewer potential opportunities for delays, Maersk has calculated.

Punctuality is a crucial factor in logistics. However, the conditions in the sea freight sector have deteriorated considerably since the COVID-19 crisis for many reasons. Maersk wants to be able to offer a higher degree of reliability again, hoping to retain customers in this way. This could pay off even more if the industry is hit by a storm of collapsing trade volumes and overcapacity. The model's initial results with regard to punctuality are promising, the company says.

For its part, MSC continues to operate according to the traditional model, letting its freighters sail to a handful of destinations per voyage. In this regard, it is betting that customers retain their traditional aversion to frequent off-loading and reloading of their cargo.

## Are larger ports even necessary?

On the day of the Adrian Maersk's christening, the Gudrun Maersk was anchored nearby. It was the shipping company's first freighter to arrive in Europe from Asia on the Gemini schedule. The giant remote-controlled cranes whirred high above the pier, lifting containers after containers off the ship, stacking them into new mountains or placing them on self-propelled transporters.

This game of Tetris with the containers on the ground has to go nearly perfectly if the reloading process is to be fast and efficient. As Europe's largest port, Rotterdam is one of the central hubs in Maersk's network. Maersk is today in the process of upgrading its facilities to handle the more complex Gemini operations. Over the next few years, the Rotterdam container terminal at Maasvlakte 2, built on reclaimed land, is set to double its capacity. A collapse in world trade would be inconvenient to say the least.

In the past, the Gudrun would have been called at seven ports on the continent. Under the Gemini model, this has been reduced to only two: Rotterdam and then Hamburg. Nevertheless, on the morning of the christening, the ship had to hurry, as it is only able to enter the port of Hamburg at high tide. To stay on schedule, it would have had to leave Rotterdam by 9 am – and in the end, it didn't make this deadline. When the champagne bottle was broken against the Adrian's hull, the Gudrun was still floating nearby.

But at least one thing is certain: The next high tide is always sure to come. At least that much can still be dependent upon at sea.
