# Emma Larsson sells sex – even though nobody is allowed to buy it in Sweden

*In Sweden, buying sex has been illegal since 1999 – selling it is still permitted. A visit to the sex work industry reveals that opinions on the law are sharply divided.*

When Emma Larsson meets a client, she always arranges to meet in a public place. She never gives out her address – not even to journalists. Instead, a day before the interview, she sends a message with the name of a bus stop in one of Gothenburg's notorious outer districts. She picks up her visitors there. On the way to her apartment, she has a few minutes to assess the person she's meeting.

Larsson isn't afraid of her suitors. She's afraid of the police.

In Sweden, buying sex has been illegal since 1999. Anyone who pays for sexual services is committing a crime. The ban is intended to deter buyers and thus destroy the business. There is a broad political consensus in the country that something like self-determined sex work does not exist.

The ban has pushed prostitution out of public spaces and made it invisible. It hasn't disappeared, however. And among those who sell sex in Sweden, opinions on the Swedish model vary widely.

## The sex worker: «I see this as a feminist project.»

The path to Larsson's apartment leads past a playground to a high-rise apartment building with several entrances. Outside, a few women wrapped in niqabs and hijabs sit chatting; the neighbor in the stairwell greets her warmly. Larsson's apartment is also her workplace. On the kitchen table lies a half-finished Dalmatian jigsaw puzzle, several giant snails crawl in a terrarium, and on the wall in the hallway hangs a picture of a vulva with hair glued on. Larsson painted it herself. For a long time, sex was just a hobby for her. In 2019, she decided to make money from it.

She uploads pictures and videos daily to platforms like OnlyFans. She also offers some of her clients sexual services offline. According to her own account, she earns between 1,500 and 2,000 euros a month from sex work, which makes up about half of her income. «I could live without this money,» says Larsson, who has a university degree in gender studies and works part-time for a human rights NGO. «But I want to take early retirement at some point.»

For Larsson, what she does is sex work. She doesn't want to use the terms prostitution or exploitation. «I see it as a feminist project,» she says. She's annoyed that in Sweden everyone who offers paid sex is seen as a victim. «I want to educate people that women can also experience and express sexual pleasure.»

Larsson was in her late twenties when she first posted pictures and videos of herself online, initially making them freely available. «I thought about it for a long time and set clear boundaries for myself beforehand.» The most important rule: she remains anonymous online – even in this article. She only shows her face from the mouth down. Emma Larsson is her real name, but it's so common in Sweden that she remains unrecognized. Offline, she always meets her clients for a conversation first and agrees on which services she offers – and which she doesn't. If she feels comfortable with the client, they have sex on the second date.

Larsson has never had any dealings with the police. She is a member of Red Umbrella Sweden, an organization that advocates for the rights of sex workers. She knows from other members that the ban on buying sex doesn't just affect the clients. The Swedish police have far-reaching powers. If there is suspicion of pimping, they are allowed to confiscate sex workers' mobile phones – without a warrant from a prosecutor or investigating judge.

«The police can easily raise such suspicions,» says Larsson. Simply sharing an apartment with someone or being picked up from a meeting with a client can be enough to accuse a roommate or friend of pimping. The situation becomes even more critical when the police inform the landlord. Since the landlord profits from the sex work through rent, he has almost no other option but to terminate the lease. Otherwise, he risks running afoul of the law himself.

Larsson lives alone. This also means she meets her clients alone. There's no social oversight. She's never been afraid for her safety – partly because she can choose her clients. If she has a bad feeling, she refuses. The idea that only unkempt, lonely men who otherwise experience no intimacy buy sex is wrong. «My clients are perfectly normal people who often have a fetish they can't act on in their relationships.»

Statistics bear Larsson out: buying sex is a widespread phenomenon, at least among men. According to studies , one in four men in Germany has done it, and in Switzerland, it's said to be one in five . And even in Sweden, where the ban on buying sex has been in effect for almost three decades, one in ten men has paid for sexual services at least once in their lives.

In July, Sweden extended its ban on buying sex to the internet. Since then, it has also been a crime to purchase personalized sexual content on platforms like OnlyFans, such as private video calls in which the customer asks the performer to do something. Pornography produced for a wider audience, however, remains legal. Larsson doesn't believe that stricter laws will prevent exploitation. On the contrary.

Many earn a large part of their money by fulfilling customer requests, says Larsson. «If this income disappears, they have to compensate for it in other ways. This could lead some to cross their boundaries – for example, by showing their face in videos for a wide audience because such clips sell better.»

Gabriella Kärnekull Wolfe and Anna Olsson* have repeatedly crossed their boundaries. They see things very differently than Emma Larsson.

## The victim: «Buying sex is always a form of violence.»

Gabriella Kärnekull Wolfe was 16 when a client bought sex from her. He picked her up from her home and took her to his apartment. He forced alcohol on her until she was drunk. Then he took a stick and beat her 137 times. When he was finished, he dragged her into the bathroom and urinated on her. He recorded all of this on a video. Then he drove her to school.

Anna Olsson was 15 when a client bought her sex in the room of his daughter, who was the same age. He complimented her on her looks, on how small and young she was. It was a forbidden fantasy he was acting out on her. When he was finished, he showed her pictures of his daughter. Olsson received clothes and alcohol from him as payment.

The two events took place several years ago. Gabriella Kärnekull Wolfe is now one of the most prominent advocates of the Swedish model. The 27-year-old founded the association Inte din Hora (Not Your Whore) in 2017. There she met 24-year-old Anna Olsson. Wolfe and Olsson would never use the term sex work. For them, prostitution is nothing more than exploitation.

At 15, Olsson posted her first pictures and videos online. She says, «It was so easy. Nothing and no one could stop you—except yourself.» Wolfe was just 12 when she was first contacted by an adult man in a forum. Eventually, the men started offering her money and alcohol in exchange for sexually explicit photos. Wolfe says, «To me, the men's behavior was normal, and I didn't understand that they were doing anything wrong.»

Both women were 15 – just below the age of consent in Sweden – when they first had sex for money. «The men were at least twice their age, some considerably older. They had respectable jobs, families,» says Olsson. And they were violent. This is exemplified by the video of Wolfe's abuse, which was later played in court and described in various media reports . The video shows Wolfe screaming and writhing. She cries and shakes her head.

Wolfe says: «There’s nothing wrong with selling sex. But buying sex is always a form of violence.» Being able to buy another person’s consent is evidence of inequality, abuse of power – and, not least, the objectification of women. Anyone who allows this is essentially giving rich men unlimited access to poor women’s bodies. The Swedish model creates clear boundaries: «Those who buy are perpetrators. Those who sell are victims.»

Neither Wolfe nor Olsson worked for the money. Both teenagers were mentally ill and self-harmed. Wolfe's arms are covered in scars. Olsson says: «For a long time, I told myself I had everything under control—but in reality, I had lost it long ago.» The moment she first accepted money. When a client insisted on having sex without a condom. When he hit her because she resisted.

Olsson managed to leave prostitution after a year, Wolfe much later, at 19. «I first told social workers and my therapist when I was 15 that I was meeting older men for money. My parents also suspected what was going on.» Nevertheless, years passed, with several police investigations and court hearings, before she finally received help.

The 40-year-old man who had brutally abused her when she was 16 was convicted in February 2015 of buying sexual acts with a child. However, both the Stockholm District Court and the relevant appeals court acquitted him of the rape charge. Based on past chat messages, the courts concluded that Wolfe had consented to the violent sex and, consequently, the abuse. «The verdict was traumatic for me because it made me feel like what had been done to me was okay.»

Of Olsson's eight clients, two were convicted. Wolfe estimates the number of men she had paid for sex with at twenty to thirty. Only five cases resulted in a police investigation, and three—including the 40-year-old—were convicted. Thanks to a dedicated policewoman, Wolfe finally found a way out. Being recognized as a victim was crucial for both Wolfe and Olsson. Wolfe says, «Things only changed for me when I realized I was being abused.»

Wolfe and Olsson, unlike Emma Larsson, agree that the ban on buying sex should also apply online starting in July – even if its enforceability is questionable. «What's forbidden offline should also be forbidden online,» says Wolfe. Despite their differences, all three women agree on one point: abuse can hardly be prevented even with stricter legislation, as long as there are men who don't care about the legal consequences.
