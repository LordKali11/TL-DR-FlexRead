# On course for growth: How the BRICS alliance intends to expand its global footprint

*As the BRICS group of emerging economies wraps up its annual summit this week, it is betting on deeper trade and financial ties to increase its global economic influence. However, several factors could slow its momentum.*

**Matthias Kamp, Hong Kong** · NZZ in English · 2024-10-24 · 1520 words · [ld.1854065](https://www.nzz.ch/english/brics-group-sets-sights-on-strength-through-integration-ld.1854065)

> Summary
> -  As it seeks to expand its global economic influence, the BRICS group of states faces challenges due to its members' diverse political systems and internal geopolitical rivalries.
> - Though its members are growing faster than the G7 countries, the BRICS alliance lacks the strong institutions necessary to act as a cohesive political entity.
> - Russia and China in particular are working toward ending the U.S. dollar's dominance in international financial transactions.

Two weeks before the start of this year's summit of the BRICS group of states, this year's host, Russia, had already set about creating the right rhetorical framework for the meeting.

«The BRICS states are the engine, the driving force of economic growth,» said Russia's Finance Minister Anton Siluanov at a conference in Moscow on Oct. 12. The average annual economic growth rate within the BRICS economies will be 4.4% both this year and next, Siluanov noted.

By contrast, the G7 countries would achieve average growth of only 1.7%, he said. «It is clear whose economic development is more dynamic,» Siluanov added. It was a targeted broadside against the West.

The 16th BRICS summit , held this week in Kazan, Russia, concluded on Thursday, Oct. 24. Moscow has sought to use the meeting to deepen integration within the still somewhat informal alliance. This goal has been given new urgency by the comprehensive sanctions imposed by the West on Moscow in response to its attack on Ukraine. To cushion the economic impact of the sanctions, Russia wants to promote the exchange of goods and investment among the BRICS members.

## More new members

On the surface, the prospects seem favorable, given that the alliance has gained several new members in recent years. In addition, the BRICS countries have recently tightened their mutual ties, driven by China.

South Africa joined the original alliance of Brazil, Russia, India and China in 2010. At last year's summit in South Africa, invitations were extended to Argentina, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia.

Only Argentina's government decided not to join the BRICS group. Saudi Arabia wants to wait before making a commitment. Nevertheless, the association is likely to continue to grow, as 12 additional countries have since applied for membership.

«Recent crises have added momentum to BRICS expansion,» says an April study by the Boston Consulting Group. A number of large developing nations aligned neither with NATO nor with Russia have resisted Western pressure to join the sanctions against Russia, it notes.

Other countries have voiced frustration over what they see as the G7's disregard for their interests in combating climate change and managing the pandemic. Such sensitivities have provided the BRICS group with a tail wind.

With its expansion to nine members, representing half the world's population, the group's economic clout has grown. The BRICS countries account for more than 35% of global gross domestic product. The comparable share in the G7 countries is only 30%. In addition, the BRICS countries account for around two-fifths of all global trade.

## Growing influence in the energy sector

The influence of the BRICS countries is particularly significant in the energy sector. If, as is generally expected, Saudi Arabia joins the group, the BRICS states will account for 43% of global crude oil production and 32% of natural gas production. Should accession candidates Kuwait, Kazakhstan and Bahrain also join, these figures could rise even higher. Led by China and India, the BRICS countries already import 38% of the world's oil.

This development could have significant repercussions for the West. «During times of volatility in energy markets, having many of the biggest energy buyers and sellers within the same group could give rise to a parallel energy trading system,» the Boston Consulting Group experts write.

The integration between these countries can be seen most clearly in their mutual trade, which has spurred the states' economic development in recent years. Between 2002 and 2022, intra-BRICS trade as a share of global trade doubled, reaching 40%. This surge has been buoyed by China's rapid economic development in recent years.

This growing economic integration is also evident in the interdependencies between the bloc's individual countries. For example, China's importance as a supplier of industrial and consumer goods has increased significantly in recent years.

## Iron ore and soybeans from Brazil

At the same time, China is one of the largest importers of raw materials. For example, the country imports iron ore and soybeans from Brazil on a large scale. In return, China exports electric cars, solar modules and heavy machinery to the South American country.

The sanctions against Russia have also ensured that more Russian goods are today going to BRICS countries – especially India and China – instead of Western countries.

The BRICS countries have also increased rapidly in importance as investment locations in recent years. According to a study by the UN Trade and Development organization , $84 billion in foreign direct investment flowed into the four BRICS countries in 2001. Twenty years later, this sum had reached $355 billion.

In 2021, the BRICS group – now expanded to five countries – accounted for 22% of global foreign direct investment, compared to 11% just 20 years earlier.

## Money flowing into China

As expected, a significant amount of money flowed into China, which was booming during this era. However, the sums invested in India, Brazil and South Africa also increased.

The years 2001 to 2011 in particular saw foreign direct investment in the BRICS countries surge. Investment totals grew by an annual rate of 13.5% in this period. In the years that followed, the growth rate declined sharply to just 1.7% per year, largely due to a weaker global investment environment.

Despite the group's growing economic clout in recent years, doubts remain about the future of its integration. Indeed, the alliance could face an uncertain future.

The reasons for this caution are plentiful. The BRICS countries have vastly different political systems and institutional frameworks. The countries also differ with regard to economic models and cultural traditions.

## Lack of an institutional framework

The alliance also includes geopolitical rivals such as China and India. The dissonance created by these factors could reduce the BRICS countries' economic and political clout over the medium term. In addition, the group still lacks the kind of robust institutions that would transform the loose association into a coordinated political force capable of taking effective joint action.

Yet other factors are also likely to curb a further increase in the BRICS countries' influence in the near future. China's demand for raw materials from abroad has waned in recent years, and the country's overall appetite for imports has shown only anemic growth. This slowdown is due to the ongoing economic crisis, particularly in a real estate sector that has hit rock bottom.

Meanwhile, resistance to China's export dominance in sectors like green technology, electric vehicles and steel is growing globally. Even BRICS member Brazil has now imposed punitive tariffs on a number of individual product groups from China.

Russia's war against Ukraine, in turn, is likely to tie up more and more of Moscow's resources in the coming years, potentially weakening that country's economy.

## Ending the dollar's dominance

The BRICS countries, with China and Russia at the forefront, are actively working to weaken the US dollar's dominance in global payment systems. As a first step in this direction, the countries founded the Shanghai-based New Development Bank in 2015. Among its other tasks, this development bank grants loans in currencies other than the US dollar.

Russia, hobbled by Western sanctions, is now pushing for a new payment system for BRICS countries. Moscow's plan envisages the creation of a «multicurrency system» that would «ring-fence its participants from any external pressures such as extraterritorial sanctions,» according to a report by the Bloomberg news agency.

To this end, Russia's finance minister and the country's central bank are proposing to set up a network of commercial banks that can process transactions directly in local currencies. Moscow also wants to create centers for mutual trade in raw materials such as oil, gas, grain and gold. While no firm announcements emerged, these subjects were presumably the subject of intense discussion at this week's BRICS summit.

In a joint statement released on Wednesday, the participating countries said: «We welcome the use of local currencies in financial transactions between BRICS countries and their trading partners.» However, the statement also noted that the cross-border payments initiative was «voluntary and non-binding,» and that the countries looked forward to further discussion on the issue.
