# France's political crisis is being fueled by the gap between expectations and reality

*The government crisis in France is more than the result of a political blunder. Behind it are economic trends that should serve as a warning to the rest of Europe. Seven graphics illuminate the country's current troubles.*

**Peter A. Fischer** · NZZ in English · 2024-12-10 · 1639 words · [ld.1861505](https://www.nzz.ch/english/frances-political-crisis-is-due-to-economic-illusions-ld.1861505)

> Summary
> -  France's crisis is being driven by political polarization, as well as by a widespread refusal to face economic realities.
> - Growth has stagnated in France for years, and its industrial sector has become less competitive. The government has consistently attempted to solve problems with deficit spending.
> - The economic problems have exacerbated political tensions and eroded trust in democracy. France's experiences should serve as a warning for other countries in Europe.

France is known the world over for its monumental cathedrals and castles, lush fields, refined multicourse menus, and good wines. The German expression «living like God in France» is rooted in real experience.

This makes it all the more unfortunate that France is today drawing the world's attention primarily with negative headlines. In the country's last parliamentary elections, the extreme right and left political poles gained ground once again. Last week, the highly polarized parliament voted to bring down the weak government headed by Prime Minister Michel Barnier. President Emmanuel Macron, who has pushed through some sensitive reforms against great resistance during his time in office, is widely unpopular. Within the financial markets, there is a growing concern that France is living chronically beyond its means.

What is going on?

In fact, France's problems derive from a series of economic trends that have long been traveled, as illustrated in the following seven graphics.

## A gradual decline

With its mixture of agricultural strength and large, often state-tied industrial groups, France was able to keep pace with Germany economically for decades. Between 1980 and 2006, prosperity as measured by gross domestic product per capita increased by around 50% in real terms in both countries. However, since the global financial crisis of 2007/08, what began to be seen in Italy in the early 2000s has been happening in France as well: Prosperity has stagnated, and the economy is no longer making any real progress. Germany, by contrast, continued to grow until the pandemic.

France's competitiveness has been stunted by labor laws that are illiberal and hamper growth, too much centralized bureaucracy, and too little openness to international markets. The consequences of these characteristics do not manifest themselves immediately. Rather, the decline comes gradually, as the example of Italy has shown. These consequences can now be observed in France as well.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b2486f0c8bc5df60643339df4a4d1fd4]

By trying to hold back structural change, the French government has paralyzed its own economy even more. Its once-proud industrial sector has diminished both in competitiveness and significance, and there have been too few innovative small and medium-sized enterprises to take its place.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b2486f0c8bc5df60643339df4a4d4e53]

While industrial sector output accounted for 25% of France's GDP in 1990, this share had fallen to just 16% in 2021. Last year, this share rose back to 19%, probably in part due to Macron's efforts. But this figure also comes with caveats: First, the government procured this increase via the use of expensive subsidies, and second, the proportion still remains much lower than Germany's 28% or Switzerland's 25% share.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b2486f0c8bc5df60643339df4a4ed30e]

The fact that the French economy is not broadly export-oriented has certainly contributed to this issue. In France, the overall value of exports in 2023 amounted to just 33% of GDP. In Germany, the comparable figure was 47%, while in Switzerland, it was 75%. A national economy that is only somewhat exposed to international competition always runs the risk of losing innovative strength.

## Soothing discontent with transfers

In an economy that is no longer growing properly, productivity stagnates. This makes it difficult to increase wages without undermining firms' competitiveness. Popular resentment then tends to build, intensifying distribution struggles.

In France, this can be seen in part in the high level of absenteeism due to strikes since the financial crisis. The frequency of such work stoppages has fallen sharply in Spain, where strikes used to be equally popular, but it has increased in France. The «yellow vest» protest movement was one high-profile expression of this trend. In 2022, there were 99 strike days per 1,000 employees in France. In Spain, the comparable figure was 35, while in Germany it was just seven, and in Switzerland 0.7.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 435c535d94e2373b90529667883c298b]

The French government has repeatedly tried to address overdue reforms in areas such as pensions and energy policy. But when confronted by protests or problems, it has persistently sought to overcome them with money. After the financial crisis and again during the pandemic, social spending therefore increased by leaps and bounds. In addition, Macron's government has provided companies with increasingly generous subsidies.

Last year, social transfers and subsidies together accounted for 56% of all government spending in France.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b2486f0c8bc5df60643339df4a4fe14f]

As the French government has increased its spending in response to problems and crises, government expenditure has also gone up as a share of GDP. In 2023, this ratio was about 57% – which means that well over half of the value added in France is spent by the state. By way of comparison, government expenditure as a share of GDP is also high in Germany, at 48%, while this ratio is just 32% in Switzerland, according to Trading Economics.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) b2486f0c8bc5df60643339df4a50f2c2]

But that's not all. France's politicians have consistently boosted spending, but have not increased state revenue. Instead, they are generating deficits. The last time the French government recorded a budget surplus was in the 1970s.

In 2023, the French state's budgetary deficit – the amount by which total spending exceeded total revenue – amounted to 5.5% of GDP. This year, the figure is likely to be above 6% of GDP. EU rules ostensibly permit a maximum budget deficit of 3% of GDP, and total public debt of no more than 60% of GDP. France's public debt has now risen to 112% of GDP. In Germany, the comparable figure is 63%, and in Switzerland 32%.

France has thus slipped into the kind of debt-fueled economy that was previously characteristic of southern European countries such as Italy or Greece. The primary difference now is that it is not the smaller, southern European economy of Greece that lacks the money it wants to spend, but rather a large core country in the eurozone. Furthermore, this predicament is now setting the government squarely against voters and political parties that simply refuse to accept the situation, as the fall of the Barnier government has just shown.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 435c535d94e2373b90529667883d5ee6]

Investors and financial markets are still counting on the idea that France, like Italy, is too big to fail. However, it is clear that market trepidation is growing. For the first time, the yield premiums of 10-year French government bonds as compared to German government bonds – effectively, the amount of additional interest that the French government must offer when issuing bonds as compared to the German government, due to the higher perceived risk – recently exceeded the premiums for Greek government bonds, and were almost as high as those of Italian government bonds. As a result, France may have to spend more on interest payments next year than it devotes to its military.

Nevertheless, at 0.78%, the spread between French and German government bond yields is still much lower than that associated with Greek state debt during that country's sovereign debt crisis, when the difference rose above 10%. If investors ultimately lose confidence as they did during the Greek crisis, the yields required still may skyrocket in the future. As yet, however, the market still seems convinced that France will be able to service its debts in an orderly manner despite the turmoil.

The example of Italy – which is currently behaving in a much more disciplined manner – shows that a major eurozone country can go relatively far before triggering a serious debt crisis. Italy's public debt has now reached 137% of its GDP.

Investors obviously trust that eurozone countries would stand by each other in a crisis, despite rules to the contrary, and that the European Central Bank would act to prevent debt from becoming unmanageable. Therefore, no new euro crisis is likely to emerge in the very short term. Yet France's profligate financial behavior is narrowing the country's remaining fiscal flexibility and weakening the eurozone as a whole.

## Catching problems early

The most important lesson to be learned from the analysis of France's problems is that it is important to take countermeasures and tackle undesirable developments in the early stages, as the problems emerge.

If a country loses competitiveness and sacrifices fiscal discipline in response, it inevitably goes down a slippery slope from which it is difficult to recover. When a population has grown used to prosperity, expectations remain high. People believe that nothing should be allowed to get worse, and that everything should get better. However, in such circumstances, those who continue to hold such beliefs are in fact moving further and further away from reality, as there is no longer sufficient money available to satisfy these demands. Political tensions and a loss of trust in democracy are obvious consequences.

France should therefore serve as an urgent warning to the rest of Europe – especially Germany. The country's mounting difficulties portend a period of sweat and tears. Once again, more has to be done, and many people will no longer be able to live like God in France. It would be much better not to let things go this far in the first place.
