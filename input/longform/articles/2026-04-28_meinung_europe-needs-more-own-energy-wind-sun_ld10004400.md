# Europe needs more of its own energy – wind and sun are not enough

*Four years after the invasion of Ukraine, the Iran war once again exposes Europe's strategic vulnerability: The continent produces too little energy. This creates dangerous dependencies and does nothing for the climate.*

The repercussions of the Iran war are reaching all continents like a tsunami. They are being transmitted via the shipping routes of the large oil and liquefied gas tankers, which have been disrupted for more than a month by the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz.

Even if the war and the blockade are ended through negotiations in the coming weeks, one thing is already clear: the Israeli and US attack on Iran will lead to a surge in global inflation, dampening economic growth. The wholesale price of natural gas in Europe is around 50 percent higher for the remainder of the year than before the war. Gasoline has become 12 percent more expensive on average, and diesel 26 percent more expensive. In the best-case scenario of a swift reopening of trade, the International Monetary Fund projects a 0.3 percent slowdown in global growth.

The obvious answer to the recent oil crisis might seem to be to accelerate the electrification of economies through electricity from renewable energy sources. Europe wants to leave the fossil fuel age behind with all its crises and dependencies. This strategy remains correct, but for now it is a dream. Despite significant progress in the installation and use of wind and solar power, the use of fossil fuels in Europe, while declining, is indispensable. And this will remain the case for many years to come.

## Nothing works without fossil fuels

Wind and solar energy reached a record high share of electricity production across Europe in 2025, at 30% – one percentage point more than fossil fuels. However, Europe as a whole still needs fossil fuels. In 2024, only 20% of total energy consumption came from renewable sources, compared to 38% from oil, 21% from natural gas and 10% from coal. Europe has to import 57% of its energy needs.

A rapid shift away from fossil fuels is unrealistic. In Great Britain, for example, three-quarters of private households still heat their homes with gas burners, and there is no realistic plan to change this in the short term. And despite the planned ban on coal-fired power plants by 2038, Germany burns more coal for electricity generation than any other European country. Economics Minister Katherina Reiche therefore wants to build new gas-fired power plants with a capacity of at least 11 gigawatts. This is roughly the same output as the eight nuclear power plants that were taken offline in Germany in the last 10 years. The new gas-fired power plants are necessary because electricity production from wind and solar power fluctuates considerably. The situation is similar in Switzerland, which will find it difficult to avoid building several gas-fired power plants due to its growing electricity demand.

## Successes and shortcomings of climate policy

Under the U.N. climate agreements, European states have committed to a further drastic reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, aiming to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050 at the latest. However, Europe is far from reaching this goal, and the rest of the world is even further behind. Global warming compared to pre-industrial levels has already reached 1.5 degrees, a threshold that, according to the 2015 Paris Agreement, was not supposed to be exceeded by the end of the century. Realistically, the best hope now is that warming remains within the range of 2 to 3 degrees in the long term.

Historically, the West has released by far the largest quantities of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere due to its significant head start in industrialization and its high level of prosperity. Climate activists rightly derive from this a moral obligation to take the lead in climate protection. The West has also achieved a great deal since the 1992 Rio Earth Summit. Economic growth has been decoupled from emissions. In 2024, emissions in Europe were around 40% lower than in 1990.

But with Donald Trump's return to the White House, the U.S. withdrew from all climate policy commitments. National regulation of greenhouse gases was halted. Instead, the production of natural gas and oil is being accelerated. Trump is obsessively fighting the further installation of wind and solar power plants.

And even in Europe, it's doubtful how strong the will and willingness to make sacrifices for the goal of climate neutrality actually are. While there's no lack of abstract support for climate protection, which is also reflected in legislation, citizens' consumption patterns offer little evidence of this. The much-discussed «flight shame» of the climate strike era is a figment of the imagination. If a country cuts subsidies for electric cars, solar panels, or heat pumps, demand collapses.

There have also been recent setbacks at the political level. The emissions trading system for large consumers is under attack from various governments. The new German government has weakened overly costly and unpopular regulations for building heating systems. While it remains committed to the goal of climate neutrality by 2045, the country is not on track, according to the German Council of Experts on Climate Change. In several countries, including Italy and Germany, governments could not withstand the pressure of rising fuel prices for even a few weeks and quickly moved to cut taxes. The fact that this undermines a price signal that could incentivize the switch to electric vehicles is apparently of secondary importance.

## More realism is needed

It is high time that European energy policy adopts a more realistic self-assessment. Further reducing greenhouse gases is a valid goal. Climate change poses risks that affect everyone and should not be accepted without action. However, it is not crucial whether Europe achieves climate neutrality a few years earlier or later – especially since the rest of the world, responsible for roughly 90% of emissions, is not following Europe's lead anyway. What is crucial is that the energy transition, with all its consequences, is accepted and supported by society and the economy.

Electricity demand will rise sharply in the coming years, driven by the electrification of transportation, building heating, industry and booming data centers. Growth in Europe is estimated at more than 2% per year. Renewable energies are insufficient to meet this demand, both now and in the foreseeable future, partly due to a lack of storage capacity. If gas is scarce or too expensive, decommissioned coal-fired power plants will be reactivated as a last resort (as happened after Russia's invasion of Ukraine) – the worst-case scenario for the climate. In the U.S., on the other hand, growing electricity demand is primarily met by more climate-friendly gas-fired power plants, thanks to abundant natural gas and gas prices that, even before the Iran war, were only about one-third as high as in Europe. These low gas prices impact electricity prices, which are roughly half as high for large consumers in the U.S. as in Europe.

It does nothing for the climate to relocate industrial production to other continents where energy prices are lower due to less stringent climate protection requirements. Despite government subsidies, high energy prices for industry and the extremely expensive programs to replace natural gas with (non-existent) hydrogen are driving too many companies out of Europe.
