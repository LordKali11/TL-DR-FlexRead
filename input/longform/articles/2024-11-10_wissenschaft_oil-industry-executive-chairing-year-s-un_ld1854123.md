# An oil industry executive is chairing this year's UN climate conference in Baku. Climate policy experts view Mukhtar Babayev with skepticism

*A president of the World Climate Conference is expected to balance the interests of diverse countries and forge compromises behind the scenes. Observers doubt that Babayev can live up to these expectations.*

Starting tomorrow, it's that time again: For two weeks, representatives from nearly 200 countries will gather for the 29th UN Climate Change Conference, or COP29. It will take place in Baku, the capital of Azerbaijan, whose economy is heavily dependent on natural gas and oil exports. The president of the COP is Mukhtar Babayev. The 57-year-old has been the Minister of Ecology and Natural Resources of the authoritarian-ruled country since 2018. He has already led the Azerbaijani delegation to five UN Climate Change Conferences and is therefore very familiar with the challenging format.

For some, it marks the beginning of a great career. For example, for Angela Merkel, who first stepped onto the world stage in Berlin in 1995 as head of the first UN Climate Change Conference (COP1). Merkel had taken an English course beforehand. She led the negotiations with prudence and assertiveness.

For others, the task ends in disgrace, as was the case for Danish Prime Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen. Under his presidency, COP15 failed in Copenhagen in 2009. This jeopardized the success of international environmental policy altogether.****

Some rise above themselves and surprise their biggest critics – such as Abdullah bin Hamad al-Attiyah from Qatar. As president of the 2012 conference, he left behind his past as president of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), achieved a consensus on compensating the victims of climate change, and sealed the negotiated agreement with swift hammer blows.

Others are remembered not for their successes, but, like Sultan al-Jaber at the last COP in Dubai, for debates surrounding conflicts of interest. This president was the head of the state-owned oil and gas company Adnoc.

The office of COP President comes with a great responsibility. They are expected to lead a highly complex negotiation process encompassing hundreds of topics and subtopics, where the interests of poor and rich countries, democracies and dictatorships, fossil fuel economies and environmental pioneers clash. COP Presidents are expected to demonstrate vision on the public stage and to forge compromises behind the scenes. Moreover, especially in the final stages, they must be able to negotiate late into the night without faltering.

## Much is expected of the president of COP29

Babayev, the president of this year's COP, is an unknown figure to most people involved in climate protection. At COP29, he will take his place on the podium alongside Simon Stiell, the Executive Secretary of the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. He also heads the so-called «Troika,» which includes his predecessor from the United Arab Emirates and his successor from Brazil, where COP30 will be held. This trio is intended to ensure continuity across the annual summits through collaboration. A key focus of this COP will be whether the three countries act credibly and strengthen their national climate targets.

Will Babayev succeed in advancing the UN climate conference as decisively as necessary? Progress is hoped for primarily in the area of ​​financing. In speeches leading up to COP29, Babayev signaled that he considers the situation very serious and rapid action crucial, thus making it clear he does not intend to downplay the crisis.

All countries must now significantly increase their efforts to reduce emissions and prepare for climate stress, he said, for example, during Climate Week in New York at the end of September. His homeland, the Caucasus region, is also «facing extreme heat, water scarcity, and falling water levels in the Caspian Sea, which directly impact our lives and livelihoods,» he wrote in a 20-page circular to conference participants in September, outlining his agenda.

Among his most important goals for COP29, the minister says that rich countries should commit to significantly higher annual payments for climate protection in poorer countries than the current 100 billion dollars, which was already very difficult to agree on.

COP29 follows months in which one temperature record chased another on land and in the oceans, while West Africa, Central Europe, and the USA experienced unusually severe flooding, the Rio Negro, a major tributary of the Amazon, dried up, and the Pantanal, the world's largest wetland, burned. The warnings from the scientific community that predictions are already becoming reality are unmistakable.

Business representatives are also alarmed: «Geopolitical tensions and record-breaking climate events underline the urgency of international negotiations,» says Alexander Keberle, Head of Energy, Infrastructure and Environment at Economiesuisse, the umbrella organization of the Swiss economy.

## The focus is on financing issues

Christoph Bals, the managing director of the environmental organization Germanwatch, is one of the most experienced observers of UN climate negotiations. He says that Babayev is right to raise fundamental financial questions for discussion, such as the role of development banks and how private funds could be channeled into climate protection. «If the goals for climate protection, adaptation, and damage management are to be achieved,» he says, «at least a tenfold increase in the 100 billion euros mobilized so far annually is needed.»

Alexander Keberle of Economiesuisse also believes the planned focus of COP29 on climate finance is the right approach. «A uniform CO2 levy for industrialized countries, emerging economies, and developing countries would make sense, so that as many countries as possible participate,» he says.

Which countries contribute how much to the «Loss and Damage» fund should be based on their contribution to historical emissions, says Alexander Keberle, Head of Energy, Infrastructure and Environment at Economiesuisse. This fund is intended to compensate for losses and damages caused by climate change. Furthermore, Keberle adds, countries should be able to pool their climate targets to achieve them jointly. A desirable outcome of the climate conference would also be a binding date for phasing out coal.

Another crucial point of the conference is the question of what new climate targets the countries will set. Current measures and their sluggish implementation will result in a dangerous global warming of 2.5 to 2.9 degrees Celsius by 2100. «Only with significantly increased ambition from the countries causing climate change is it still possible to limit warming to 1.5 degrees,» says Delia Berner of Alliance Sud, a development policy working group of six Swiss organizations.

Christoph Bals noted positively that Babayev had already clearly committed himself in April at the «Petersberg Climate Dialogue» in the Berlin Foreign Ministry to the goal of keeping global warming below 1.5 degrees Celsius, which would necessitate immediate and far-reaching measures. In the summer, Babayev announced that his country would also tighten its «Nationally Determined Contributions» (NDCs) to align them with the climate target.

## Criticism of Azerbaijan's climate record

Niklas Höhne, Professor of CO2 Reduction at Wageningen University and initiator of the Climate Action Tracker , considers Azerbaijan's own plans «absolutely inadequate.» At the end of 2023, the host country submitted a new target to the United Nations to reduce CO2 emissions by 40 percent by 2050 compared to 1990 levels. However, according to the Climate Action Tracker, a much faster reduction would be necessary to contribute to the 1.5-degree target: a reduction of current emissions from 70 million tons to 35 million tons in 2030 and to just 10 million tons in 2050.

It is virtually impossible to achieve such goals with Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev. Since 2010, his country's emissions have increased by 38 percent . At the Petersberg Climate Dialogue, he still referred to the country's oil and gas reserves as a «gift from God» and described their use as the «right» of oil- and gas-producing states.

As expected, Babayev faces considerable mistrust from environmentalists. Not only does he represent a government heavily reliant on oil production, but he also comes from the oil industry himself. Before entering politics and becoming a member of parliament in 2010, he worked for the State Oil Company of Azerbaijan. There, he was responsible for sustainability, meaning he wasn't quite as deeply involved in the global oil business as Sultan al-Jaber.

Sir David King, former chief scientist for the British government and founder of the Climate Crisis Advisory Group, called the appointment of another former oil executive as COP president earlier this year «as disappointing as it was inevitable given Azerbaijan's dependence on oil».

«The COP presidency of a country whose business model largely depends on fossil fuels and which is governed in an authoritarian manner is challenging – for the world, but also for the COP president,» says Christoph Bals.

«I expect the host country to put aside its national interests regarding fossil fuels and do everything possible to enable ambitious decisions in line with the Paris Agreement,» says Delia Berner, who is responsible for international climate policy at Alliance Sud and will be part of the Swiss delegation in Baku as a representative of civil society.

Azerbaijani environmental activist Cavid Qara is highly critical of Babayev's actions to date. Qara, representing the small, unregistered organization Ecofront, was recently invited to meet with members of the German Bundestag who wanted to learn about the situation in the authoritarian Caucasus republic. Until shortly before Babayev took office at the Ministry of the Environment, Qara stated that he worked there as an advisor on forest policy.

«Babaev wasn’t interested in the environment, only in being praised by everyone and seen as the most powerful man, primarily focused on cutting costs in the ministry,» says Qara. His constant micromanagement made his staff feel very insecure, and he never even attempted to challenge larger companies or achieve significant successes. While he will act in a friendly and diplomatic manner at the COP, he has yet to present a convincing plan for Azerbaijan to phase out fossil fuels: «How can anyone be credible under those circumstances?»

After leaving the Ministry of the Environment, Qara moved to Great Britain, where he has since been orchestrating protests against illegal deforestation in Azerbaijan. He will travel to his home country for the COP, but embarks on the journey with trepidation: «The human rights situation in Azerbaijan is bad,» he says.

Against this backdrop, Christoph Bals of Germanwatch is deeply concerned about the extent to which civil society in Azerbaijan is under pressure from the authoritarian government. He sees the first benchmark for Babayev's performance as COP president as whether activists and non-governmental organizations from Azerbaijan and around the world can «express themselves freely and without inhibition and also demonstrate freely» in the context of COP29.

In the long run, however, Babayev's success will be judged more on whether COP29 becomes a mere «interim conference» quickly forgotten, as some EU negotiators believe – or whether it produces groundbreaking decisions. The COP president himself raises rather high expectations when he says that the successes of Baku should «form the foundation for all future efforts in climate policy» and «open a new chapter.»

For this to even begin to succeed, a great many things have to go right in Baku – including that the Azerbaijani president doesn't take over the COP from Babayev in a coup d'état.
