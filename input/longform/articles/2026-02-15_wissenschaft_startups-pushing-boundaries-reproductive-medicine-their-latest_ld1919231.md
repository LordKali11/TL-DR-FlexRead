# Startups are pushing the boundaries of reproductive medicine. Their latest goal: genetically modified babies.

*Backed by billionaires from Silicon Valley, several US companies are planning to manipulate the DNA of human embryos. They want to eradicate diseases – and improve the intelligence of future generations.*

**Martin Amrein** · Wissenschaft · 2026-02-15 · 1769 words · [ld.1919231](https://www.nzz.ch/wissenschaft/crispr-startups-wollen-die-gene-von-babys-veraendern-ld.1919231)

She was considered a wunderkind of the American biotech scene. Cathy Tie dropped out of college at 18 to found a genetic testing company. A $100,000 grant from billionaire Peter Thiel made it possible. Several startups followed. The most recent was one that develops genetically modified pets – including rabbits that glow in the dark.

Today, ten years after founding her first company, Tie has much bigger plans: «I want to change the entire medical system. It's extremely outdated and doesn't serve patients,» she says. The Chinese-born Canadian explains her vision in a video call: «We should treat diseases before someone is born.»

With her latest venture, Manhattan Genomics, Tie aims to modify the genes of human embryos to protect them from disease. She plans to use the gene-editing tool CRISPR. Eight years ago, Chinese researcher He Jiankui used the same method to create the first designer babies. His experiments caused a global uproar: He exposed the children to significant risks and provided inadequate information to the parents. Since then, manipulating embryos with CRISPR has been taboo.

Tie believes it's time to revive such research. And she's not alone: at least two other American startups are pursuing very similar plans. They are backed by tech billionaires from Silicon Valley. Investors, proponents of progress, and pronatalists, who see the declining birth rate as a threat to humanity, are euphoric. Others, including scientists and ethicists, view the companies' intentions with concern.

Tie originally named her New York-based company the Manhattan Project – like the U.S. atomic bomb program in the 1940s. The startup's co-founder is geneticist Eriona Hysolli, who previously headed biological research at Colossal Biosciences. This is the company that aims to revive extinct animal species and recently made headlines with the alleged resurrection of the direwolf.

The two women appear to be ahead of their competitors: They have already hired their first employees. And they can draw on the expertise of experienced researchers. Their advisors include the renowned New York reproductive medicine specialist Norbert Gleicher, a data scientist and two primatologists from Oregon Health and Science University.

## Wedding with the creator of designer babies

The love affair that Cathy Tie made public last spring caused quite a stir: she married none other than He Jiankui, the creator of the Chinese designer babies. Previously, the biophysicist had served a three-year prison sentence in China for «illegal medical practices» because he had used CRISPR to modify the genes of embryos in order to make them immune to HIV. The current status of the three children conceived in this way is unclear. Since his release, He has been trying to re-establish himself as a researcher.

However, the two are no longer together, and Tie asserts in interviews time and again that He also has nothing to do with Manhattan Genomics.

What Tie also consistently emphasizes – and no one disputes – is that CRISPR has enormous potential. If the tool intervenes early in an embryo's DNA, as Manhattan Genomics plans, the changes will later be present in all cells of its body. This includes germline cells, sperm and egg cells. Removing a harmful mutation would protect not only the individual but also future generations from the disease.

With her company, Tie initially wants to tackle serious diseases caused by a single gene. «There are many of these,» she says, citing examples such as cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease, blood disorders like sickle cell anemia and hereditary cancers.

But germline editing is currently banned almost everywhere. Not only in European countries, but also in the U.S. or in China. «Quite rightly so,» says CRISPR researcher Jacob Corn from ETH Zurich. «There is still no study that shows the method is safe.» Instead, experiments demonstrate that CRISPR treatment of embryos can lead to so-called off-target effects: unintended changes that affect not the target gene, but other sections of DNA.

Such damage has also occurred in the Chinese designer babies. This was discovered by the American CRISPR researcher Kiran Musunuru, who had access to the experimental data of He Jiankui. According to Musunuru, these off-target effects likely increase the cancer risk of the gene-edited children.

## First therapies based on CRISPR

Corn says that science is not yet ready for interventions in the germline. However, gene editing of specific body cells has already found its way into clinical practice.

Since 2023, a CRISPR-based therapy has been approved for adolescents and adults with sickle cell anemia. And last year, an even newer method, base editing, was used on a baby with a metabolic disorder: the tailored treatment corrected the DNA in the liver cells of little KJ Muldoon in the U.S. These gene changes are not passed on to the next generation.

Corn is convinced that such therapies, focused on somatic cells, could also be developed for many other diseases. "Research should now concentrate on this, less on germline editing."

Furthermore, options already exist for parents who carry disease genes. If they use preimplantation genetic diagnosis during in-vitro fertilization, they can select from several embryos those that have an intact gene. Only in rare cases, such as when there are too few embryos, is this not possible.

This is precisely where Cathy Tie wants to support parents. "Couples attempting in-vitro fertilization are typically older," she says. "Often they don't have many embryos to choose from."

Tie believes that germline editing research is too restricted. «We want to show that the method has become safer thanks to recent developments,» she says. To that end, her company is initially planning experiments with mice, primates and human cells. «Hopefully, we can persuade the regulatory authorities to allow more research in this area.» Given the Trump administration's deregulating stance, such a step is quite possible in the coming years.

Manhattan Genomics plans to apply its method clinically in the long term, as confirmed by reproductive medicine specialist Norbert Gleicher, who sits on the company's scientific advisory board. All research is to be conducted in the U.S. and in a transparent manner. «I have been assured that no trials are planned abroad and that contact has already been established with the American authorities,» he says.

## Trials in countries with laxer regulations

The two other startups that want to tackle germline editing, however, may choose to conduct their experiments in countries with less stringent regulations. According to the Wall Street Journal, Preventive and Bootstrap Bio are planning trials in Honduras or the United Arab Emirates.

Preventive's founder is molecular biologist Lucas Harrington. He earned his doctorate in the lab of Jennifer Doudna, who received the Nobel Prize in 2020 for her groundbreaking work on CRISPR. His startup is financially backed by Brian Armstrong, CEO of the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase and by the husband of OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. Bootstrap Bio's founder and CEO is the young entrepreneur Chase Denecke. Little is known about his company, and he does not disclose its funding. Manhattan Genomics, incidentally, also does not.

Some scientists are uneasy about private companies wanting to advance the sensitive research area of gene editing. For example, bioethicist Kelly Ormond from ETH Zurich is concerned that experiments conducted outside of academia are not adequately regulated. «Unlike universities in the U.S., which have their own ethics committees, these companies are free to rely on less stringent guidelines,» she explains.

Others, however, find it problematic that companies want to relocate abroad. This move, combined with American entrepreneurial culture, could dangerously accelerate the startups' approach.

In the tech world, the motto is "Move fast and break things": innovations are released quickly, even if they are still buggy. "That might work for developing a smartphone, but not for babies," says Jacob Corn.

Bootstrap Bio has the most ambitious plans. While the other two companies aim solely to prevent diseases, the Californian startup's website states that its goal is "to give as many people as possible the opportunity to choose their own genes (and those of their offspring)." The company intends to offer genetic interventions in areas such as height and intelligence.

This would mean that humanity would take its evolution into its own hands. The philosopher Jonathan Anomaly, a leading figure among pronatalists in Silicon Valley, initially called this "liberal eugenics," but now prefers the term "genetic enhancement."

The emerging pronatalist movement in the U.S. is already using biotech tools to ensure that, from their perspective, the best possible babies are born. Companies like Nucleus and Heliospect Genomics offer genetic screenings of embryos suitable for in-vitro fertilization.

Parents can then, for example, choose a child with brown hair, blue eyes, above-average intelligence and a low risk of breast cancer. At least, that is the promise. How accurate the predictions are for these traits, which often depend on many genes, is a matter of debate. Hundreds of children are said to have been born this way, including one of Elon Musk's.

## Critics fear a new eugenics.

Direct intervention in the genome, as planned by Tie and her competitors, seems like the next logical step. Critics fear a new eugenics – a world in which the wealthy can buy higher intelligence and better health.

Partly due to such fears, several scientific societies called for a ten-year moratorium on germline editing last year . However, many researchers believe this goes too far. Jacob Corn agrees: «I'm not a fan of rigid moratoria that run for long periods. Such an approach makes no sense,» he says.

Regulation must be based on facts, he said. While genome editing is not yet safe enough, it might be in a few years. «And then we as a society will have to decide whether and in which cases we want to use it.»

What Corn appreciates about the emergence of new startups, despite his concerns, is that they are already generating an important public debate.
