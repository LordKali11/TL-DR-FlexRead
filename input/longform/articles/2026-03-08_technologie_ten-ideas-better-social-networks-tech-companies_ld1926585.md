# Ten ideas for better social networks that tech companies have kept quiet about

*Scroll pauses, night modes, and a reset button for the algorithm: Internal documents show that employees of social networks worked on better protecting young people on the platforms. But profits were more important to the companies than protecting minors.*

**Ruth Fulterer, Gioia da Silva, Olivia Fischer (illustration)** · Technologie · 2026-03-08 · 1859 words · [ld.1926585](https://www.nzz.ch/technologie/zehn-ideen-fuer-bessere-soziale-netzwerke-die-tech-firmen-verschwiegen-haben-ld.1926585)

What are Instagram, TikTok, and other social media platforms doing to young people? This concern is on many people's minds. Governments are discussing bans . In Switzerland, entrepreneur Guido Fluri launched an initiative this week aimed at providing greater protection in the digital sphere and holding tech companies accountable. And courts in the US will soon decide whether these platforms can be held responsible for the depression, anxiety, and eating disorders of their young users.

The plaintiffs argue that social networks are designed to harm and addict young people. Among the strongest pieces of evidence in court are internal documents such as emails and presentations from the tech companies, as well as statements from whistleblowers . Many of these documents were brought to light for the first time through the trials.

The documents show that employees recognized a risk to the mental health of young users and worked on measures to make social networks a safer place for teenagers. Based on these documents, we highlight their most creative ideas and why they were rarely implemented.

We confronted the companies with the allegations from this article. We were only able to include responses from TikTok. We received no statement from Meta and Snap, the owners of Instagram and Snapchat.

1. Avoiding FOMO elements

On TikTok, Snapchat, Instagram and even WhatsApp, there are posts that disappear after 24 hours, known as Stories. Among teenagers, these create a fear of missing out (FOMO). Anyone who isn't online often enough might miss a friend's post. These ephemeral elements could simply be eliminated for children – or Stories for them could be set to disappear later, for example, after a week. This would relieve them of the pressure to be constantly online.

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/02/26/4ebc7b0b-16b4-4c51-8f8c-16d5ac1478bd.png?width=1024)
**

2. Mental hygiene streak instead of Snapstreak

Social media platforms are turning teenage friendships into a business model. If two users on Snapchat send each other a picture or video every day for three days, the app displays a fire emoji (🔥) next to their friend's name. This is called a streak. After four consecutive days, they're rewarded with a counter. If the two users continue to send each other a photo or video daily, the counter keeps increasing. However, if they don't reply within 24 hours, the fire goes out. All the effort was for nothing; the streak is over.

Teenagers are thus forced to log into Snapchat at least once a day. Forced to abstain from online activity, some therefore have others maintain their streaks. For example, they entrust their smartphones to their parents during ski trips so that the parents can keep the streak going. Other teenagers have emailed Evan Spiegel, the CEO of Snap, asking him to reactivate a streak that had died.

Spiegel recognized how problematic this feature was. Documents from the plaintiffs cite internal records indicating that Spiegel himself had requested a plan to «kill» the Streak feature, calling it «the right thing to do.» However, the change Snapchat actually implemented went in a different direction: Since 2023, users have been able to pay to restore a Streak. According to the plaintiffs' documents, the feature is said to have generated $47 million in revenue in its first year.

Reward elements also work for «healthy» goals, such as in learning, fitness, and meditation apps. What if they were used to encourage responsible app use? Why not include reward emojis for «not using the app at night,» «48-hour digital detox,» or «only opening the app for 15 minutes»?

3. Night mode to protect sleep

Teenagers are sleeping less than they would like because they can't put their phones down. Internal studies by the platforms themselves confirm this. This could be changed.

According to presentation slides available to the plaintiffs, Instagram found that nearly half of all teenagers visit the platform at least once a week between midnight and 4 a.m. – in two out of three cases prompted by a notification. Such notifications can be turned off at night. After years of pressure, TikTok and Instagram finally did so. Snapchat does not offer a similar sleep mode.

Furthermore, a color filter, such as black and white or a strong blue light filter, could make the content less stimulating. Such a filter was discussed at Instagram but not implemented. The plaintiffs cite the testimony of former employees.

China shows that things can be done very differently: The country enforced a strict nighttime curfew for young people on Douyin, the Chinese version of TikTok. The app is completely blocked for young users at night.

4. Tools for parents to set usage time

In China, the state regulates screen time – in the West, this could at least be made possible for parents. Parents already have the option of linking their profiles to those of their teenagers on some social networks. On TikTok and Instagram, they can set time limits for their children, establish blackout periods, and see how long their child spends on the platform. This allows them to adjust blackout times to their individual sleep needs and ensure that a child doesn't access social media during school hours.

However, this appears to have little effect. Meta's own research found that parental controls have hardly any impact on teenagers' usage. Paradoxically, teenagers whose parents reported more strictly controlling their usage actually spent more time on the platform, according to an internal document cited by plaintiffs . This could indicate that parents only increase their monitoring when they are already worried about their child.

The situation might be different if all parents were required to grant access to their children's profiles. The approval process could be designed in such a way as to raise parents' awareness of potential harm and dangers.

5. No like counter

Under the codename «Project Daisy,» Instagram intensively researched how to hide public like counters. Participants in the experiment could still see how many people had liked their picture. However, the counter remained hidden from all other users.

Internal tests proved this improved well-being and significantly reduced social pressure. Users would be «significantly less likely to feel worse,» plaintiffs quoted from an internal document. Meta nevertheless decided to keep the public like counter. Tests showed that removing it had reduced user dwell time by 0.29% and advertising revenue by 1%.

Instead, the feature was buried deep in the settings as an opt-in option. As a result, it is hardly ever used.

6. A way out of the rabbit hole

The platforms are aware that young people can quickly fall into harmful filter bubbles. For example, an Instagram product manager wrote in an internal message, which the plaintiffs quote: «If you start following borderline accounts or interacting with such content, our recommendation algorithms will push you into a rabbit hole with even worse content.»

The algorithm increasingly shows young people content they are attracted to, even if it involves the glorification of suicide or eating disorders. This problem could be fixed.

A team at Meta developed an artificial intelligence that recognizes videos that trigger self-deprecation in teenagers. For one in ten teenagers, 57% of the suggested videos fell into this category. These videos could easily be blocked for users under 16. According to statements from those involved in the plaintiffs' documents, Instagram CEO Adam Mosseri halted the project. Two employees reportedly resigned in disappointment as a result.

7. Abolishing location sharing

Snapchat encourages young people to constantly share their location with their contacts. These contacts can see the location of all their friends at a glance on a map. Negative feelings are practically guaranteed when someone realizes that friends are meeting up without them.

Even more serious: Snapchat's location sharing feature has already been used by several men to determine the whereabouts of teenage girls or to stalk them. The men pretended to be teenage girls on Snapchat in order to befriend them and constantly access their location.

Snapchat didn't remove this feature after these incidents. Instead, competitor Instagram followed suit in 2025. At least on Instagram, location sharing is disabled by default, not enabled. Instagram only shares the location when the app is open.

8. Slowed-down scrolling

Douyin, the Chinese version of TikTok, enforces a five-second break for users under 14 who have been scrolling for an extended period. These breaks cannot be shortened. After 40 minutes of use, the app locks itself – a requirement of the Chinese government. This measure has curbed the excessive use of the app by young teenagers.

TikTok, the parent company of Douyin, has not implemented mandatory screen timeouts. Former Google ethicist Tristan Harris, among others, compared Douyin to spinach and TikTok to opium for this reason. TikTok told the NZZ that there is no consensus on how much screen time is «too much.» However, since 2023 , underage TikTok users have had to enter a code after 60 minutes to continue scrolling.

Scrolling could also be slowed down in other ways. For example, by not allowing videos to play automatically (autoplay), but instead requiring users to click on each video to start it. This would make watching new videos a more conscious decision. Such measures have been discussed at all tech companies, but only YouTube has disabled autoplay for young people in the default settings.

9. No contact from strangers

In 2020, 500,000 teenagers on Instagram had «inappropriate interactions» with adults every day – and that's just in English-speaking countries. This is according to internal company documents. For years, employees have been discussing the fact that cyber grooming, in which adults establish contact with minors to sexually harass, manipulate, abuse, or even blackmail them , is a real problem.

For a long time, nothing happened. But the solution is simple: young people should only be able to receive direct messages from people they already follow. Unknown adults should not be able to contact them, nor should they be able to add them to group chats.

After years of external pressure, Instagram, Snapchat and TikTok now have such restrictions – however, there are still loopholes, for example through comments or when teenagers or adults provide a false age.

10. Effective age limits

Effective age verification is a fundamental prerequisite for all the measures described here. After all, young people can circumvent any protection by using adult accounts. Currently, children often use social networks even before the official minimum age of 13, that is, before they are cognitively capable of assessing the impact, consequences, and manipulation of images and videos online.

Some platforms, including Instagram, now use facial scans to estimate the age of their users. This is an infringement on user privacy and can also be circumvented. Yet for at least four years, there have been systems to verify age online that preserve user privacy. These can, for example, prove that a user belongs to a certain age group – such as over 18 or between 13 and 16 – without the user having to disclose a photo of their face, their name or their date of birth. The Swiss e-ID and the planned EU Digital Identity Wallet are also examples of such secure systems.

TikTok says it strives to combine privacy and security. However, users can simply enter a date of birth. The company attempts to identify users under 13 based on published videos and their behavior on the platform. Through a pilot project, «thousands» of accounts belonging to children under 13 were reportedly deleted in 2025.

The research shows that the companies behind social networks have dedicated employees with good ideas for making the platforms a safer place for young people. It's high time management implemented at least a few of these ideas.
