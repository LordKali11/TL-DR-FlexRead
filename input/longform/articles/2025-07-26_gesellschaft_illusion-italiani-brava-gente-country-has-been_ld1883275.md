# The illusion of «Italiani, brava gente»: A country has been downplaying fascism for eighty years – and cannot shake it off.

*How a perpetrator country created a victim myth.*

**Giorgio Scherrer (text), Massimiliano Corteselli (images)** · Gesellschaft · 2025-07-26 · 3219 words · [ld.1883275](https://www.nzz.ch/gesellschaft/die-illusion-der-italiani-brava-gente-ein-land-verharmlost-seit-achtzig-jahren-den-faschismus-und-kommt-nicht-von-ihm-los-ld.1883275)

High in the Italian Abruzzo region, on a mountainside almost 2000 meters above sea level, stand 20,000 trees – pine trees, a particularly resilient variety. Each one was planted by hand here in 1939, when a gigantic monument was erected.

The trees – located on Monte Giano, a two-hour drive from Rome – form three gigantic letters: a D, a U, and an X. When it snows in the Abruzzo region, the inscription can be seen all the way to the capital, a dark message on a white background.

«DUX» – that stands for Duce, the title of the fascist dictator Benito Mussolini. He is immortalized here in roots, trunks and leaves.

Eighty years ago, when Mussolini's reign of terror ended with the conclusion of World War II, the trees began to grow wild. The inscription became increasingly illegible. The memory of the Duce faded. A few years ago, some of the trees even went up in flames.

And yet the inscription is still there. In the years since Mussolini's fall, it has been restored repeatedly. In 2004, the state carefully pruned the overgrown pine trees until the text was as legible as on the first day. In 2018, 200 volunteers replaced the burned trunks with fresh saplings.

A monument to a mass murderer, right in the heart of a democracy: In Italy, that's normal. A gigantic obelisk in Rome, a mausoleum in his birthplace of Predappio, monuments and inscriptions throughout the country: Anyone wanting to pay homage to the Duce and his regime doesn't have to look far.

This is no coincidence. Fascism has been history in Italy for eighty years – and for just as long, the memory of it has been whitewashed, repressed, and forgotten.

A double lie dominates the memory of this event. The lie that all of Italy once liberated itself from Mussolini through its own efforts and thereby atoned for his crimes. And the lie that these crimes weren't so bad after all, that the fascists themselves were actually more victims than perpetrators.

## A country of resistance fighters

The first lie is born even before the war is over. In the summer of 1943, as the Allies slowly fight their way toward victory, they land in Sicily on July 10th. Shortly afterward, the Italian monarch, Vittorio Emanuele III, deposes the fascist Duce, Benito Mussolini, with the support of the military and parts of the fascist elite.

The calculation: The war is over anyway. So it's better to quickly switch to the winning side.

The new head of government becomes Pietro Badoglio, a marshal in the Italian military who committed barbaric war crimes for Mussolini. It is Badoglio who negotiates a ceasefire and declares Germany, a long-time ally, an enemy overnight. He does so so chaotically that thousands of Italian soldiers are taken prisoner by the Germans or forced to flee headlong.

And yet. Italy, suddenly part of the Allies. Italy, somehow also on the right side: It is the birth of a collective self-deception.

This view was reinforced in the months between the defection and the end of the war in Europe. During this time, Italy was a divided country, embroiled in civil war. In the north, Adolf Hitler established a satellite state, the Italian Social Republic, led by Mussolini. In the south, a country of fascists acted as if the previous twenty years had never happened.

And somewhere in between, in mountains, hills, and hiding places, the Italian resistance, the Resistenza, fought against the German troops and Mussolini's remaining loyalists. The exact number of people who belonged to the resistance movement remains a matter of debate. What is certain is that they risked their lives fighting fascism. In brutal reprisals, entire villages were burned to the ground.

Militarily, their success is limited, but ideologically, the Resistenza soon becomes a central element of Italian post-war identity.

In April 1945, northern Italy fell to the Allies, who advanced from the south. Simultaneously, partisan units streamed out of the mountains to meet them. On April 28, Benito Mussolini was captured while attempting to flee towards Switzerland. Partisans shot him and brought his body to Milan, where it was mutilated with gunshots, whippings, spit, and urine.

Hanging upside down from the roof of a gas station, the Duce looks like dead cattle. «The life of free Italy,» writes historian Sergio Luzzato, «begins thus with a celebration of death.»

The image of resistance fighters bringing the brutal dictator to justice and liberating the country from all corners: it is powerful. And it becomes a collective myth. The supposed self-liberation from fascism, in the collective perception, is also a liberation from any responsibility for his actions.

Suddenly, nobody wants to have been a fascist, and if they do, it's only under duress. Instead, Mussolini loyalists throughout Italy are discovering that they've always been resistance fighters – a country of resistance fighters.

## The lie becomes the truth

This initial lie – about an Italy that ultimately stood on the right side as a whole and liberated itself – is reinforced by a fateful political compromise. Left-wing partisan groups, bourgeois ex-fascists, and the military quickly agree on a strategy for reconstruction.

The message is this: Instead of overhauling the state, the administration, the economy, the universities – completely permeated by the old regime during twenty years of fascist rule – the old guard remains where it is. It's a reinvention based on the old. Blind forgiveness instead of atonement.

Unlike in Germany, the new government is not being forced to remove party officials from the state apparatus. The consistent prosecution and conviction of leading war criminals, as seen in the Nuremberg Trials, does not exist in Italy.

Virtually all fascist officials and judges remain in office. Various laws from the dictatorship also remain in effect – for example, the one that mandates film censorship. It is actively applied by the democratic governments of the post-war period as well.

On June 22, 1946, the new Italian government also enacted a far-reaching amnesty. Almost no one who had murdered, killed, fought, or incited hatred for fascism had to fear legal consequences. The civil war, the occupation of northern Italy by German troops: all of that, according to this logic, was punishment enough.

The amnesty becomes the basis for widespread amnesia. There is no «denazification» in Italy like in Germany. Instead, the myth of «Italiani, brava gente» arises – the notion that this nice people couldn't possibly be so bad after all.

In the post-war period, this self-image solidified. While the First Republic was founded on a broad anti-fascist consensus spanning from the left to the bourgeois camp, the re-establishment of the fascist party was prohibited by law, and the monarchy was also discredited for its support of fascism. It was finally abolished in June 1946 in a truly democratic manner, through a referendum – the first in which women were allowed to participate.

And yet, Italian anti-fascism does not stand on a solid foundation. Because: it lacks a genuine culture of remembrance.

The crimes of Mussolini, the crimes of Italy, in which an entire nation participated, mostly cheering, with outstretched arms giving the fascist salute: they are forgotten, repressed and downplayed.

## The repressed past

Before it ended with Mussolini's death in 1945, fascist rule had lasted for over twenty years – the so-called «Ventennio.» Thus, Mussolini's regime not only served as a model for Hitler's Germany, but it also lasted twice as long. During this time, the subversion of state, economy, and society was total: journalists, civil servants, professors, and trade unionists were brought into line – or removed.

From the early 1920s onward, political opponents were intimidated by fascist thugs using violence, or by administering castor oil, a potent laxative. Later, they were imprisoned on islands. An unknown number were killed. The most famous case is the assassination of the socialist parliamentarian Giacomo Matteotti on June 10, 1924.

The superiority of the Italian «race,» its historical predestination as rulers of the Mediterranean – this permeated fascist thought. And the actions of the regime: In brutal colonial wars in Libya and Abyssinia (now Ethiopia), the Italians killed between 450,000 and 860,000 people.

They dropped mustard gas bombs from airplanes, bombed refugee camps, established concentration camps, and sent civilians on death marches. The Italian invasion of Abyssinia – an independent state and member of the League of Nations – even impressed the Nazi propagandist Joseph Goebbels in 1936.

International law is being violated – and the international community is doing nothing: This is a key realization for Nazi Germany.

The colonial mass extermination was commanded by Pietro Badoglio, later an ally of the Allies and the first head of government of anti-fascist Italy. He was never held accountable for his war crimes.

In 1938, Italy enacted sweeping racial laws that primarily affected tens of thousands of Jews in the country. In 1943, Mussolini's rump state in the north became part of the extermination machinery of the Holocaust. Around 8,600 Italian Jews were deported from the Italian Social Republic, most of them murdered. This is in addition to the atrocities committed by Italian troops in Yugoslavia, Albania, and Greece after Italy's entry into the war in 1940.

Researchers estimate that a total of one million people fell victim to Italian fascism.

## Fascism as a «disease»

The fact that these deaths remained taboo for decades—as did the fact that more than one in ten Italians was a party member—is related to a central characteristic of Italian fascism. Unlike German fascism, it is less clearly defined ideologically. There is no pure doctrine, no canon—like Hitler's «Mein Kampf.»

Monarchists, conservative Catholics, radical Futurists and former socialists, like Mussolini himself: they all find a place in the fascist movement.

This allows former fascists after 1945 to portray all criminal acts as the result of a corruption of «true,» benign fascism. Or, even more popular: as the product of a collective mental derangement.

In its most extreme form, this argument declares fascism as a whole to be a «disease» from which democratic Italy suffered for twenty years. It portrays it as an alien trait in the collective psyche, a foreign body in national history.

This view – popularized by the well-known philosopher and anti-fascist Benedetto Croce – is doubly convenient. Because no one is to blame for illnesses. And they can potentially be cured – once the pathogen is removed, they soon disappear.

As early as July 27, 1943, the day of Mussolini's removal from power, Croce noted in his diary: «Fascism already seems to me like a piece of the past – like a circle that has closed. I feel no desire for revenge.»

This collective amnesia was facilitated by the beginning of the Cold War. Italy, the country with the largest communist party outside the Eastern Bloc, was considered contested territory. Anti-communism began to replace anti-fascism.

## The myth is crumbling

The legacy of fascism lives on, not in secret, but in full public view. As early as 1946, Mussolini's former loyalists founded a new, neo-fascist party – the Movimento Sociale Italiano. It repeatedly escaped being banned thanks to legal maneuvering.

Just 15 years after the end of the war – in 1960 – something happened that is still considered taboo elsewhere today: A conservative government secured a majority in parliament by winning the votes of the neo-fascists.

The next turning point in Italian memory culture – when repression turns into pride in parts of the fascist past – finally begins when bombs, death and terror return to the country.

The «anni di piombo»—the leaden years of right-wing and left-wing terrorism—began in the late 1960s and lasted into the 1980s. Fascist splinter groups indiscriminately killed civilians in brutal attacks on banks, trains, and government buildings. Meanwhile, the far-left Red Brigades murdered and kidnapped people in the name of the revolution. Between 1969 and 1987, 419 people were killed and 1,181 injured.

*On March 16, 1978, left-wing terrorists kidnapped the designated Italian Prime Minister Aldo Moro from his car. His body was found 55 days later.* (AP)

Many far-right attacks are initially attributed to left-wing terrorists. At the same time, the desire for security and order is growing. Both provide fertile ground for Mussolini nostalgia.

During the dark years, criticism of left-wing antifascism also became socially acceptable – because it was increasingly less associated with the resistance fighters of the Resistenza and more and more with the terrorists of the Red Brigades.

The Resistance myth is crumbling. The heroes of yesteryear are fading. The fragile framework of the Italian post-war order is beginning to slowly collapse.

During the 1980s, neo-fascist parties gained strength and were able to mobilize voters beyond their aging base. A generation of young people who had never known fascism began to take an interest in the ideas of their fathers and grandfathers.

## The victim myth arises

On the ruins of the Resistance myth, the second great lie that Italy still tells itself about its past emerged in the 1990s: that the Fascists, too, were victims of a senseless war started by Nazi Germany. And that they actually only wanted what was best for their country.

It is a time when a forgotten past becomes a glorified one. A time when a perpetrator nation creates a victim myth for itself.

The central figure in this reinvention is Silvio Berlusconi – entrepreneur, bon vivant, and owner of several television channels. In 1994, he is elected Prime Minister. The Italian people place their trust in him, the charismatic showman, after the Christian Democrats, who had ruled for decades, were brought down by a corruption scandal.

The old party system is breaking down; with TV star Berlusconi, Italian politics is becoming show business.

It is an earthquake that shatters old certainties. This becomes clear at the latest when Berlusconi makes the neo-fascists a permanent part of his government – ​​and thus definitively normalizes them politically.

Mussolini did some good things for Italy, the Cavaliere explained. Italian fascism certainly had its flaws, «but nothing comparable to Nazism or Communism.» The Duce's dictatorship was «benign.»

It is the narrative that still dominates Italy today: Nazi Germany was the true villain, with its war and its racial laws. Hitler merely seduced the harmless Mussolini – and with him the unsuspecting Italians – into going along with it.

Instead of focusing on the crimes of the fascist regime, the crimes of the German troops in northern Italy are increasingly coming to the fore. And those of the Resistance fighters: in the bloody final phase of the war, they killed around 12,000 Italians in acts of vigilante justice.

The memory of massacres of the Italian population in Istria and Dalmatia by Yugoslav partisans is also increasingly being brought into focus, as evidence of Italy's victim status.

Meanwhile, critical research is becoming the target of political attacks. For example, the state broadcaster RAI blocked the airing of a critical BBC film about the legacy of fascism.

In 1996, a French television crew accompanied a 19-year-old activist from a neo-fascist youth organization during her election campaign. She said things like: «Mussolini was a good politician. Everything he did, he did for Italy.»

Her name: Giorgia Meloni.

## The Duce returns

Throughout the country, the restoration of fascist monuments began during the Berlusconi era. Municipalities renamed streets after Giorgio Almirante, a fascist racial theorist who entered parliament as a representative for the neo-fascists after the war.

In Rome, the «Apotheosis of Fascism»—a mural of the Duce with his followers—is being restored and uncovered. It is located in a state building, the headquarters of the Italian Olympic Committee. To this day, the committee's press conferences are held beneath the image. Representatives of democratic Italy sit like dwarfs under a godlike Mussolini.

But nothing can surpass what the village of Filettino near Rome still proudly maintains: an iron archway, erected in 1938, reminiscent of the one above the entrance to the Auschwitz concentration camp. Instead of «Arbeit macht frei» (Work sets you free), it reads: «Parco Rodolfo Graziani.» Graziani was one of the leaders of the mass murders in Abyssinia and an ally of the «Third Reich» until 1945.

A children's playground in his birthplace is named after him. It was restored a few years ago for a quarter of a million euros.

Predappio, the birthplace of Benito Mussolini, has long been a place of pilgrimage. Thousands flock to the town every year on his birthday and death anniversary, raising their arms in the Roman salute – the Italian version of the Hitler salute. This salute is also frequently seen elsewhere in Italy, for example at police parades. In 2009, the then Minister of Tourism made it at a Carabinieri festival. She was not fired.

In addition, there are busts, lighters, kitchen calendars, and aprons bearing the Duce's likeness, sold at market stalls throughout the country. Fascism as pop culture, Mussolini as a jolly loudmouth.

Nothing is serious, everything is relative: This is the current state of Italian memory culture. An attitude with consequences for the country's political culture.

## The Meloni Paradox

The writer Umberto Eco once compared fascism to an «ideological supermarket.» «The Italians could pick and choose what they liked.»

They still do this today when they elect their politicians. Berlusconi, the comedian Beppe Grillo, the post-fascist Meloni: in Italy, success belongs to charismatic alpha figures for whom style and presentation count more than clear policy. The strategy of ideological vagueness, which once characterized fascism, remains the path to political success.

The current governing party, Fratelli d'Italia, is also a melting pot for right-wing voters of all stripes – from Mussolini nostalgics to former Christian Democrats.

They all see something different in Meloni: the young activist from the past who only appeared to moderate her views; the charismatic populist with her casual pronouncements and Roman accent; or a moderate conservative who has long since left her youthful indiscretions behind.

Meanwhile, their party's logo still features the emblem that has been the symbol of post-war fascists since 1946: a blazing flame in the colors of the Italian tricolor. It is meant to represent the eternal flame burning on Mussolini's tomb.

Is this the return of fascism in a new guise, which Umberto Eco warned against in a famous essay in 1995? Has the past truly become the present in Italy?

Even a few years before Meloni's election, Italian filmmakers were seeking an answer to this question. They had a bald man in a Mussolini costume wander through the streets of Rome, saluting, his uniform covered in medals.

The scene, filmed for the comedy «Sono tornato» («I'm back»), was intended to shock. Just like its German inspiration: «Look Who's Back» (2015), in which Adolf Hitler paid Germany another visit after seventy years. The Führer in the streets, passersby cheering him with outstretched arms: it was a scandal, a sensation, a box office hit.

The Italian remake was a different story. Nobody cared. The Romans ignored the costumed Duce. There was neither frenzied cheering nor much protest. The film flopped at the box office, languishing at number 65 in the charts.

The return of fascism? The Italians didn't care. Perhaps because it had never truly disappeared from their daily lives.
