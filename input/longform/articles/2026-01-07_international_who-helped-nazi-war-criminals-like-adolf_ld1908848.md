# Who helped Nazi war criminals like Adolf Eichmann escape to Argentina? The answer might lie in the archives there.

*During the Cold War, the mysterious Odessa Organization was blamed for the escape of Nazi war criminals. Historians consider this refuted. The role of Argentina's President Perón is dubious.*

Under cover of darkness, agents of the Israeli secret service Mossad overpowered him and took him to their hideout. There, Klement confessed to being Adolf Eichmann. Eleven days later, the Mossad brought one of the main perpetrators of the Holocaust to Israel, where he was executed in 1962 after a sensational trial.

The Holocaust Museum in Buenos Aires displays photographs taken by Mossad during the planning of Eichmann's abduction. They identified him based on the unusual shape of his left ear, says Marcia Ras, the museum's historian.

However, his impoverished living conditions – his house had neither running water nor electricity – initially led the agents to doubt that they had actually tracked down Eichmann. After all, it was suspected that Nazi criminals like him were part of a powerful organization.

## The «largest escape organization in world history»

Above all, the US military investigated rumors of an organized mass exodus of Nazis after the war, but found no concrete evidence. It was Holocaust survivor and «Nazi hunter» Simon Wiesenthal who tied together the many loose ends of the stories circulating at the time. Thus, an «Organization of Former SS Members,» or Odessa for short, smuggled Nazi criminals into the South American country with the help of the Vatican, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), and the governments of Spain, Italy, and Argentina.

![Simon Wiesenthal, the legendary Nazi hunter (photo from 1967).](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/12/31/efc87f13-53f1-4fd7-ae0c-2d1435018206.jpg?crop=397,496,x27,y43)
*Simon Wiesenthal, the legendary Nazi hunter (photo from 1967).* (Getty)

There, the refugees were to prepare for a return to Germany in order to proclaim the «Fourth Reich.» As early as August 1944, nine months before the end of the war, high-ranking Nazis and major industrialists, facing imminent defeat, are said to have planned the mass exodus and the transfer of designs for new wonder weapons and assets to Argentina in Strasbourg.

«Just as they had perfectly organized the mass murder, they now perfectly organized the escape of the murderers,» wrote Wiesenthal in his book «Justice, Not Revenge.» His narrative of the «greatest escape organization in world history» owed its popularity primarily to the 1972 bestseller «The Odessa File» by Frederick Forsyth, which was written with Wiesenthal’s assistance and deals with sinister Nazi networks in Argentina.

This narrative gained traction partly because there was indeed widespread sympathy for Hitler and his fight against communism in Argentina – both within the German émigré communities and among the government. The Allies attempted to exploit this during the war, claiming at the end of 1944 that leading Nazis had already fled to Argentina. They hoped this would weaken the morale of the Wehrmacht.

The East-West conflict that erupted at the end of the war led Stalin to claim at the Potsdam Conference in July 1945 that Hitler had fled to Spain or Argentina. In reality, the Russians had already identified Hitler's body in Berlin. However, the West—including the Vatican—was accused of complicity with the fascists. Thus, the East German State Security (Stasi) was constantly on the lookout for—and indeed existing—connections between the West German establishment and Nazi criminals.

## Personal networks instead of global organizations

Historians like Holger Meding, Gerald Steinacher, and Heinz Schneppen demystified «Odessa» after the end of the Cold War. They proved that the Strasbourg Conference, intended to plan Odessa, never took place. Some of the alleged participants were already dead by August 1944.

Furthermore, it became clear that it was primarily the chaos at the end of the war that had enabled SS members such as Eichmann or the Auschwitz doctor Josef Mengele to go into hiding in Germany for years with false identities and the help of comrades and friends.

During his escape to northern Italy in 1950, Eichmann used a route recommended by comrades. He later recounted having to pay smugglers a considerable sum for the crossing of the Alps. Furthermore, he could count on the assistance of Nazi sympathizers in Austria and South Tyrol.

Italy was safe, as the Allies had already withdrawn by the end of 1945. And South Tyrol was the perfect place to issue false Italian documents. Following the German-Italian Option Agreement of 1939, tens of thousands of German-speaking South Tyroleans had been resettled in the German Reich, many of whom were now returning to South Tyrol. They mingled with the fleeing Nazis.

Like the concentration camp doctor Mengele, Eichmann also received forged identity papers from the South Tyrolean municipality of Tramin. Thus, the Protestant Eichmann, born in Solingen, became the Catholic Ricardo Klement, born in Bolzano. The excuse that he was a returning ethnic German from South Tyrol explained why he didn't speak Italian.

As a Catholic in Italy, one could also count on the Vatican's help. In Genoa, a Hungarian Franciscan priest provided assistance, while in Rome, the Croatian Krunoslav Draganovic and the Austrian Alois Hudal, rector of the German Priests' College Santa Maria dell'Anima, helped those wishing to emigrate.

Did they know that these individuals were war criminals? That remains unclear. However, communism was considered the enemy at the time, and a possible Nazi past of the refugees seemed secondary. Thus, the church officials confirmed in letters the pro-Catholic and anti-communist views of the person wishing to leave and wrote letters of recommendation to the Red Cross and the Argentine consulate.

Eichmann's Red Cross passport, with which he left Italy via Genoa in June 1950, is on display at the Holocaust Museum in Buenos Aires. Generally, these passports were issued without any background checks. This would have been impossible anyway, as Italy was then home to tens of thousands of undocumented or stateless refugees, including Holocaust survivors on their way to Palestine and citizens of Central and Eastern Europe fleeing the Soviets. These were ideal conditions for Nazis in hiding.

Neither Eichmann nor any other Nazi criminals ever reported that an organization like Odessa helped them escape. Behind the escape movements, there were no networks originating from the «Third Reich,» but rather individual networks of the refugees themselves, says historian Holger Meding from the University of Cologne in a video interview.

## Perón's dubious role

While Wiesenthal's Odessa theory lost credibility among historians, the role of President Juan Domingo Perón, who ruled Argentina from 1946 to 1955, came into sharper focus.

In his 2002 book «The Real Odessa,» Argentinian journalist Uki Goñi portrays Perón as the true mastermind behind the escape organization. According to Goñi, Perón's associates coordinated the operation through a «transit office» in Switzerland, with the Vatican allegedly providing false identities and Swiss banks handling the necessary money transfers.

Perón's sympathy for Hitler and Mussolini is undeniable. He admired the corporatist structure of Italian society and Germany's military organization and industrial capacity. His recruitment of engineers from the «Third Reich» to build up domestic industry was common practice at the time. During the Cold War, thousands of former German Nazi engineers worked for the USA, Canada, or the Soviet Union.

In 1948, Perón established an agency for European immigrants, whose entry permits were issued by Argentine consulates. Did Perón, as Goñi claims, also deliberately use this agency to save war criminals?

This theory remains a subject of domestic political debate in Argentina. President Javier Milei, an outspoken opponent of Perón, promised US senators and the Simon Wiesenthal Center in Buenos Aires in the spring that he would open the archives. The circumstances and financing of the «ratlines,» as the Americans called the Nazi escape routes, were to be investigated. The Wiesenthal Center, named in honor of the Nazi hunter Simon Wiesenthal, who died in 2005, continues to search worldwide for traces of escaped Nazis.

## Yellowed newspaper clippings

In May, at Milei's behest, the National Archives in Buenos Aires made 1,850 archival documents available online under the title «Nazis in Argentina – 1957 to 1985.» They offer nothing new, as they have been accessible to researchers since 1992. However, the yellowed newspaper clippings and documents provide insight into the narratives circulating at the time.

Much attention was paid to the search for Hitler's deputy, Martin Bormann, who, according to Wiesenthal, allegedly headed the Odessa Organization, also known as «The Spider,» from South America. It is said that he had to pay Perón 200 million dollars from the Nazi treasure transported to Argentina by submarine. The wild stories surrounding Bormann end with the discovery of his body in Berlin in 1973, where he was in reality killed during a breakout from the Reich Chancellery in early May 1945.

But do any relevant new findings still lie in Argentine archives? The Commission for the Investigation of Nazi Activities in Argentina (CEANA), appointed in 1997 by then-President Carlos Menem, has already meticulously searched them. The result of the two-year investigation was unequivocal: nothing substantial was found of the assets suspected to be in Argentina, the so-called Nazi treasures.

Furthermore, the number of Nazis who fled to Argentina was significantly lower than the 50,000 figure cited by Wiesenthal. Historian Meding, responsible for investigating Nazi refugees on the commission, estimated that around 40,000 people from German-speaking countries emigrated to Argentina between 1945 and 1952. Among them were approximately 2,000 individuals who could be described as committed Nazis – though not automatically as Nazi war criminals.

The highest-ranking refugees were two state secretaries and an SS group leader. Files were also found on five convicted Nazi war criminals and seven individuals wanted on arrest warrants, against whom no trial had yet been initiated. Six other individuals were already facing trial at the time of their escape. Traces of ten high-ranking Nazis were also discovered, against whom no legal action had been taken.

In total, CEANA found traces of around thirty Nazi war criminals, as well as fifty Croatian war criminals from the fascist Ustaša movement and around one hundred Nazi collaborators, including French and Belgians.

Historian Meding believes that opening previously secret archives of the Argentine intelligence service (SIDE) and of military armaments projects could potentially bring to light «one or two burning secrets».

## Safe haven Argentina?

The perception of Argentina as a country of asylum for Nazis stems from Stalin's claim that Hitler fled to Argentina, as well as from Forsyth's Odessa novel and the Eichmann kidnapping, summarizes Marcia Ras of the Holocaust Museum. However, this stigmatization is unfair, since most Nazi criminals ultimately lived undisturbed in Germany.

Argentina, on the other hand, extradited ghetto and camp commander Josef Schwammberger to Germany in 1990 and Erich Priebke, who was responsible for the massacre in the Ardeatine Caves, to Italy in 1995.

Furthermore, Argentina admitted 45,000 Jews legally before the end of the war, in addition to an unknown number of Jewish people who entered with false papers or secretly across the border, says Ras. After the war, 4,200 Holocaust survivors joined them – a uniquely high number in Latin America.
