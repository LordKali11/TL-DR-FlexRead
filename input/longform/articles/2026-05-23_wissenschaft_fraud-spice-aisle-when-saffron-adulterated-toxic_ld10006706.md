# Fraud in the spice aisle: when saffron is adulterated with toxic dyes

*Inspectors repeatedly discover foreign plants, dyes and other contaminants in spices – in Europe, including in Switzerland. Some of these substances are hazardous to health. It is therefore advisable for consumers to heed certain tips.*

In the Middle Ages, shady spice merchants occasionally ended up at the stake if they had adulterated saffron with safflower petals. But that did not deter the counterfeiters. The scam is still widespread today – and the fraud is not limited to saffron.

Last year, the Zurich Cantonal Laboratory discovered a container of dried parsley in retail that contained celery leaves, as well as thyme that turned out to be savory. Another thyme sample consisted primarily of North American dodder, a parasitic plant that wraps its tendrils around other plants.

Whether such cases are due to deliberate deception, negligent farming practices (where unwanted plants end up in the harvest) or faulty labels is often impossible to determine. What is certain is that herbs and spices frequently do not contain – or do not contain only – what is listed on the ingredients list.

«The consequences for consumers range from mere deception regarding quality to serious health risks,» explains British food researcher Chris Elliott.

Illegal additives endanger both allergy sufferers and general health. Many prohibited additives are not approved for use in food, for example, because they are carcinogenic. While testing methods exist, official inspections are carried out randomly and fraudsters use sophisticated methods, meaning that questionable products repeatedly end up on our spice shelves.

## Scammers even mix dyes into spices

«Wherever in the world we looked for fraud in this area, we found it – and in abundance,» says Elliott. He established the Institute for Global Food Security at Belfast University and founded Bia Analytical, a company that provides laboratory tests and measuring instruments for food.****

Around 10 years ago, Elliott's team discovered that almost a quarter of all oregano samples sold in British and Irish retail consisted of 30% to 70% olive or myrtle leaves. Dried and crumbled, the leaves all look the same. Elliott explains that he also found fraud rates of between 20% and almost 50% in investigations of other herbs and spices.

The results of an EU inspection campaign, in which Switzerland also participated, support Elliott's findings. Inspectors discovered olive leaves in 80 out of 300 oregano samples. Overall, they detected foreign plant material in almost half of the samples.

They also found problems with saffron. Four of 141 saffron samples consisted mainly of safflower blossoms, and one sample primarily contained calendula components. In one of these samples, food inspectors also detected a brownish-red azo dye, which is used in floor wax and shoe polish but is not approved for food.

Problematic dyes were also found in 11 other saffron samples that contained no foreign plant material. Toxic dyes are also repeatedly discovered in turmeric, paprika and chili powder.

The EU campaign analyzed nearly 1,900 samples of six herbs and spices. For pepper, cumin and turmeric, the rate of suspicious samples was in the low double-digit percentages, while for paprika and chili samples it was 6%. The study was conducted five years ago. However, according to sources within the EU Commission, there is no indication that the frequency of fraud has changed.

The problem is also known to the Swiss authorities. «Spices are among the food categories most susceptible to fraud, partly because supply chains are long and complex, making fraud more difficult to detect,» explains the Federal Office for Food Safety and Veterinary Affairs in Bern. The fraud, the office stated, is not limited to prohibited colorings and foreign plant parts. In some cases, products also reportedly contain fillers such as starch, flour or gypsum, which are not declared on the packaging.

## Deception can harm your health

Counterfeiters will stop at nothing to deceive consumers. They mix papaya seeds or empty husks left over from processing pepper berries into peppercorns. Some chili powder contains tomato skins; cumin is adulterated with regular caraway, peanut shells and almond skins.

Cinnamon counterfeiters grind not only the flavor-giving bark but also roots, leaves and other parts of the cinnamon tree. They also adulterate Ceylon cinnamon, which is referred to as «true cinnamon,» with cassia cinnamon. Because the latter is more robust to cultivate, more of it is produced at a lower price.

The fraudsters are willing to risk consumers' health. Cassia cinnamon, for example, contains higher levels of the flavoring agent coumarin, which can damage the liver. Official recommendations advise that those who frequently use a lot of cinnamon, for example in rice pudding, should choose the low-coumarin Ceylon cinnamon. But that's easier said than done, because the products aren't always correctly labeled.

Last year, an EU study examined over 100 cinnamon samples from 13 countries. Nine percent of the products labeled as Ceylon cinnamon were blended with or entirely made of cassia cinnamon.

Other herbs and spices also often contain illegal, potentially harmful ingredients. This applies, for example, to olive leaves found in oregano. Olive tree leaves are not intended for consumption. In some cases, they are heavily contaminated with pesticides.

Components of nuts, as well as celery leaves sold as parsley, can trigger symptoms in allergy sufferers ranging from itchy mouth to anaphylactic shock, a life-threatening circulatory collapse. The same applies to wheat, which must also be strictly avoided by people with the autoimmune disease celiac disease. Their intestinal lining becomes inflamed upon ingesting even the smallest amounts of the grain protein gluten.

Last year, the Zurich Cantonal Laboratory discovered gluten in a sample of ground nutmeg. Previously, the cantonal chemists had detected undeclared wheat flour in spice mixes and pepper. Five years ago, during a testing campaign in Northwest Switzerland, two out of 37 pepper samples tested also failed because they contained wheat flour.

## Hardly noticeable to consumers

This raises the question of how consumers can protect themselves from such products. With products like ground pepper and crumbled herbs, it is virtually impossible for consumers to detect the fraud, says Ilka Haase of the Max Rubner Institute in Karlsruhe. The food chemist heads the National Reference Center for Authentic Foods located there.

A very low price could be an indication of adulteration, for example with saffron, says Haase. «But given the abundance of spices and herbs in the most diverse price categories, no general rule of thumb can be established.»

Even the EU campaign, which analyzed almost 1,900 products, does not provide a clear purchasing recommendation. While the samples came from various sources – from border control points to wholesalers, retailers and online shops – their respective numbers were too small to generate statistically reliable consumer advice.

Aside from that: If customers have to wonder whether a product contains potentially illegal ingredients before buying it, something is wrong. «We should all be able to expect our food to be safe and unadulterated,» says Elliott – adding that companies are responsible for that. Manufacturers who buy herbs and spices have numerous ways to identify and avoid fraudulent products.

The good news for consumers is that reputable suppliers are taking advantage of this. The German Fuchs Group, for example, which also owns brands like Ostmann and Ubena, buys unground raw materials whenever possible and monitors them along the entire supply chain: Initial tests take place in the country of origin, and laboratory analyses are conducted before further processing at the factory. The Fuchs Group stated that adulteration in global supply chains is a known risk that cannot be completely eliminated, adding: «In practice, however, we very rarely encounter this.»

The Swiss spice manufacturer Fridlin, a smaller, family-run company, also claims to have the problem under control. «We have not detected any cases of fraud in the past 15 years,» says André Willimann, who heads sales and product management at Fridlin. He added that the company continues to take the issue very seriously. Fridlin relies on long-standing relationships with trusted suppliers, also prefers unground herbs and spices and subjects them to laboratory analysis.

Control methods – and this is also good news – are constantly improving. Dyes and other additives can be detected in ever smaller quantities, and modern genetic analysis facilitates the identification of foreign plant material. The EU's Joint Research Centre has just published 13 DNA methods that allow inspectors to detect, among other things, safflower and marigold in saffron or caraway in cumin. They can also distinguish between unintentional contamination and deliberate adulteration.

The company founded by Elliott, Bia Analytical, even offers a handheld scanner for on-site quality control that is easy for non-experts to use. This is ideal, for example, for pre-checking goods in wholesale or for incoming goods inspections at borders. The device tests almost 20 herbs and spices, from basil to cinnamon, virtually at the touch of a button. It creates spectroscopic fingerprints of the samples and compares them with stored templates. Deviations indicate the presence of unwanted additives.

A laboratory analysis is necessary for a detailed clarification. Ilka Haase points out that the counterfeiters know very well which analytical methods are currently being used and adapt their fraudulent schemes accordingly: «It's a constant race between the fraudsters and the regulatory authorities.»

Until the inspectors have won the battle, consumers can protect themselves by using fresh herbs instead of dried ones and buying whole spices – such as whole nutmegs, cumin seeds or chili peppers. If this is not possible, for example with paprika for goulash or cinnamon for rice pudding, or if it is simply too much trouble, quality products should be preferred and extremely cheap offers avoided. This not only reduces the risk of fraud but also usually tastes better.
