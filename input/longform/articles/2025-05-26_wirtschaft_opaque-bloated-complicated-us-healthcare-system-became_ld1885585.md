# Opaque, bloated, complicated: why the US healthcare system became as bad as it is today

That's not what good value for money looks like. The US spends more on healthcare than any other country in the world. According to data from the OECD, more than 16 percent of the gross domestic product (GDP) flowed into the healthcare system in 2021. The next highest-grossing country, Germany, spent 12.7 percent.

But the average American doesn't seem to get much for their money. Compared to other wealthy industrialized nations, the USA stands out with particularly poor results.

Americans have one of the lowest life expectancies among OECD countries, averaging only 76.4 years. The Swiss and Germans can expect to live to 83.9 and 80.8 years at birth, respectively. Americans live only about as long as Mexicans and Colombians – even though the latter spend less than half as much on healthcare as a percentage of their GDP and live in significantly poorer economies.

The cost-benefit ratio is also abysmal when considering preventable deaths. This is a commonly used measure of the quality of a country's health policy. It encompasses premature deaths of people under 75 who could have been avoided through appropriate preventive measures or medical treatment. The first group includes deaths from accidents, drug abuse, or inadequate preventive healthcare. The second group includes deaths resulting from inadequate medical treatment, for example, for cancer or cardiovascular diseases.

Despite high healthcare spending, many people in the US die prematurely. According to OECD data, there were 336 preventable deaths per 100,000 inhabitants in 2021. In Switzerland and Germany, the figures were 133 and 195, respectively.

## Problems outside of healthcare

The reasons for the poor performance lie not only in the healthcare system, but often in factors prior to it. There are four main reasons why young people in the US die far more often than in Europe: accidents, drugs, firearms, and lifestyle. These deaths of adolescents and young adults have a particularly significant impact on life expectancy calculations because the death of an 18-year-old lowers the average more than the death of a 75-year-old.

So why do so many young adults die in the USA? First, there are far more fatal accidents in the USA than in European countries, especially on the roads. The death rate is about five times higher than in Switzerland. The list of reasons for this is long: Americans drive more often and for longer distances, their cars are heavier, and the road infrastructure, especially in cities, is worse. Furthermore, traffic rules are poorly enforced and less frequently observed, as any Swiss visitor to New York, for example, quickly discovers.

## Many deaths caused by drugs and firearms

Secondly, the US is still suffering from a fentanyl epidemic: The opioid has spread rapidly and in waves across the US over the past ten years. Fentanyl is much stronger than heroin or other opioids, which is why fatal overdoses are more frequent. From 2021 to 2023, more than 100,000 Americans died each year as a result of drug use.

The number of deaths appears to have decreased by almost 30 percent to 80,000 in 2024, as reported by the CDC a week ago. One reason for this is likely the improved availability of the antidote naloxone, which can save lives in the event of an overdose .

Third, nearly 50,000 Americans die from firearm injuries each year. According to CDC figures, in 2023, 58 percent of these deaths were due to suicide and 38 percent to homicide ; the remainder were accidents and fatal police encounters. The widespread availability of firearms is an obvious reason for the high death toll— according to a 2017 Small Arms Survey, the US is the only country in the world where the number of firearms exceeds the population.

## Lifestyle poses a risk to health

Fourth: Perhaps the most significant difference is lifestyle. Approximately one-third of the additional preventable deaths can be attributed to chronic diseases such as diabetes and organ failure, as the non-governmental organizations Peterson Center and KFF have shown . Americans are significantly more likely to be severely overweight and, on average, exercise less than Europeans.

All these reasons overlap; in 2015, economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton coined the term «deaths of despair» to describe this phenomenon, attributing these deaths to the impoverishment of broad segments of the US population, particularly white men without college degrees. Since then, an intense debate has ensued about the precise relationship between these causes and effects.

However, one thing is clear: suicides, drug or alcohol addiction, and poor health are often linked to poverty, loneliness, and mental illness in the USA. In West Virginia, for example, a small state in the Appalachian Mountains, the highest number of people who die from accidents or drug overdoses has been recorded for years. These high death rates are a major reason why West Virginia has been slowly depopulating for years, almost uniquely among US states.

## The healthcare system is also to blame

These preliminary reasons already explain a large part of the difference between the US and other rich countries. However, a healthcare system that is very expensive, opaque, and complicated also contributes to the problem.

First, the American healthcare system is expensive. Both drug prices and treatment costs at doctors' offices and hospitals are significantly higher than in other OECD countries. It's no secret among specialists such as dermatologists or ophthalmologists that they can often earn many times more in the US than they would in Europe.

Secondly, the high costs stem from a lack of price transparency. There are significant differences in drug prices and hospital treatment costs depending on whether a person has comprehensive health insurance or pays for these goods and services privately. Providers have no interest in transparency. For example, hospitals have so far successfully resisted disclosing their rates.

Third, the system is very complex. While almost all Americans now have health insurance, for the majority this is through their employer. For the others, the US has built up a near-universal basic healthcare system over the past decades: 72 million mostly poorer Americans are covered by Medicaid, including about 40 percent of all children and adolescents, and 67 million retirees through Medicare.

But this system has major flaws, as health economists Amy Finkelstein and Liran Einav describe in their book «We've Got You Covered.» For example, a teenager with a rare disease might receive excellent care for years, only to die shortly after their 18th birthday because their treatment is suddenly no longer covered by Medicaid. It is often very difficult for patients to determine whether a particular treatment will be reimbursed by their insurer.

## Whom should the state help?

The shortcomings of the American healthcare system stem from the ambivalent relationship Americans have with their welfare state. On the one hand, a large majority believes that the state should help all those who become ill through no fault of their own and cannot pay their healthcare bills. In the past, state coverage was often expanded hastily when the media reported shocking cases, such as poor children being denied treatment.

On the other hand, there is widespread reluctance among the population to use public funds to help those who are themselves responsible for their poverty. This is also the basis for the criticism of European healthcare systems, which are often described as «socialist»: this free healthcare could allow able-bodied people to take it easy instead of looking for work.

One example of this is the latest cost-cutting measure that Republicans in Congress have just incorporated into their major budget bill. In the future, all Medicaid patients who are able to work will have to prove that they are employed for 80 hours per month in order to be covered by Medicaid. The necessary paperwork will certainly deter slackers – but probably also many patients who would otherwise meet the eligibility requirements for Medicaid.

## No chance for a system change

Consequently, the Americans have never been able to commit to introducing a universal basic health insurance system for all residents. This has resulted in a Byzantine-style system that drives up administrative costs.

The complexity and lack of transparency of the system are an open invitation to all those who want to line their pockets at the public's expense. The New York Times recently revealed that Medicare 's costs for specialized wound dressings have exploded in the past three years—to over $10 billion . Suppliers are exploiting, among other things, a bizarre rule whereby Medicare pays them virtually any price they charge for newly launched products for the first six months. Only after that does the reimbursement decrease. Some suppliers are now launching new products every six months.

Some health economists, such as Finkelstein or Katherine Baicker from the University of Chicago, advocate replacing the complicated patchwork system with a simple, streamlined, state-run basic healthcare system. However, such a system change is very difficult to implement politically.

Even minor reforms encounter considerable resistance. The established interest groups in the healthcare system—hospitals, health insurers, and pharmaceutical companies—navigate the regulatory thicket more easily than individual patients. They profit handsomely from it—and fight back. The poor cost-benefit ratio of the American healthcare system is unlikely to change anytime soon.
