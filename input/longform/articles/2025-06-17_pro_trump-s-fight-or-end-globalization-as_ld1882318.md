It is an irony of history that the USA, the «longtime champion of open markets» (as Foreign Affairs magazine called it), is the very entity that wants to dismantle the system it created – a system once hailed as the «most ambitious economic project of all time.» Another irony is that this frontal assault is coming from the right, after the WTO has been demonized primarily by the left since its founding thirty years ago. But one could have seen it coming.

## The rising tide

The liberalization of world trade increases welfare—this is one of the oldest insights in economics. It was already described by David Ricardo (1772–1823) in his theory of comparative advantage: if each country concentrates on the product it can produce more cheaply, relative to its size, prosperity grows in the trading countries. However, it was only after the upheavals of the protectionist global economy and the Second World War that there was a consensus that a liberal international trading system with fixed rules would be preferable.

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) came into force in 1947. While the first 23 signatory states could not agree on a firmly institutionalized organization like the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund – both founded in Bretton Woods in 1944 – the multilateral treaty nevertheless proved to be a highly efficient generator of prosperity. The USA, in particular, feared infringements on its economic sovereignty.

While average industrial tariffs initially exceeded 40 percent, they fell to less than 4 percent in the early 1990s thanks to progressive rounds of liberalization. However, the rapidly growing trade, driven by the capitalist superpower USA, now demands more regulation for the ever-increasing number of member states, including for dispute resolution.

After seven and a half years of negotiations (and initial skepticism from the US), the GATT was replaced by the World Trade Organization, which began its work in 1995. The WTO, which retained the Geneva headquarters of the GATT, was intended to finally liberalize the global economy. Following the end of the Cold War, optimism prevailed. Over one hundred countries, including Switzerland, signed the more than 10,000-page rulebook. It contained not only the most-favored-nation clause but also legally binding measures for resolving disputes. In the event of a dispute, the only international court accepted by the Americans would decide: the Conciliation Body in Geneva. The rule of law replaced the law of the jungle – at least in theory.

Renato Ruggiero, the first Director-General of the WTO, said: «The global trading system is more than just world trade. If no country is allowed to discriminate against another, then the future world will be more livable.» Politicians and the media celebrated the founding euphorically. There was talk of an explosion in global GDP, of the «rising tide that will lift all boats.» And of the hope that autocracies and dictatorships would open up and democratize: change through trade.

## Prelude of Trumpism

The WTO was demonized early on. Left-wing critics of globalization warned of a new «colonialism,» of a looming homogenization of culture: «As above, so below,» the German sociologist Wolfgang Sachs polemicized. He argued that «constant growth for all» would inevitably lead to environmental catastrophe on our finite planet. Furthermore, he claimed the WTO was an undemocratic deregulation machine, the command center of unbridled profit maximizers. The acronym, he asserted, stood for «World Takeover.»

The WTO is primarily a secretariat that moderates and coordinates global trade. While it can submit its own proposals and, through its dispute settlement body, monitors compliance with multilateral trade rules, decisions can only be made by member states, and only by unanimous vote.

At the WTO summit in Seattle in 1999, the protests of globalization critics escalated for the first time. Hundreds of NGOs called for a «Festival of Resistance,» 100,000 demonstrators blocked the convention center, and street battles ensued. Thanks to the internet, the movement gained enormous publicity. The WTO became the most hated organization in the world among leftists, the main culprit for global injustice. But not only that.

Especially in the US, there are also prominent conservatives who oppose the «globalists» in government. One of them is Pat Buchanan, former speechwriter for Richard Nixon, failed Republican presidential candidate, and present at the «Battle in Seattle.» He prophesies mass factory closures and a loss of sovereignty. The US would jeopardize its economic dominance and its own identity for a «garbage dump called multiculturalism.» He advocates for «America First,» and his 1998 bestseller says it all: «The Great Betrayal: How American Sovereignty and Social Justice Are Being Sacrificed to the Gods of the Global Economy.» It is the prelude to Trumpism.

The WTO, however, has more than just an image problem. While global prosperity continued to rise in the following years, including in developing countries, and more and more nations wanted to participate in this successful project, with global trade volume increasing by 125 percent by 2008, growth slowed. Most importantly, no major steps toward liberalization were achieved. The Seattle meeting, like all subsequent conferences, ended in failure. The problem: the unanimity principle was causing gridlock, even though the need for regulation was enormous: in services, intellectual property, climate change, and, most recently, artificial intelligence. Thus, globalization—and the WTO's inability to act—was increasingly subject to critical debate.

And while multilateral trade liberalization stalls, a competition for bilateral and regional free trade agreements breaks out. The US and the EU, in particular, are focusing on this. From 2013 onwards, China joins the fray with the launch of its Belt and Road Initiative.

## China's unfair game

China, in particular, has been a key player in the WTO since its founding in 1995, aiming to become a member in order to market its products globally. The fact that China was admitted in 2001 is thanks to the Americans, especially former President Bill Clinton. Opponents of integration into global trade warn that economic integration prior to political liberalization will only strengthen the authoritarian adversary. However, the optimists prevail: they believe the People's Republic can be forced into a framework of multilateral rules.

Yet China continues to practice state capitalism and has risen to become the world's second-largest economy, partly because it benefits massively from the WTO and its anti-discrimination rules. And because it still clings to its status as a developing country and derives protective rights from it. Americans have long been outraged that China is «the world's largest protectionist, mercantilist economy.» Trade between the two superpowers amounted to $8 billion in 1985. By 2004, it had already reached $231 billion, and by 2024, it was projected to reach $582 billion – each time with a massive trade deficit for the United States.

Criticism of China is not an invention of Donald Trump. He was simply the first to enact countermeasures, imposing new tariffs during his first term in the White House, tariffs which his successor, Joe Biden, also upheld. And now Trump's «reciprocal tariffs» threaten all of China's trading partners.

## The Law of the Jungle

However, at least as serious for the multilateral trading system is the fact that the US has crippled the independent dispute settlement system, which was once considered the «crown jewel» of the WTO. Since 2019, the Americans have refused to appoint replacement judges, claiming that the Appellate Body has unduly expanded its powers. Just a few weeks ago, the 86th application on this matter failed due to the US's refusal.

If the WTO is no longer seen as an institution that helps one assert one's rights in global trade, this sends a dangerous signal. Because of American obstructionist policies, more than fifty countries have now agreed to an alternative, voluntary dispute settlement mechanism to facilitate the resolution of trade conflicts, including the EU, China, Japan, Brazil, Mexico, and Switzerland. But will this help the WTO overcome its systemic crisis? Or will other powerful states follow the US example and shamelessly violate WTO rules? Will the law of the jungle, the survival of the fittest, soon prevail again on the world markets?

Meanwhile, at the Centre Rappard in Geneva, they are practicing a kind of pragmatic optimism. Director-General Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala says the WTO is far from finished, even after Trump's tariff offensive. The remaining 165 members are still interested in a rules-based system. And the WTO continues to regulate more than 75 percent of global trade: «Funnily enough, the members have just realized how valuable the WTO is.» The disruption in global trade is a good opportunity to address the system's weaknesses and position the WTO for the future.

«It sounds like whistling in the dark,» writes the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung. In fact, experts emphasize, the situation is much more serious. Roberto Azevêdo, Okonjo-Iweala’s predecessor from 2013 to 2020, recently answered a question from Der Spiegel about whether the system was dead: «As we knew it, yes.» Perhaps some parts of the rules-based global trade system could be salvaged in a different form. «But I can’t imagine us returning to the old order. No matter what happens in the next few weeks.»

And what do the former left-wing critics say? In their disgust with Trump, WTO free trade suddenly appears to them as a promise. It is the greatest irony of this story.
